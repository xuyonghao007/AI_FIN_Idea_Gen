Trade credit and the stability of supply chains 

Nuri Ersahin a,1, Mariassunta Giannetti b, 1, *, Ruidi Huang a,1 
a Cox School of Business at Southern Methodist University, United States 
b Stockholm School of Economics, the Swedish House of Finance, CEPR, and ECGI, Sweden   

A R T I C L E  I N F O    

A B S T R A C T    

We show that trade credit flows increase when a firm in a production network becomes a less reliable supplier 
due to an operating shock. Affected firms extend more trade credit when their customers have lower switching 
costs or expect more disruption. Suppliers that are more dependent on the affected firms facilitate the trade credit 
extension. However, when financial constraints at the affected firms and their suppliers prevent the increase in 
trade credit, customers sever their relationships with the affected firms, and the sales of the affected firms and 
their suppliers drop, suggesting that trade credit enhances production network stability.

1. Introduction 

Most studies of trade credit focus on bilateral supplier-customer re-
lationships,  thus  considering  firms  as  either  a  borrower  or  a  lender. 
However, in the real world, firms are part of complex production net-
works and simultaneously act as borrowers and lenders. Technological 
complementarities, incentives, and hold up problems along the supply 
chain  are  crucial  for  the  success  of  upstream  and  downstream  com-
panies.  Financial  claims  and  obligations  interlocking  firms  along  the 
supply chain could be used to improve incentives and enhance supply 
chain  stability,  as  suggested  by  theoretical  work  by  Kim  and  Shin 
(2012).  However,  we  still  have  scant  knowledge  of  the  mechanisms 
through which trade credit lubricates supply chain operations and on 
whether it indeed enhances their stability. 

This paper tests whether trade credit flows indeed serve as a “glue” to 
cement supply chains by exploring changes in trade credit usage around 

events that imperil the supply chain’s survival. We also investigate the 
extent  to  which  changes  in  trade  credit  provision  depend  on  the  in-
centives  and  constraints  faced  by  the  supply  chain  participants,  and 
crucially, the effects of trade credit on the stability of customer-supplier 
relationships. 

We  conjecture  that  operating  shocks  impair  firms’  reliability  as 
suppliers of goods and services and explore for the first time how in-
terdependencies  over  the  supply  chain  affect  the  provision  of  trade 
credit  in  their  aftermath.2  Operating  shocks  affect  a  firm’s  ability  to 
deliver timely and high-quality goods and services to customers and may 
arise from natural disasters, strikes, cyberattacks, geopolitical tensions, 
wars, or simply accidents. In most of our analysis, we consider tempo-
rary  disruption  to  operations  caused  by  natural  disasters.  Natural  di-
sasters  are  known  to  disrupt  firms’  operations  and  to  propagate 
upstream and downstream (see, e.g., Acemoglu et al., 2012; Barrot and 
Sauvagnat, 2016; Carvalho et al., 2021) and have the advantage of being

We build a simple framework in which operating shocks decreasing a 
firm’s reliability as a supplier in expectation have the largest negative 
effects on the production of downstream firms, which may incur losses if 
inputs  are  delivered  late  or  are  of  inferior  quality.  The  customers  of 
affected firms may thus have incentives to look for other suppliers to 
substitute  or  complement  those  experiencing  operational  difficulties. 
Since customers are difficult to replace, the interruption of existing re-
lationships would be costly for the affected firms. Thus, in an attempt to 
preserve  the  relationship  as  the  expected  quality  of  their  services 
weakens,  firms  experiencing  operational  difficulties  may  transfer  sur-
plus to their customers through trade credit. This would be consistent 
with  theories  and  empirical  evidence  that  firms  use  trade  credit  to 
transfer  surplus  to  their  customers  and  expand  their  customer  base 
(Klapper et al., 2012; Murfin and Njoroge, 2015; Barrot, 2016; Breza and 
Liberman, 2017; Giannetti et al., 2021; Grigoris et al., 2023). 

The effects however are unlikely to stop at the affected firm and its 
customers. As Glode and Opp (2023) note, if firms are interconnected 
through sequential trade credit claims, their ability to extend liquidity to 
their customers depends on their own liabilities and on whether they 
expect  those  to  be  renegotiated.  More  generally,  given  that  a  firm’s 
revenue depends on the revenues of its customers (Elliott et al., 2014), 
lower sales for an affected firm with shrinking customer base would also 
translate into smaller sales for their suppliers. Put differently, the spe-
cific  production  network  to  which  a  firm  belongs  is  important  for  its 
revenue. The suppliers of affected firms have a stake in the stability of 
the whole supply chain and want to avoid the breakup of the relation-
ships between the affected firms and their customers. Thus, the suppliers 
may extend more trade credit in order to facilitate the extension of the 
affected firms’ trade credit. 

Using  customer-supplier  linked  data,  we  show  that  firms  that  are 
affected  by  natural  disasters  provide  more  trade  credit  to  their  cus-
tomers and receive more trade credit from their suppliers. We are able to 
confirm this finding using hand-collected trade credit data that allow us 
to observe trade credit flows between customers and suppliers and to 
identify the supply and demand of trade credit. 

The finding that the accounts payable and the accounts receivable of 
firms facing operational difficulties simultaneously increase would be 
hard to reconcile without considering supply chain operations, as we do 
in our framework. If trade credit were to flow from firms with easy ac-
cess to finance to firms experiencing negative shocks, as theories based 
on  the  suppliers’  financial  advantage  would  suggest  (Petersen  and 
Rajan, 1997), we should observe that the accounts payable of affected 
firms  increase,  while  their  accounts  receivable  do  not  vary  or  even 
decrease. However, affected firms simultaneously provide and use more 
trade credit, indicating that firms obtain trade credit from their suppliers 
and  pass  it  on  to  their  customers,  even  if  this  behavior  drains  their 
liquidity and may have negative consequences on investment (Murfin 
and Njoroge, 2015). 

We  perform  a  number  of  cross-sectional  tests  to  shed  light  on  the 
mechanisms driving our findings. First, we analyze how switching costs 
affect the provision of trade credit. Customers purchasing products that 
are easy to substitute face lower switching costs (Cunat, 2007). Hence, 
firms  supplying  these  products  may  have  to  transfer  more  surplus  to 
maintain  their  customers  when  they  face  operational  difficulties.  We 
show that affected firms that produce easy to substitute products indeed 
extend  more  trade  credit  to  their  customers.  In  addition,  suppliers 
extend more trade credit to the affected firms if the customers of the 
latter can easily switch to alternative suppliers of the input. The fact that 
not only the affected firms, but also their suppliers provide more trade 
credit indicates that suppliers accommodate the provision of liquidity, 
which is essential for the survival of the supply chain. 

Second, we consider that the extent to which customers expect the 

operations of a firm to be disrupted by natural disasters should matter 
for trade credit flows. Accordingly, we observe that firms that can be 
expected to deliver high-quality and timely inputs and services after a 
natural disaster, because they have operations in many locations or have 
matured a strong reputation with the customers, extend and receive less 
trade credit. 

Third, we show that the propensity of firms affected by natural di-
sasters and their suppliers to extend trade credit depends on their stakes 
in the survival of the production network. Trade credit flows increase 
more  if  the  affected  firm  is  highly  dependent  on  a  major  customer 
because the severance of the relationship would imply a larger drop in 
sales  for  the  affected  firm  and  its  suppliers.  Furthermore,  suppliers 
provide more trade credit if they are highly dependent on the affected 
firm.  Overall,  these  findings  suggest  that  firms  use  trade  credit  to 
enhance supply chains’ survival. 

To shed additional light on the mechanisms driving our findings, we 
investigate  the  effects  of  financial  constraints  that  limit  trade  credit 
provision on the stability of supply chains. We find that following nat-
ural disasters, affected firms’ accounts payable do not increase if their 
suppliers are financially constrained. Affected firms do not extend the 
payment terms to their customers if both they and their suppliers are 
financially constrained. Customers that do not receive more trade credit 
in  turn  become  more  likely  to  terminate  their  relationships  with  the 
affected  firms and  start new  relationships.  Consequently, the  affected 
firms’  and  their  suppliers’  sales  decrease.  Thus,  pervasive  financial 
constraints over the supply chain limit trade credit usage and imperil the 
stability of supply chains. On the contrary, supply chains remain stable 
when the affected firms are financially constrained, but their suppliers 
are not. In this case, thanks to the liquidity provided by their suppliers, 
the affected firms are able to extend more trade credit, even if they are 
financially  constrained,  and  the  consequences  on  the  sales  of  the 
affected firm and its suppliers are limited. 

This paper contributes to several strands of the literature. First, by 
exploring trade credit usage in response to operating shocks, we are the 
first to show empirically that trade credit is used as a glue to enhance the 
stability  of  production  networks.  The literature  has  so  far  focused  on 
bilateral relationships between customers and suppliers and emphasized 
the role of financial constraints and customer bargaining power (see, e. 
g.,  Petersen  and  Rajan,  1997;  Cunat,  2007;  Giannetti  et  al.,  2011; 
Klapper et al., 2012). A notable exception is Gofman and Wu (2022), 
who document a number of stylized facts regarding a firm’s position in 
the production network and trade credit provision. Our finding can help 
explain  why  Gofman  and  Wu  (2022)  find  that  central  and  upstream 
firms provide more trade credit: These firms are indirectly affected by 
more  downstream  shocks  through  their  customers  and  extend  trade 
credit to enhance the stability of their supply chains. We show that trade 
credit  provision  depends  not  only  on  the  characteristics  of  a  firm’s 
customers, but also on the characteristics of the customers’ customers. In 
addition,  while  previous  literature  emphasizes  that  trade  credit  can 
emerge because a firm has strong relationships with its clients (Wilner, 
2000; McMillan and Woodruff, 1999), we show that it also enhances the 
stability of direct and indirect customer-supplier links. 

Second, a growing body of research documents how the transmission 
of  shocks  over  production  networks  affects  the  performance  of  cus-
tomers  and  suppliers  and  ultimately  leads  to  shock  propagation  and 
aggregate fluctuations (see, e.g., Hertzel et al., 2008; Barrot and Sau-
vagnat,  2016;  Giroud  and  Mueller,  2019;  Cen  et  al.,  2020;  Carvalho 
et al., 2021; Pankratz and Schiller, 2021). In this context, trade credit 
has been shown to help explain the propagation of negative shocks as 
firms default on their suppliers (Kyiotaki and Moore, 1997; Boissay and 
Gropp, 2013; Jacobson and van Schedvin, 2015). Several papers docu-
ment that firms that face liquidity shocks obtain more trade credit from 
their suppliers and extend less trade credit to their customers (see, e.g., 
Love  et  al.,  2007;  Garcia-Appendini  and  Montoriol-Garriga,  2013; 
Restrepo et al., 2019; Costello, 2020; Alfaro et al., 2021; Amberg et al., 
2021). Since firms affected by liquidity shocks provide less liquidity to

their customers, trade credit is considered to amplify the initial shock. 
The  literature  is  silent  on  the  effects  of  operating  shocks  on trade 
credit  provision.  We  introduce  to  the  literature  on  trade  credit  the 
distinction  between  liquidity  and  operating  shocks  and  argue  that 
operating shocks are more pernicious than liquidity shocks for supply 
chain stability because firms’ ability to produce is temporarily impaired 
and affected firms may cause losses for their customers (Acharya et al., 
2022). Liquidity shocks do not hamper a firm’s ability to produce and 
therefore  do  not  affect  as  much  as  operating  shocks  the  value  of  the 
relationships  for  the  affected  firms’  customers.  Following  a  liquidity 
shock,  customers  have  weak  incentives  to  search  for  more  reliable 
suppliers of the products and are willing to renounce some trade credit 
to save on switching costs and avoid a supplier’s default on its financiers. 
Accordingly, following liquidity shocks, we do not expect an increase in 
credit  flows  to  cement  supply  chain  relationships.  We  provide  a  con-
ceptual framework to explore how the financial structure of the supply 
chain  adapts  to  operating  shocks.  Our  results  imply  that  trade  credit 
enhances the stability of production networks after operating shocks. 

2. Conceptual framework and testable implications 

We consider a three-step production process, in which the most up-
stream firm, Firm 2, delivers an input to Firm 1, which transforms it and 
delivers it to Firm 0. The output at each of the three steps of production 
is I, aI, and a2I, where a>1. For simplicity, we assume that there is a 
homogeneous  good  or,  equivalently,  that  outputs  are  expressed  in  a 
numeraire. Therefore, net of the payment for any inputs, the profits of 
each stage of production are: I, I(a-1), and aI(a-1).3 

In the empirical analysis, we consider the case in which an operating 
shock affects Firm 1. Specifically, we consider an operating shock that 
occurs at t=0, before I is shipped by Firm 2 and paid. Following such an 
operating  shock,  the  expected  output  of  Firm  0  to  be  realized  at  t=1 
incorporates  the  probability  that  the  input  is  delivered  late  or  is  of 
inferior  quality, disrupting  Firm 0′s production  process. The expected 
output of Firm 0 after an operating shock affecting Firm 1 is: x a2I, where 
0<1-x<1 is the probability of disruption, arising from the fact that Firm 
1 may deliver the input late or the input may be of inferior quality. 

Following an operating shock to Firm 1, the expected profits of Firm 
0 are consequently aI(xa-1). Firm 0 can avoid disruption by switching to 
another supplier at t=0. Changing supplier implies switching costs K. 
Thus, Firm 0 switches when Firm 1 has experienced an operating shock 
if aI(xa-1)<aI(a-1)-K. That is, if K<(1-x) a2I. 

If Firm 0 switches, we assume that it is hard for Firm 2 and Firm 1 to 
find new customers to replace Firm 0. Consequently, Firm 1′s and Firm 
2′s profits are equal to zero if Firm 0 switches supplier and the supply 
chain collapses. If the operating shock occurs, but Firm 0 does not switch 
supplier, for simplicity, we assume that the sales of Firm 1 and Firm 2 are 
as large as without the operating shock. Thus, the total surplus that the 
supply chain loses if Firm 0 switches is: aI+K. 

Note that not only the affected firm would lose I(a-1), but also its 
supplier has a stake in the survival of the supply chain because it would 
lose, I. These assumptions capture that operating shocks have negligible 
negative effects if the supply chain remains stable. Yet, they may have 
considerable negative effects on the affected firm and its suppliers if the 
production network is disrupted. For these reasons, in the absence of 
financial constraints, both upstream firms have incentives to share part 
of their future profits with Firm 0 to avoid the breakage of the supply 
chain. In this respect, Firm 2 internalizes the need to preserve down-
stream  customer  relationships  for  the  survival  of  the  supply  chain. 
Specifically,  if  K<(1-x)a2I,  Firm  1  and  Firm  2  can  prevent  that  Firm 
0 switches to another supplier by transferring surplus. 

Following Giannetti et al. (2021), we assume that surplus is trans-
ferred through trade credit. This may be the case for the following two

reasons. First, in the presence of multiple customers, as in Giannetti et al. 
(2021), trade credit allows firms to transfer different amounts of surplus 
to customers with different switching costs, without charging different 
input prices, a conduct that could be sanctioned by antitrust authorities. 
Second,  trade  credit  can  provide  a  guarantee  for  the  punctuality  and 
quality of the input delivery (Lee and Stowe, 1993; Long et al., 1993; 
Klapper et al., 2012). In this context, trade credit can emerge without 
any need to renegotiate if customers delay payments when uncertainty 
on the quality and timely delivery of goods and services increases, while 
affected firms do not enforce the repayment and continue to deliver the 
inputs to maintain their customer relationships. 

The subsidy through trade credit that Firm 1 provides to Firm 0 can 
be written as ϕ0aI. That is, Firm 1 allows Firm 0 to delay the payment for 
part or all the input purchase aI, providing credit at a cost below Firm 0′s 
cost of capital. The parameter ϕ0 capture the size of the subsidy that may 
depend both on the amount of credit provided and the extent to which 
this credit is subsidized. If trade credit is provided at zero cost, as an 
increasing number of studies surveyed in Giannetti (2023) shows, ϕ0 can 
be interpreted as the proportion of the purchase that Firm 0 can pay late. 
Trade credit thus alters Firm 0′s condition for switching supplier. The 
supply chain remains stable if: aI(xa+ϕ0-1)≥aI(a-1)-K. 

This  yields  the  first  set  of  empirical  implications  of  our  simple 

framework. 

Implication  1. Firms  affected  by  operating  shocks  extend  more  trade 
credit  ϕ0aI  if  their  customers  have  low  switching  costs  K  or  the  expected 
disruption due to the operating shock (1-x) is particularly high. 

In the empirical analysis, not only do we test that on average firms 
affected by operating shocks should extend more trade credit to their 
customers, but we also explore the cross-sectional implications consid-
ering plausible proxies for K and x. For instance, the switching costs of 
the customer of Firm 1 may depend on the availability of products that 
are close substitutes provided by other suppliers, but also on the char-
acteristics  of  specific  customers.  Switching  costs  are  largely  fixed  in 
nature as in our simple model and are particularly low for large cus-
tomers  (Draganska  et  al.,  2010),  which  may  thus  require  a  larger 
transfer not to switch supplier. In this case, besides transferring surplus, 
as in Giannetti et al. (2021), trade credit may have the additional benefit 
of  not  affecting  the  cost  of  the  marginal  unit  purchased  by  Firm  0, 
mitigating any costs from the cannibalization of Firm 1′s sales to other 
smaller  customers  with  potentially  higher  switching  costs  (which  are 
outside of our model). In sum, we expect that large firms with suppliers 
affected by operating shocks should receive more trade credit. 

Following an operating shock to its supplier, a customer’s inclination 
to sever the relationship depends not only on its switching costs, but also 
on the expected disruption. Any characteristics of the affected firm that 
lower the expected disruption should be associated with less trade credit 
provision. Expected disruption should be lower for firms that operate in 
many locations, which can presumably continue to produce and ship the 
input from unaffected plants. Similarly, firms with more inventories may 
have finished products ready to ship to their customers, thus limiting 
backlogs and disruption for downstream firms. 

We also consider that the expected disruption may be lower for firms 
that have developed a  strong reputation with  their customers.  Strong 
reputation is likely to be associated with relationship length, which may 
also  reveal  high  switching  costs  for  the  customers  that  have  tailored 
their production processes to those suppliers for a long time. Both effects 
would go in the direction of decreasing the provision of trade credit by 
the company experiencing operating shocks. Another proxy for reputa-
tion is linked to the superstar status of a company. The largest firms in an 
industry are often considered superstars (Autor et al., 2020; Gutierrez 
and Philippon, 2019). These firms are also harder to substitute for more 
reliable suppliers. Thus, we expect that they provide less trade credit 
when they are hit by operating shocks.

The provision of liquidity by suppliers to firms affected by negative 
shocks mirrors the findings of papers exploring how supply chains adapt 
when  liquidity  shocks  occur  (see,  e.g.,  Costello,  2020;  Amberg  et  al., 
2021)  because  suppliers’  profits  are  jeopardized  by  the  breakage  of 
downstream customer-supplier relationships, regardless of the nature of 
the shock. Operating shocks differ from liquidity shocks for their effects 
on the customers, which are directly exposed to issues with the quality 
and timely delivery of the affected firm’s products only when operating 
shocks occur. In the case of a liquidity shock, x is equal to 1 if Firm 1 has 
enough liquidity not to be forced to default by external financiers. Firm 
0  is thus  willing to renounce some trade  credit to guarantee Firm  1′s 
survival and save the switching cost K. 

In what follows, we explore how trade credit flows vary following 

operating shocks and the effects of constraints to trade credit provision 
on the stability of supply chains. 

3. Data and summary statistics 

To  test  our  hypotheses,  we  need  information  on  supply  chains, 
operating  shocks,  and  financial  information  to  measure  trade  credit 
flows  and  evaluate  the  cross-sectional  implications  of  our  theoretical 
framework. Below, we introduce our data and most important proxies. 

3.1. Supply chains and trade credit 

We  obtain  firms’  financial  information  and  the  location  of  the 
headquarters from Compustat North America Fundamentals Quarterly 
Database. We exclude firms in the utility industries (SIC code 4900 – 
4999),  the  financial  services  industries  (SIC  code  6000  –  6999),  and 
government entities (SIC code 9000 – 9999) and construct two supply 
chain datasets, which complement each other. 

The first dataset merges Compustat with supply chain relationships 
from Factset Revere Supply Chain Relationship database. Factset Revere 
identifies  customer  and  supplier  relationships  from  SEC  10-K  annual 
filings,  investor  presentations  and  press  releases.  Following  Adelino 
et al. (2023), we make use of the reported information on customer and 
supplier  relationships  to  create  a  comprehensive  network  of  supply 
chain interconnections.4 

Factset Revere spans the period 2003 –  2019. Overall, we observe 
7806 customers and 8306 suppliers. For the average firm in the sample, 
we observe 21 customers and 20 suppliers. Since Factset does not pro-
vide information on how much trade credit is used in a relationship, we 
proxy for trade credit flows using information on accounts payable and 
accounts  receivable  from  Compustat.  As  is  common  in  the  literature 
(Petersen and Rajan, 1997), we scale accounts payable by the costs of 
goods sold and accounts receivable by sales to measure the amount of 
funding  a  firm  receives  from  its  suppliers  and  the  provision  of  trade 
credit to its customers.5 

The second dataset relies on hand-collection of information on the 
amount of trade credit extended by a firm to its important customers as 
recorded  in  the  10-K  disclosures  to  the  SEC.  Starting  from  1990,  the 
Financial Accounting Standard Board’s (FASB) regulation No. 105 re-
quires firms to disclose any concentration of credit risk.6 Typically, large 
amounts of accounts receivable to a major customer qualify as concen-

supplier  pair  in  the  Compustat  Segment  data  to  be  non-missing  to

We refer to this second dataset as the “SEC sample.” The final SEC 
sample has an annual frequency and includes 729 firms (both customers 
and suppliers) from 1991 to 2019. We observe a total of 317 customers 
and  430  suppliers.  On  average,  we  observe  1.59  customers  and  2.15 
suppliers per firm. While the SEC sample has limitations due to the small 
size and selection in firms’ reporting, its advantage is that we observe 
the amount of trade credit to a given customer. As we explain below, this 
will allow us to absorb non-parametrically customer (or supplier) un-
observed  heterogeneity  and  sharpen  the  interpretation  of  our  main 
findings. 

3.2. Natural disasters 

Our conceptual framework implies that any shocks leading to oper-
ating difficulties, such as strikes, labor or input shortages, and regula-
tions,  should  affect  trade  credit  flows  in  the  production  network.  We 
consider operating difficulties arising from natural disasters in a firm’s 
headquarters county, because natural disasters are exogenous and un-
likely  to  be  correlated  with  firm  shocks.  In  addition,  they  have  been 
documented to lead to operating difficulties for the affected firms and 
their customers (Barrot and Sauvagnat, 2016), as implied by our theo-
retical framework. 

We identify the date and estimated damages of natural disasters as 
well as the FIPS codes of the affected counties using SHELDUS (Spatial 
Hazard  and  Loss  Database  for  the  United  States),  a  database  by  the 
Center for Emergency Management and Homeland Security at Arizona 
State University.8 Following Barrot and Sauvagnat (2016), we consider 
natural  disasters  with  estimated  damages  larger  than  1  billion  2012 
dollars. We require the disaster to last less than 30 days. 

The  sample  covers  42  disasters,  including  blizzards,  earthquakes, 
floods, and hurricanes. These disasters affect a broad range of U.S. states 
and counties over the sample period. However, they are generally very 
localized and affect on average 47 counties, and at most 156 counties in 

a quarter, which is less than 5% of the total number of counties in the U. 
S. 

We introduce additional proxies in the empirical analysis and define 
all the variables in Appendix A. Table 1 presents the summary statistics 
of our samples.

Throughout the analysis, we control for firm size, leverage, age, and 
profits as well as the exposure of the firm’s customers and suppliers to 
the  natural  disaster.  We  also  absorb  unobserved  heterogeneity  by 
including firm (i) fixed effects, year-quarter (t) fixed effects, interactions 
of  firm  i’s  industry  (j(i))  and  year  (y(t))  fixed  effects  as  well  as  in-
teractions  of  the firm  i’s state (g(i))  and  year (y(t))  fixed  effects. The 
inclusion of these fixed effects allows us to explore whether the behavior 
of  firms  in  counties  affected  by  natural  disasters  changes  in  the  four 
quarters following the disaster in comparison to other firms in the same 
year, state, and industry. We cluster standard errors at the firm level, 
which  is  particularly  important  because  our  dataset  includes  over-
lapping quarters. 

Table 2 shows that in the year following a natural disaster, the ratio 
of  accounts  payable  relative  to  the  cost  of  goods  sold  increases  by  8 
percentage points (column 1). Contextually, affected firms’ receivables 
relative  to  sales  increase  by  2  percentage  points  indicating  that  they 
extend more trade credit to their customers (column 2).10 In fact, firms 
appear to entirely pass the extra liquidity received from their suppliers 
to their customers, as shown by the fact that in column 3 the net re-
ceivables, defined as accounts receivable minus accounts payable scaled 
by sales, are neither statistically nor economically different from zero. 
The  rest  of  the  table  considers  how  natural  disasters  affect  other 
corporate outcomes. Natural disasters appear to be associated with an 
increase in write-downs (column 4), but we do not detect a statistically 
significant effect on cash, assets, sales,11  cost of goods sold, or invest-
ment,  suggesting  that  the  overall  effects  of  natural  disasters  on  firm 
performance  may  indeed  be  negligible  if  the  supply  chain  survives. 
However,  Table  IA.4  shows  that  affected  firms’  sales  drop  in  less 
demanding specifications that do not include state × time and industry 
× time fixed effects, indicating that on average natural disasters lead to a 
drop in the scale of operations. This conclusion is invariant in Table IA.5 
where we consider the dynamics of affected firms’ sales.

Overall, these findings contrast with evidence that firms that expe-
rience negative liquidity shocks require faster payments from customers 
(Amberg  et  al.,  2021)  and  are  consistent  with  our  conjecture  that 
operating shocks are different because they decrease firms’ reliability in 
delivering the product. Table IA.6 and its dynamic version in Table IA.5 
show that customers of affected firms indeed experience a drop in sales, 
as  documented  by  Barrot  and  Sauvagnat  (2016),  and  may  thus  have 
incentives  to  look  for  more  reliable  suppliers,  as  suggested  by  our 
theoretical framework. Trade credit could, therefore, be used as a glue to 
enhance  the  stability  of  the  production  network  when  negative  oper-
ating shocks occur. 

Before evaluating whether cross-sectional differences in trade credit 
are consistent with our hypothesis, we carry out several additional tests 
to evaluate the robustness of our result that firms affected by natural 
disasters offer more trade credit. First, Table 4 tests for the existence of 
pre-existing trends that could invalidate our identification strategy. We 
estimate Equation (1) using indicator variables that equal one if the firm 
or one of its customers (suppliers) is impacted by a natural disaster in the 
quarters following the interval in which we consider the change in trade 
credit  usage  (after  t+4).  The  estimates  confirm  that  changes  in  trade 
credit usage do not emerge before the disaster.12 

We also conduct a placebo test, which helps to address the concern 
that a latent variable correlated with natural disasters, not captured by 
our control variables and fixed effects, may be driving our results. To 
implement the test, we assume that natural disasters happen in nearby 
counties  that  are  within  50  miles  of  the  actual  disaster  counties. 
Table IA.8 shows that the receivables and payables of firms in counties 
that are close but unaffected by natural disasters do not change, indi-
cating that unobserved shocks correlated with natural disasters are un-
likely to drive our findings.

5. Testing the mechanisms 

5.1. Customers’ switching costs 

We start by testing the first insight of Implication 1. The propensity 
of a firm to switch from a supplier that is likely to deliver inputs late or of 
inferior  quality  should  be  higher  if  switching  costs  are  low.  Affected 
firms’ customers are likely to face lower switching costs if the input is 
easy to substitute. In this case, affected firms should increase the supply 
of trade credit to a larger extent to maintain their customer relationships 
following natural disasters. 

In Table 6 Panel A, we start by measuring customers’ switching costs 
using a firm’s product market fluidity proxy developed by Hoberg et al. 
(2014). Product fluidity measures the availability of close substitutes for 
a  firm’s  product  using  the  product  descriptions  from  a  firm’s  and  its 
competitors’ 10-K filings. 

Column  1  shows  that  firms  that  are  affected  by  natural  disasters 
extend  more  trade  credit  if  their  product  has  close  substitutes.  The 
estimated  effect  is  economically  significant:  going  from  the  bottom 
decile to the top decile of the product fluidity proxy increases receiv-
ables by 9%. 

According to Implication 2, if affected firms lose market share, their 
suppliers’  sales  are  negatively  affected.  Therefore,  suppliers  should 
provide more trade credit to the affected firms when the latter have a 
high  risk  of  losing  their  customers  because  of  low  switching  costs.

Finally, we also consider that switching costs tend to be fixed and 
may therefore be less relevant for large customers. Alternative suppliers 
should  also  do  their  best  to  attract  large  customers.  According  to

should  receive  more  trade  credit  from  firms  affected  by  operating

the firm is in the 25th to 50th, 50th to 75th, or above the 75th percentile

is the omitted group. Consistent with our hypothesis, we find that firms 
with  total  assets  above  the  75th  percentile  experience  a  statistically 
significant  increase  in  trade  credit  they  receive  from  their  affected 
suppliers. This test also indicates why affected firms may use trade credit 
to increase their customers’ utility from maintaining the relationships: 
As Giannetti et al. (2021) show, trade credit can allow firms to transfer 
different  amounts  of  surplus  to  different  customers  without  being 
sanctioned  by  antitrust  authorities  and  distorting  competition  in 
downstream markets. 

In sum, the switching costs of affected firms’ customers appear to be 
an  important  driver  of  the  provision  of  trade  credit  for  both  affected 
firms and their suppliers, supporting the first two implications of our 
framework.

the insight of Implication 2 that trade credit provision by the affected 
firm and its suppliers will depend on how much they have to lose from 
the collapse of the supply chain.

6. Financial constraints and the instability of supply chains 

Implication 3 states that firms can extend more trade credit only if 
they or their suppliers are not financially constrained. For firms with 
high  financing  costs,  providing  cheap  financing  to  customers  is  too 
costly.  Consequently,  the  production  network  may  collapse  if  the 
affected firm’s suppliers are also financially constrained and unable to 
facilitate the liquidity provision by extending payment terms. 

To evaluate the effects of financial constraints on trade credit flows 
and supply chain survival, we start by testing to what extent the trade 
credit  provision  of  financially  constrained  firms  affected  by  a  natural 
disaster indeed depends on the financial conditions of their suppliers. In

affected  firms  following  natural  disasters,  when  financial  constraints 
prevail in the supply chain. 

In Table 12 Panel A, we show that firms affected by natural disasters 
appear to lose customers when both the affected firm and a larger pro-
portion  of  their  suppliers  are  financially  constrained.  We  know  from 
Table 11 that these firms are not able to provide more trade credit to 
their customers when they are hit by a natural disaster. In columns 1 and 
3, we consider the subsamples of financially constrained firms, defined 
again using either market capitalization below the median or the lack of 
a bond rating. The parameter estimates are not only statistically, but also 
economically significant. A standard deviation increase in the percent-
age of constrained suppliers leads the affected firms to lose 4.12% and 
1.58%  of  their  customers  using  the  market  capitalization  and  bond 
rating  measures  as  proxies  for  financial  constraints,  respectively;  a 
sizeable  number  considering  that  on  average,  in  our  sample,  firms 
experience a yearly increase in the number of customers of about 2%. 
In columns 2 and 4, we consider subsamples of financially uncon-
strained firms, defined as above. Being able to offer trade credit, these do 
not lose customers after experiencing natural disasters regardless of the 
financial conditions of their suppliers, as indicated by the statistically 
insignificant  coefficient  on  the  interaction  Affected*Percentage  of  con-
strained  suppliers.  Also  in  this  case,  estimates  are  qualitatively  and 
quantitatively  invariant  when  we  use  different  proxies  for  financial 
constraints. 

In Panel B, we take the perspective of the customers of affected firms. 
We ask whether the customers add new suppliers in the industry of the 
affected firms to avoid bottlenecks. Firms could also add suppliers if they 
want to sever the relationship with the affected firms. We observe that 
the customers of affected firms start new relationships with the affected 
firms’ competitors only if the affected firms are financially constrained 
(columns 1 and 3) and have a large proportion of financially constrained 
suppliers.  In  columns  2  and  4,  we  consider  the  subsamples  of 

unconstrained firms and find no evidence that the customers of affected 
firms  seek  new  suppliers,  regardless  of  the  financial  conditions  of 
affected  firms’  suppliers.  These  findings  indicate  that  the  financial 
conditions of suppliers matter only for affected firms with high funding 
costs, as is consistent with our conceptual framework. 

Overall, the evidence in this section supports the mechanism behind 
our conceptual framework that trade credit helps preserve supply chain 
stability when negative operating shocks occur. The financial conditions 
of suppliers play a crucial role in the provision of trade credit and the 
resulting stability of the supply chain. 

Importantly,  the  provision  of  trade  credit  affects  not  only  the  sta-
bility of the affected firms’ customer relationships, but also their prof-
itability  and  sales.  Table  13  distinguishes  between  subsamples  of 
constrained  and  unconstrained  firms,  following  the  same  structure  as 
Tables 11 and 12, and provides evidence that the increase in trade credit 
usage following natural disasters helps to maintain the sales and prof-
itability of affected firms. In particular, we observe a negative and sig-
nificant coefficient on the interaction Affected*Percentage of constrained 
suppliers  only  in  the  subsamples  of  financially  constrained  firms  (col-
umns  1  and  3),  indicating  that  the  sales  and  profitability  of  affected 
firms  drop  only  when  both  the  affected  firms  and  their  suppliers  are 
financially  constrained,  that  is,  when  firms  are  unable  to  obtain  and 
offer more trade credit and thus lose customers. We do not detect any 
negative  effects  of  natural  disasters  on  the  sales  and  profitability  of 
affected firms if they are financially unconstrained (columns 2 and 4), 
regardless of their suppliers’ ability to provide liquidity. 

In Table IA.12, we also consider the effects on the suppliers of the 
affected firm. Our framework implies that unconstrained suppliers are 
willing to extend trade credit to the affected firms because their sales 
and  profits  would  also  drop  if  their  direct  customers  lost  their  own 
customers. In columns 1 and 3 of Panels A and B, we consider the sub-
sample of constrained firms. The estimates show that regardless of the 
definition  of  financial constraints  we  use,  the  sales  and  profits  of  the 
affected  firms’  suppliers  drop  only  when  their  affected  customers  are 
financially  constrained  and  are  likely  to  have  been  unable  to  extend 
sufficient trade  credit to preserve their customer relationships. As  we 
would expect, financially unconstrained firms, being able to facilitate 
trade  credit  flows,  do  not  experience  a  drop  in  sales  when  their  cus-
tomers  experience  natural disasters even if  their customers are finan-
cially constrained. 

Taken together, not only are these results consistent with the idea 
that  trade  credit  enhances  the  stability  of  production  networks  when 
operating shocks affect a firm in the network but also suggest that firms 
affected  by  negative  shocks  can  preserve  their  profitability  thanks  to 
trade credit flows.

7.1. Natural disasters and supplier choice 

The analysis so far has shown that suppliers with deep pockets can 
provide insurance. Since suppliers’ financial health can benefit a firm’s 
performance, firms should pay close attention to the financial conditions 
of the suppliers (Titman, 1984; Banerjee et al., 2008). Specifically, we 
expect that firms that have the most to lose from negative realizations of 
operating risk seek the reliability provided by suppliers with financial 
flexibility.  Table  14 provides  some evidence that  this is  the  case. For 
each firm, we consider the average size of the suppliers in a given year 
and industry. Given that larger suppliers are more likely to be financially 
unconstrained, we expect that firms that are more concerned about the 
negative consequences of operating shocks would be more likely to work 
with those suppliers.

7.2. Other operating shocks and comparison with liquidity shocks 

8. Conclusions 

We expect trade credit flows to increase when operating difficulties 
for a firm in the network imperil the survival of the supply chain. Nat-
ural disasters allow us to capture operating difficulties using an exoge-
nous shock that is likely to be orthogonal to other firm shocks. But we 
expect the same effects to arise all the times in which disruptions due to 
strikes,  labor  shortages,  cyberattacks,  regulations,  or  difficulties  in  a 
firm’s ability to obtain inputs impair the firm’s reliability as a supplier of 
high quality and timely products and services. 

While it is hard to capture all the above events in a systematic way, 
we rely on an insight from Acharya et al. (2022), who argue that oper-
ating shocks arise from exposure to supply chain risk. We use the proxy 
for supply chain risk based on textual analysis of earnings conference 
calls  of  Ersahin  et  al.  (2024).  In  Table  15,  we  show  that  firms  that 
experience  heightened  supply  chain  risk  extend  more  trade  credit  to 
their customers. These firms also receive more trade credit. While the 
accounts receivables of firms experiencing heightened supply chain risk 
may increase not only because their suppliers try to accommodate their 
liquidity needs but also because at least some of their suppliers have to 
compensate them for the expected disruption costs, these results support 
the external validity of our earlier findings. 

By converse, we do not expect a firm to provide more trade credit 

Supply chains are important for firms’  performance. But operating 
shocks  due  to  natural  disasters,  cyberattacks,  trade  wars,  and  other 
sources  threaten  supply  chain  stability  and  survival.  We  show  that 
supply chains remain stable when firms in the network are able to in-
crease  trade  credit  flows  to  increase  the  value  of  the  relationship  for 
their  customers.  The  trade  credit  flows  within  the  supply  network 
following operating shocks suggest that not only affected firms but also 
their suppliers try to preserve the stability  of the supply chain. Thus, 
suppliers extend trade credit to the affected firms, especially if the latter 
are financially constrained and would otherwise be unable to provide 
short-term funding to their customers. 

Production networks become unstable and the affected firms’ prof-
itability decreases when financial constraints are pervasive and affect 
not only the affected firms, but also their suppliers. These problems may 
be far stronger for production networks that include small businesses. 
Our  empirical  analysis  thus  provides  a  lower  bound  for  the  negative 
effects of operating shocks on the instability of supply chains and pro-
vides support for policies, such as the American Rescue Plan, enacted at 
the start of the COVID-19 pandemic: Besides relieving a firm’s financial 
constraints and supporting employment, these policies are likely to have 
sustained trade credit flows and the stability of the supply chains.
