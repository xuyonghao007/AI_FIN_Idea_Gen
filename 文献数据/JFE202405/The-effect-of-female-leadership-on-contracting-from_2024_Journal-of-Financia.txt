The eﬀect of female leadership on contracting from Capitol Hill to Main 
Street

This  paper  provides  novel  evidence  that  female  politicians  increase  the  proportion  of  US  government 
procurement contracts allocated to women-owned ﬁrms. For identiﬁcation, we use a regression discontinuity 
design on a sample of mixed-gender elections in the US House of Representatives. The eﬀect grows over a 
female representative’s tenure and concentrates in female representatives who are on powerful congressional 
committees. Changes in the pool of and behavior by government contractors cannot explain the result. The more 
gender-balanced representation in government contracting is not associated with economic costs.

The single biggest customer in the world is the US federal govern-
ment,  which  procured  more  than  $665  billion  in  ﬁscal  year  2020.1
Women-owned businesses (WOBs) are underrepresented as government 
contractors relative to their share in the economy: between 2008 and 
2020, only 9%2 of government contracts were allocated to WOBs while 
36%3 of small ﬁrms are owned by women. Receiving government con-
tracts  has  been  linked  to  long-run  success,  employment  growth,  and 
reducing ﬁnancial frictions for small ﬁrms.4 Hence, alleviating barri-
ers faced by WOBs in government contracting will increase their eco-
nomic potential. To quote Senator Jeanne Shaheen, Democrat from New 
Hampshire, “Women entrepreneurs may be the country’s greatest un-
tapped economic resource.”5 This paper studies the impact of female 

political representation on government spending being distributed to 
WOBs.

We ﬁnd that female members of the US House of Representatives 
cause an increase in government contracts allocated to WOBs. We es-
tablish causality by exploiting a regression discontinuity design (RDD) 
around mixed-gender elections. The eﬀect increases over a female rep-
resentative’s tenure and concentrates in female representatives who are 
on congressional committees with budgetary and oversight powers. We 
ﬁnd no evidence that changes in the pool of government contractors 
(i.e., more female contractors) or changes in their behavior can explain 
our results. In both ex-ante contract terms (i.e., amount and duration) 
and ex-post performance measures (i.e., contract modiﬁcations), we fail

Our main contribution is to examine how female customers allevi-
ate barriers faced by WOBs. We focus on one customer in particular, the 
US federal government, and use the genders of members of Congress 
as  shocks  to  the  customer’s  gender.  While  most  economics  research 
that  examines  gender  focuses  on  private  ﬁrms,  we  focus  on  govern-
ment because it provides three unique characteristics. First, the federal 
government is the largest customer in the US, which provides millions 
of contracts to analyze. Second, government transparency rules allow 
us to access high-quality data on individual contracts, including terms, 
product descriptions, and the gender of the ﬁrms’ owners. Third, the 
government setting is one where gender bias should be hardest to ob-
serve, given the numerous programs supporting minorities.6

The election of a female politician is not random and leads to pos-
sible endogeneity concerns. To mitigate these problems, we exploit an 
RDD and mixed-gender elections. At the threshold, outcomes are uncer-
tain and provide quasi-random variation in election winners. As such, 
it is as if the gender of the winning candidate is exogenously assigned.7
The full sample spans from the 109th to the 116th Congress, a total 
of 3,473 general and 9 special elections, 1,055 of which were between 
candidates of diﬀerent genders.8 We merge this data with detailed infor-
mation on individual government procurement contracts obtained from 
the Federal Procurement Data System-Next Generation (FPDS-NG). We 
consider a total of 970,968 contracts under the small business set-aside 
program.

The focus on small business contracting provides a number of advan-
tages. First, 94% of women-owned ﬁrms are small businesses and small 
businesses account for a large portion of the US economy. To be speciﬁc, 
in 2018, there were more than 30 million small businesses in the US 
that employed 47% of the private workforce and added 1.8 million net 
jobs.9 Second, 23% of federal procurement spending each year must go 
to small businesses by law. In ﬁscal year 2022, the federal government 
exceeded this goal by awarding 27.2% of contracts to small businesses, 
which translates into over $150 billion of contracts.10 Third, in con-
trast to collective political eﬀorts such as legislative changes, federal 
procurement contracting can be linked to an individual district repre-
sentative and an individual recipient.

We show that the election of a female representative increases the 
probability that a government contract is awarded to a women-owned 
ﬁrm by 4.5 percentage points. In comparison, the share of Small Busi-
ness Association (SBA) contracts allocated to WOBs in the US overall 
increased only by 11 percentage points from 2005 to 2020. A back-of-
the envelope calculation for the ﬁscal year 2020 suggests female repre-
sentation led to a transfer of $6.9 billion from non-WOBs to WOBs in 
federal government procurement contracts.11 Lastly, government con-
tracts are economically important to WOBs in our sample. The median 
WOB has $1.1 million in annual revenue, of which 9% are due to gov-
ernment purchases. For 26% of the WOBs in our sample, government 
contracts under the SBA program amount to even more than 50% of 
their revenue.

of the research design hinges on the assumption that the gender of the 
elected representative is as if randomly assigned close to the threshold 
(Imbens and Lemieux, 2008). A McCrary (2008) test conﬁrms that the 
distribution of the vote margin is continuous around the threshold. The 
congressional districts won by women are similar to the districts won 
by men for numerous preelection district dimensions, such as district 
output, bias against women, and female government contractors. The 
causal ﬁndings are robust to varying the bandwidth as well as to remov-
ing individual states from the model. Consistent with our interpretation, 
the results disappear in placebo regressions with the randomized gender 
of the winning legislator.

We next test the underlying mechanism. We diﬀerentiate between 
two possibilities: (1) a supply-side mechanism whereby the presence of 
female politicians changes the pool of WOBs or their behavior, lead-
ing to more contracts being allocated to them; or (2) a demand-side 
mechanism whereby the government actively allocates more contracts 
to WOBs because of the female politician.

We start by providing evidence that the supply-side mechanism is 
an unlikely driver of our results. One straightforward test to analyze 
the supply-side mechanism would be to examine how contract bidding 
changed after the election of female representatives. Unfortunately, the 
data does not allow us to observe the identity of all bidders for a given 
contract.12

The ﬁrst test uses historical snapshots of government contractors. 
This data is free of survivorship bias and captures all potential contrac-
tors, not only the ﬁrms that got at least one contract. We show that 
the election of female legislators does not cause changes in the pool of 
contractors in general, contractors’ reallocation to female-led districts, 
or changes in their status as WOBs. Assuming the pool of contractors 
represents the pool of bidding ﬁrms, this is evidence against a supply-
side  eﬀect.  Second,  we  show  that  the  election  of  a  female  legislator 
does not increase the number of bidders for government contracts. This 
is not a direct test of whether more WOBs apply for government con-
tracts. However, the reported evidence is consistent with a supply-side 
mechanism only if a potential increase in WOBs bidding for contracts is 
compensated by an exact one-to-one decrease in non-WOBs in the pool 
of applications at the same time, which seems unlikely. Third, our main 
eﬀect already occurs within 180 days after an election. Under the as-
sumption that it takes time to become a government contractor, this 
evidence suggests that supply does not explain the main eﬀect. As a ref-
erence, it takes 18 months after registration for the average company 
to sign its ﬁrst contract with the Department of Defense. These three 
pieces of evidence are inconsistent with the supply-based mechanism, 
suggesting that our main eﬀect is driven by the demand-side mecha-
nism.

We further explore the dynamics of the demand-side mechanism. 
First, we study the persistence of the female legislator eﬀect. We show 
that a transition from a male to a female legislator increases the alloca-
tion of contracts to WOBs, while a transition from a female to a male 
legislator diminishes it. If a female succeeds another female, the WOB 
contract allocation only increases if the winning candidate is the in-
cumbent, consistent with legislators having a more signiﬁcant impact 
as their tenure increases. Literature focusing on female political rep-
resentation ﬁnds similar patterns. Hasan et al. (2020) show that the 
positive eﬀect of a female mayor on government ﬁnancial conditions in 
her second term is stronger than the initial eﬀect. Gerrity et al. (2007)
document that male legislators replacing female legislators leads to a

Next, we explore how representatives aﬀect contract allocation. We 
document  that  female  legislators  who  sit  on  any  congressional  com-
mittee are particularly inﬂuential. The eﬀect is even more pronounced 
when zooming in on Congress members on powerful committees, as de-
ﬁned in Cohen et al. (2011), and key budgetary and oversight commit-
tees as in Brogaard et al. (2021). We interpret this evidence as female 
legislators aﬀecting contract allocation via the power of the purse (bud-
getary committees) and the power of oversight (oversight committees). 
This mechanism rationalizes our previous ﬁnding that the eﬀect of a fe-
male representative on contract allocation increases over her tenure, as 
participation in powerful committees also increases with tenure.

We conclude the analysis of the demand channel with two pieces 
of evidence. First, we provide evidence that rules out local networks as 
an explanation. Second, focusing on political aﬃliation, we ﬁnd that 
Republican female representatives have a signiﬁcantly larger eﬀect on 
contract allocation to WOBs than Democratic female representatives.

One potential criticism of increased WOB contract allocation is that 
it may negatively aﬀect the costs and quality of government procure-
ment. Our data allows us to directly examine this concern. First, we 
investigate contract terms. We do not ﬁnd evidence that districts rep-
resented by female politicians are more lenient toward WOBs. Second, 
we evaluate contract performance using measures of cost and time over-
runs, number of modiﬁcations, and probability of contract cancellation. 
While female representatives increase contract allocation to WOBs, we 
do not ﬁnd evidence of the shift resulting in higher economic costs. 
Shifting the focus to ﬁrm outcomes, we ﬁnd that receiving government 
contracts predicts business opportunities, measured by two-year growth 
in revenue, number of employees, and credit score.

This paper adds to three literature strands. The ﬁrst is the litera-
ture  on  whether  the  presence  of  women  alleviates  barriers  faced  by 
female entrepreneurs. Much of this literature focuses on diﬀerential ac-
cess to capital studying crowdfunding (Vismara et al., 2017; Bapna and 
Ganco, 2021; Gafni et al., 2021), angel investors (Becker-Blease and 
Sohl, 2007; Ewens and Townsend, 2020), venture capital (Gornall and 
Strebulaev, 2022; Hebert, 2023), and personal debt ﬁnancing (Bellucci 
et al., 2010).13 In contrast, we focus on a customer channel, the gov-
ernment’s demand for products and services provided by WOBs. From 
an entrepreneurial perspective, this channel is important because client 
acquisition is essential for the survival and growth of small businesses 
(Shelton and Minniti, 2018). Receiving government contracts has been 
linked to long-run success and employment growth (Hvide and Meling, 
2023),  and  to  alleviating  ﬁnancial  constraints  (Hebous  and  Zimmer-
mann, 2021).14

The second is the literature on more women in politics. Previous 
literature  suggests  that  female  politicians  support  legislative  policies 
that are targeted at and beneﬁcial for female constituents (Gerrity et 
al., 2007; Volden et al., 2018).15 We show that this support extends to 
non-legislative actions, such as reallocation of government spending.16
A related paper is Ghani et al. (2014), who link political quotas for 
women in India and the role of women in the manufacturing sector. In 

contrast to our paper, the authors do not ﬁnd evidence of an increase 
in government-sponsored contracts to women-owned ﬁrms.17

Finally, this paper contributes to the literature on the role politicians 
have in contract allocation. Firms’ political connections to politicians, 
often  proxied  by  campaign  contributions,  increase  the  probability  of 
receiving contracts (Goldman et al., 2013; Tahoun, 2014; Schoenherr, 
2019)  and  aﬀect  the  associated  contract  terms  (Ferris  et  al.,  2019; 
Brogaard et al., 2021). Our paper diﬀers on two dimensions: the link 
between politicians and ﬁrms is shared gender, and this link does not 
lead to preferential treatment for connected contractors.

This paper’s ﬁndings have important policy implications because the 
gender gap in government contracts has been prominent in public de-
bates.  Bates  (2002) points  out  that  WOBs  receive  fewer  government 
contracts than their male-owned counterparts.18 According to a report 
published  by  the  Commerce  Department,  the  odds  of  women-owned 
ﬁrms winning a federal contract are about 21% lower than for otherwise 
similar companies (Beede and Rubinovitz, 2015). In 1994, Congress en-
acted the Federal Acquisition Streamlining Act (FASA), signed into law 
by President Clinton, which set a goal of awarding 5% of federal govern-
ment contract dollars annually to women-owned small businesses. Since 
then, Congress has initiated several explicit programs aiming to increase 
female participation in government contracting.19 Nevertheless, it took 
21 years – until 2015 – for the goal to be reached. Our ﬁndings high-
light an alternative pathway through which female politicians increase 
gender diversity in government contracting. This pathway is particu-
larly timely given that, at the time of writing this paper, the number 
of female representatives in the House of Representatives is at an all-
time high of 120 for the 117th Congress, and that the ﬁrst female vice 
president was inaugurated in 2021.

1.  Gender contracting gap, institutional details, and data

1.1.  Gender contracting gap

Since the ﬁrst federally sponsored National Women’s Conference in 
November 1977, the federal government has consistently acknowledged 
that WOBs confront signiﬁcant institutional barriers and discrimination 
in the United States (see Sirmons (2004)). As Brush (1998) pointed out, 
female business owners had to work harder than men to convince “gov-
ernment procurement oﬃcials and corporate customers that they are 
as competent as men.” In focus groups investigating the source of the 
gap, female business owners articulated concerns of not being taken se-
riously as business owners (see Brush (1998)).

One response to the shortfall of WOBs in procurement has been the 
development of preferential procurement programs, whereby govern-
ment agencies explicitly seek to increase their purchases of goods and 
services from WOBs. In 2000, Congress enacted a set-aside program for 
Women-Owned Small Businesses in the Equity in Contracting for Women 
Act.20 Regardless, it took ten years for the SBA to issue its ﬁnal rule for 
the program, and the program only took eﬀect in 2011.

Another push for equality is the 2014 law that allowed WOBs to 
qualify for no-bid “sole source” contracts. Federal oﬃcials have discre-

1.2.  Institutional details

Federal procurement contracts are agreements between government 
agencies and businesses to obtain goods and services for a negotiated 
price and length.

The  process  of  awarding  government  contracts  starts  with  a  fed-
eral agency identifying a need for a purchase of a good or service. The 
agency decides the degree and method by which the contract is com-
peted, the form of pricing appropriate to the contract, and whether the 
contract is for a deﬁnitive quantity or some indeﬁnite delivery vehi-
cle (IDV). Then, a contracting oﬃcer of this agency posts a solicitation 
on the SAM.gov website. A solicitation announcement identiﬁes what 
the agency wants to buy, provides instructions to potential contractors, 
identiﬁes the source-selection method to evaluate oﬀers, and includes 
a deadline for the submission of bids or proposals. Firms then submit 
their oﬀers for review by agency oﬃcers who evaluate them and make 
the ﬁnal decision.24

Firms interested in getting a federal contract must obtain a Dun & 
Bradstreet Data Universal Numbering System (DUNS) unique number 
for each of the business’s physical locations and register with the fed-
eral government’s System for Award Management (SAM). Firms that 
identify themselves as a small business in the federal government’s Sys-
tem for Award Management (SAM) must meet the Small Business Act’s 
deﬁnition of a small business and must not exceed the size standards es-
tablished and periodically updated by the SBA. The Small Business Act 
deﬁnes a small business as one that is organized for proﬁt; has a place 
of business in the United States; operates primarily within the United 
States or makes a signiﬁcant contribution to the US economy through 
payment of taxes or use of American products, materials, or labor; is in-
dependently owned and operated; and is not dominant in its ﬁeld on a 
national basis. The business may be a sole proprietorship, partnership, 
corporation, or any other legal form.

Until the summer of 2020, small businesses could self-certify as a 
WOB  when  they  registered  at  SAM.  For  the  identiﬁcation  of  WOBs 
among contractors, we utilize a data item named “Women-Owned Busi-
ness.”  As  deﬁned  by  the  Small  Business  Act,  a  women-owned  small 
business is one that is a small business, with at least 51% ownership

We focus on SBA set-aside contracts with speciﬁed terms and condi-
tions, so-called deﬁnitive contracts (DCs), for two main reasons. First, 
94% of women-owned ﬁrms are small businesses according to the Com-
merce Department (Beede and Rubinovitz, 2015). Second, DCs allow us 
to assess how female representatives impact government allocation in 
stand-alone, one-time agreements with a single ﬁrm for the purchase 
of goods or services under speciﬁed terms and conditions. In contrast, 
IDVs are contracts with one or more ﬁrms, and at the time of the con-
tract award there is uncertainty about the quantity of goods or services 
to be provided, the timing of delivery, or the scope of the agreement. 
This initial uncertainty might aﬀect the tractability of the contract per-
formance measures.25

The SBA set-aside is the most prevalent set-aside program. Around 
20% of all government contracts and 27% of contracting dollars are 
awarded through the SBA set-aside program.26 It sets aside federal pro-
curement opportunities for small business concerns, regardless of the 
owner’s gender.27 The main requirement is that federal agencies gen-
erally  reserve  contracts  that  have  an  anticipated  value  greater  than 
the micro-purchase threshold ($10,000 in 2020), but not greater than 
the simpliﬁed acquisition threshold ($250,000 in 2020), exclusively for 
small businesses unless the contracting oﬃcer is unable to obtain oﬀers 
from two or more small businesses that are competitive with market 
prices and the quality and delivery of the purchased goods or services. 
In addition, federal agencies generally set aside contracts that have an 
anticipated value exceeding the simpliﬁed acquisition threshold exclu-
sively for small businesses when there is a reasonable expectation that 
oﬀers will be obtained from at least two responsible small businesses of-
fering the products of diﬀerent small businesses (Rule of Two) and the 
award will be made at a fair market price.

We obtain the full sample of procurement contracts between the US 
government and individual ﬁrms from the FPDS-NG. This dataset is at 
the contract level and oﬀers detailed information on the contract it-
self, the contractor, and the speciﬁc government entities participating 
in the procurement. In total, there are over 300 characteristics such as 
small business or WOB statuses.28 We limit the sample to SBA set-aside 
contracts that are awarded competitively and for which only small busi-
nesses can compete. We also exclude contracts that do not contain a 
well-deﬁned completion date and budget. Additionally, we require the 
contract to be awarded directly to a ﬁrm, meaning that the contract is 
not awarded to the parent company and is not part of a bundled project, 
that the contract is awarded and performed inside the US without any 
foreign funding, that the contractor is neither a government organiza-
tion nor a nonproﬁt organization, and that the contractor is registered 
in the US and is not foreign owned.

The main dependent variable in this paper is whether a contract is 
awarded to a WOB. Fig. 1 presents a time series plot of the share of 
government contracts awarded to WOBs from 2005 to 2020. After an 
initial increase before 2010, the share of contracts awarded to female 
representatives oscillates around 20%, with a recent uptick in 2020.29
Table 1 provides summary statistics of procurement contracts be-
tween January 3, 2005, and June 18, 2020. Detailed variable deﬁnitions 
and sources are given in Table A1 in the Appendix. In addition to the 
limits based on contracts’ and contractors’ characteristics, we consider 
all contracts that can be linked to a congressional election. Contracts 
are  assigned  to  a  given  congressional  representative  if  the  contract 
was awarded between the term start and subsequent election date, and 
executed in the respective district.30 The sample consists of 970,968 
unique contracts initially worth on average $41,000, amounting to $39 
billion. Accounting for renegotiation (see Brogaard et al. (2021)), the 
contracts in the sample amount to $51 billion. 20% of those contracts 
are awarded to WOBs.

We report several contract statistics: contract amount, contract ma-
turity, number of bids, contract without pricing/cost terms, and mul-
tiyear contracts. Finally, we consider six measures of contract perfor-
mance: cost or time overruns (both as an indicator variable and contin-
uous), whether there were any modiﬁcations, and whether the contract 
was canceled. These measures are inspired by Brogaard et al. (2021), 
who show that renegotiations and changes in contract terms are fairly 
common.

2.  Congresswomen and women-owned businesses receiving 
government contracts

This section tests the eﬀect of electing a female representative on 
government contract allocation to WOBs. We start with a naïve regres-
sion before moving to an RDD for causal inference. The results show 
causal evidence that female representatives lead to more WOBs being 
allocated government contracts. The results withstand a variety of va-
lidity and robustness checks.

2.2.  Regression discontinuity design

The main challenge of identifying the causal eﬀect of legislator gen-
der on WOB contract allocation is that US House representatives are not 
randomly assigned to diﬀerent congressional districts. A naïve regres-
sion analysis of the government contractor’s gender on the gender of the 
district representative might be biased because of unobserved charac-
teristics correlating with both the dependent and independent variables. 
For example, female empowerment or the Me Too movement might si-
multaneously drive female involvement in the political arena as well as 
in business.

One solution to addressing endogeneity concerns in this setting is to 
use the RDD on a sample of mixed-gender elections.36 We follow the 
design by Ferreira and Gyourko (2014), Brollo and Troiano (2016), and 
Hasan et al. (2020) closely. Similar to us, those studies identify the ef-
fect of female US politicians (mayors) on political and policy outcomes, 
and on municipal bond pricing.37 Because of the ex-ante uncertainty 
regarding the outcomes at the threshold, this approach provides quasi-
random variation in election winners, implying that the gender of the 
winning candidate is arguably determined by pure chance.

shifts in procurement on agency, product, and state level over time, 
such as WOBs becoming the primary contractors for diﬀerent products 
at  a  given  time.  Because  of  this,  these  ﬁxed  eﬀects  help  to  rule  out 
stories that deal with structural shifts of procurement as an explana-
tion, such as the idea that women-led districts only acquire products 
that WOBs oﬀer. Table IA2 in the Internet Appendix explicitly docu-
ments that female representatives do not shift government contracts to 
female-dominated sectors.

We show that the election of a female representative increases the 
probability that a government contract is awarded to a WOB between 
4.1 to 7.1 percentage points, depending on the speciﬁcation. For the 
remainder of the paper, we use Model 3 and hence the discussion of 
economic  magnitude  is  based  on  the  corresponding  estimate  of  4.5 
percentage points. Throughout the paper, we refer to the estimates of 
the coeﬃcients as percentage points, rather than percentages, to avoid 
confusion.  The  linear  probability  model  predicts  a  linear  increase  in 
probabilities and not percentage changes.

From 2005 to 2020, the share of SBA contracts allocated to WOBs 
increased from 16.5% to 27.5%. The eﬀect of a single female repre-
sentative, 4.5 percentage points, is roughly a third of the overall time 
trend.40

To gauge the relative importance of this eﬀect for the entire gov-
ernment contracting system, we provide a back-of-the-envelope calcu-
lation. In 2020, the government procured $665 billion.41 There are 435 
congressional districts, implying that each district allocates contracts 
worth $1.5 billion. Our estimate suggests that the election of a single 
female representative implies a shift of $0.07 billion (4.5 percentage 
points, see Table 4, Model 3). In the 116th Congress, 101 women serve 
as  House  representatives.  Hence,  the  estimate  suggests  a  transfer  of 
around $6.9 billion from non-WOBs to WOBs for the whole of the US. 
This back-of-the-envelope calculation clearly relies on simplifying as-
sumptions. In the Internet Appendix, we provide two pieces of evidence 

justifying our extrapolation. First, on average, more than 75% of gov-
ernment contracts are awarded to small businesses.42 Second, the eﬀect 
of female representation on contract allocation does not depend on ﬁrm 
size as measured by revenue and number of employees.

Next, we discuss the economic relevance for a single ﬁrm in our sam-
ple. The average government contractor received 4.4 contracts under 
the SBA set-aside program.43 The median ﬁrm receiving government 
contracts has annual revenue of $2.1 million and 12 employees. 4% of 
revenue is due to government purchases. The median WOB is smaller, 
with $1.1 million in annual revenue and 7.5 employees. A consider-
ably bigger share of their revenue, 9%, is due to government purchases. 
For 26% of WOBs in the sample, government contracts under the SBA 
program amounted to more than 50% of their revenue.

Taken together, government contracts are important both to the con-
tracting ﬁrms as well as to the overall economy. While the long-term 
eﬀects of government contracts on WOB are beyond the scope of this 
paper, Hvide and Meling (2023) suggest that being rewarded a govern-
ment contract causally increases growth for start-ups even in the long 
run after the contract ends. If our ﬁndings extend to all government 
procurement, the economic impacts are a magnitude of order larger.

2.3.  Validity

In this subsection, we present the evidence on the validity of our 
research design. In particular, we conduct a McCrary (2008) test, we 
report the absence of discontinuities for 20 potential district-level con-
founders, and we show that neither time nor political aﬃliation can 
predict election outcomes.

2.3.1.  McCrary (2008) test

For  RDDs  to  be  valid,  the  distribution  of  election  vote  margins 
for mixed-gender elections has to be continuous around the threshold. 
Fig. 3 presents the results of a McCrary (2008) density test showing that 
the distribution of the assignment variable is smooth across the thresh-

systematically manipulated.

2.4.  Robustness

In this subsection, we present evidence that our results are robust 
to perturbations of our empirical approach. First, we conduct placebo 
regressions randomizing election outcomes. Second, we show that the 
main result is robust to the choice of bandwidth and to removing indi-
vidual states from the sample. Third, we show that the results are robust 
to the gender of the heads of federal agencies.

2.4.1.  Placebo regression

Another concern is that the eﬀect is not due to the ﬁnal treatment, 
whether or not a female legislator represents the district, but due to 
some unobserved variables correlated with the assignment variable, the 
female vote margin. For example, districts that are more likely to elect 
a female legislator might have more female government contractors. 
Whether or not the female legislator is elected at the margin may not 
matter. To rule out this possibility, we implement placebo regressions 
as a falsiﬁcation test. For each iteration, we use our main sample and 
randomize the election outcomes, female versus male winner. All other 
variables are kept constant. If our result is due to treatment, one would 
not expect to ﬁnd any results in the placebo regressions. We run 10,000 
regressions identical to Model 3 in Table 4 and report a histogram of the 
main coeﬃcient, as displayed in Fig. A4. 99.9% of the obtained placebo 
coeﬃcients are lower than our estimate (indicated by a red dashed line 
in the ﬁgure), suggesting that the main eﬀect is due to treatment, the 
legislator’s gender.

For  the  main  analysis,  we  use  a  bandwidth  of  30%.  To  alleviate 
concerns that the results are sensitive to the threshold, we perform the 
main analysis for diﬀerent choices of bandwidth, considering elections 
with a vote margin ranging from 5% to 75%.46

2.4.4.  Gender of government agency heads

Lastly, we explore whether the eﬀect is driven by female legislators 
shifting government contracts to agencies that are led by women, and 
that female-led agencies are more likely to respond to the representa-
tive’s preferences. That is, it may be the gender of the agency leader that 
explains the allocation of contracts to WOBs, not the politician’s gen-
der. We manually gather data on the gender of government agency and 
subagency heads from their websites. We augment our original spec-
iﬁcation with an interaction term, testing whether the gender of the 
agency head acts as a magniﬁer. The results are reported in Table A3 in 
the Appendix.

We do not observe evidence that female-led agencies award more 
contracts to women than other agencies do in the presence of female 
legislators. If anything, female-led agencies award fewer contracts to 
women.  In  our  sample,  agencies  are  rarely  led  by  women.  Only  8% 
(4%) of contracts were awarded by agencies (subagencies) with a fe-
male head. These results are consistent with Fernandez et al. (2013), 
who ﬁnd no evidence that a higher level of female representation in 
federal agencies leads to more contracts for WOBs.

3.  Mechanism

The previous section established that electing a female House repre-
sentative causes an increase in government contracts allocated to WOBs. 
Next, we examine two potential underlying mechanisms: a supply-side 
mechanism and a demand-side mechanism. In the supply-side mecha-
nism, the presence of female representatives changes the behavior or 
the pool of WOBs. This leads to more contract allocation to WOBs de-
spite the government not changing the contract-allocation process. In 
the demand-side mechanism, the government actively allocates more 
contracts to WOBs due to the female representative while ﬁrms do not 
change their behavior. The mechanisms need not to be mutually exclu-
sive. Ideally, one would like to study a setting in which the government 
is “treated” with a female politician while the composition of ﬁrms in 
the economy is unchanged. Such an ideal setting does not exist.

3.1.  Supply-side mechanism

We  start  by  investigating  whether  our  main  eﬀect  is  driven  by 
changes in the pool of potential government contractors and in their be-
havior, a supply-side mechanism. The supply-side mechanism argument 
is that female representatives do not change the government contract 
decisions, but that the mere presence of a female representative im-
proves the environment for women in business and their willingness to 
bid on government contracts. The female congressional presence may 
also inspire women to create businesses that later become government 
contractors. Such a role-model eﬀect is documented by Chizema et al. 
(2015) and Kedia and Pareek (2023), who show that female political 
representation aﬀects board composition.

One  straightforward  test  to  analyze  the  supply-side  mechanism 
would be to examine how contract bidding changed after the election of

3.2.  Dynamics of the demand-side mechanism

3.1.3.  Short-term eﬀects

Lastly, we investigate whether the eﬀect of female representatives’ 
contracts exists in the short term. If our main results were driven by the 
supply-side mechanism, we would expect to see increased WOB entry, 
which takes time.

We reestimate our main speciﬁcation, Equation (2), but restrict the 
sample to contracts awarded between the term start date and 365 and 
180 days after the representative’s election. Models 3 and 4 in Table 7
suggest that the main eﬀect already exists within 180 days of the elec-
tion.  As  outlined  above,  this  evidence  is  likely  inconsistent  with  the 
supply-side mechanism.

Across the various tests, we fail to ﬁnd support for the supply-side 
mechanism. This conclusion rests on the assumption that the pool of 
potential contractors is representative of the pool of bidders and that 
increased bidding by WOBs is not oﬀset by a one-to-one decrease in 
bidding by non-WOBs. Thus, we infer that the main eﬀect is driven by 
the demand-side mechanism: the government actively allocating more 
contracts to WOBs because of the female representative while ﬁrms do 
not change their behavior.

In a contemporaneous working paper, Krause and Sevilir (2023) doc-
ument that the election of a female mayor has a positive eﬀect on female 
start-up activity. In the Internet Appendix, Section IA.2, we reconcile 
this evidence with our ﬁnding that female representatives have no ef-
fect on the pool of government contractors. First, we show that there 
is little overlap with the start-up ecosystem Krause and Sevilir (2023)
analyze. Second, we show that the number of start-ups does not mean-
ingfully change in the relatively short horizons around the elections that 
we consider.

We turn our analysis to studying the dynamics of the demand-side 
mechanism. First, we explore the persistence of the main eﬀect: Does 
the increased allocation of contracts to WOBs persist if the female leg-
islator is replaced by a man? Conversely, is there a diﬀerence between 
conﬁrming a female incumbent versus electing a new female successor? 
Second, we test whether the main eﬀect concentrates in female legis-
lators with oversight power of government agencies, which speaks to 
how female legislators aﬀect government contract allocation. We end 
this section with an analysis of local networks and political aﬃliation.

3.2.1.  Persistence

Does temporary representation by a female legislator have a persis-
tent eﬀect? Minority groups might suﬀer from decision-makers’ inat-
tention to them and initial beliefs that they will perform worse (Bartos 
et al., 2016; Huang et al., 2023).48 Exposure to minority contractors 
might permanently modify contracting. On the other hand, evidence 
that the loss of political connections between ﬁrms and legislators af-
fects contract allocation and contract terms (Tahoun, 2014; Brogaard et 
al., 2021) suggests that the eﬀects might not persist beyond the legisla-
tors’ time in oﬃce.

We deviate slightly from our main regression approach because fo-
cusing on mixed-gender elections with a vote margin of 30% or less 
does not take into account that the election of a female candidate might 
aﬀect who will run for oﬃce going forward. For example, imagine that 
the election of a female legislator increases the probability that the los-
ing party will put forward a female candidate in the next election. In 
this case, the successive elections would not be in our sample. Because

mechanism would be consistent with our previous ﬁnding that the ef-
fect increases over time for a given individual female legislator because 
participation and seniority in committees is tied to an individual and

3.2.4.  Political aﬃliation

Next,  we  explore  heterogeneity  associated  with  the  party  of  the 
elected  representative.  Table 10 reports  estimates  of  our  main  spec-
iﬁcation  augmented  with  an  indicator  variable  equaling  one  if  the 

representative is Republican. The impact of female representatives on 
government contract allocation is bigger, both statistically and econom-
ically, when the female representative is aﬃliated with the Republican 
party. Speciﬁcally, the eﬀect is 4.2 percentage points higher, at the 1% 
signiﬁcance level.

While we cannot pinpoint the exact mechanism, it can be reasoned 
that female representatives from both of the major US political parties 
prioritize  the  needs  of  their  female  constituents.  For  example,  Sena-
tor  Jeanne  Shaheen,  Democrat  from  New  Hampshire,  says,  “Women 
entrepreneurs  may  be  the  country’s  greatest  untapped  economic  re-
source.”54 However, their emphasis on speciﬁc issues may vary. Gen-
erally, Republicans tend to prioritize business-related topics; see Witko 
and Friedman (2008). For example, Republicans stated in their 2012 
platform  that  “[s]mall  businesses  are  the  leaders  in  the  world’s  ad-
vances in technology and innovation, and we pledge to strengthen that 
role and foster small business entrepreneurship.”55 Taken together, fe-
male representatives from both parties contribute to increased contract 
allocation to WOBs, with female Republican representatives exerting a 
more substantial inﬂuence.

The argument above is in line with survey evidence on the politi-
cal orientation of small business owners. Almost 50% of small business 
owners identify as Republican or have Republican political leanings.56

4.  Contract and ﬁrm outcomes

4.1.  Contract terms and execution

This subsection addresses whether changes in government contrac-
tor composition aﬀect the costs and quality of government procurement. 
If female politicians reduce a preexisting and value-destroying gender 
bias  in  government  contracting,  this  reallocation  will  beneﬁt  the  US 
government because contract quality will improve. However, if female 
politicians  lead  to  more  contracts  to  WOBs  rather  than  to  the  best-
bidding ﬁrm, contract quality will deteriorate. It is also possible that 
the quality distribution among bidders at the margin is fairly uniform 
such that they are largely interchangeable. If so, the allocation to WOBs 
versus non-WOBs will not impact contract quality.

4.2.  Firm outcomes

Hvide and Meling (2023) document that receiving government con-
tracts has a causal and long-term eﬀect on ﬁrms. They focus on Norwe-

ﬁrms that win a procurement auction have 20% higher revenue and 
employment even several years after the original procurement auction. 
This suggests that we should expect that access to government contracts 
for WOBs has a long-term eﬀect.

5.  Conclusion

This paper provides novel evidence that female political representa-
tion causes an increase in government contracts allocated to WOBs. For 
identiﬁcation, we exploit mixed-gender elections and the RDD.

The  results  are  driven  by  a  demand-side  mechanism:  female  leg-
islators alter government procurement and ﬁrms do not change their 
behavior. We show that the main eﬀect grows over the representative’s 
tenure and that it concentrates in female legislators who are part of con-
gressional committees. In contrast, changes in the supply of government 
contractors do not explain the results. We do not observe signiﬁcant 
economic costs associated with more gender-balanced representation in 
contracting.

Taken together, we provide evidence that the gender gap can be 
narrowed by exposure to leaders from the groups that experience bias. 
Female representatives have a causal impact on WOBs receiving govern-
ment contracts. In the case of government contract allocation, the eﬀect 
of female representation is economically large and can play a role simi-
lar to that of programs dedicated to helping women-owned ﬁrms. That
