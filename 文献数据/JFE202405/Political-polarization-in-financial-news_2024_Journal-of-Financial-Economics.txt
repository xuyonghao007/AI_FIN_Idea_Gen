Comparing coverage of the same corporate ﬁnancial news by the conservative Wall Street Journal and the liberal 
New York Times, we ﬁnd strong evidence of political polarization in their reporting on both the intensive and 
extensive margins of coverage. We show that this politics-induced disagreement in corporate ﬁnancial news leads 
to an increase in abnormal trading volume for the most politically extreme ﬁrms. Our results highlight a new 
source of investor disagreement, arising out of polarized reporting of corporate ﬁnancial news, that generates 
trade among investors.

1.  Introduction

Reporting on Amazon’s quarterly earnings announcement on Jan-
uary 31, 2020, a New York Times article headline read “Amazon Powers 
Forward  With  Hefty  Quarterly  Proﬁt”,  while  the  Wall  Street  Journal
headline said “Amazon Misses $1 Trillion Milestone”. Amazon is more 
politically aligned with the Democratic Party: in 2020, 85% of Amazon’s 
employee and PAC campaign contributions supported political candi-
dates from the Democratic Party.Motivated by this example, we use a 
broad sample of corporations to test if the diﬀerence in coverage be-
tween media sources is driven by politics, and whether such diﬀerential 
coverage generates disagreement among investors.

Theory suggests that investor disagreement leads to trade (Varian, 
1985;  Milgrom  and  Stokey,  1982;  Karpoﬀ,  1986;  Harris  and  Raviv, 
1993),  where  disagreement  may  arise  because  of  heterogeneous  pri-
ors (Harris and Raviv, 1993; Kandel and Pearson, 1995) or diﬀerential 
information  (Hong  and  Stein,  1999).  Recent  empirical  evidence  has 
shown that both sources of disagreement can matter but has mainly 
used investor-centric measures of information and beliefs (see for exam-
ple Cookson and Niessner (2020); Cookson et al. (2023)). As ﬁnancial 
media are a major component of information dissemination, we study 

whether the production of ﬁrm-speciﬁc ﬁnancial news is aﬀected by 
politics and if this is an important source of informational disagreement 
among investors.

Using  political  polarization  as  the  empirical  setting,  we  study 
whether the opposing political ideologies of two major ﬁnancial news 
sources,  the  Wall  Street  Journal and  the  New  York  Times,  aﬀects  the 
reporting of corporate ﬁnancial news, and if politics-induced disagree-
ment in corporate news generates trade in a company’s stock. Our main 
ﬁndings are as follows: First, we ﬁnd evidence of political polarization 
in the coverage of corporate ﬁnancial news. We show that newspapers 
use more positive language and are more likely to report good rather 
than  bad  news  about  politically  aligned  ﬁrms.  These  eﬀects  become 
more pronounced following the sale of the Wall Street Journal to the 
more conservative News Corporation. Second, we ﬁnd that the greater 
the disagreement in the tone and content of corporate ﬁnancial news 
between news sources, the higher the abnormal daily trading volume in 
the most politically extreme ﬁrms. Our results highlight a new source 
of investor disagreement that generates trade.

The empirical literature on investor disagreement has studied the 
eﬀect  of  diﬀerences  in  information  and  beliefs  on  investor  disagree-

that diﬀerences in both investor beliefs and information may generate

We make two main contributions to this literature. First, we intro-
duce a direct measure of information disagreement arising out of pol-
itics and show that this disagreement occurs in primary news sources, 
which is a necessary building block for the diﬀerential information sets 
of investors. Second, we show that the politics-induced disagreement 
in the reporting of ﬁnancial news about ﬁrms aﬀects trading volume. 
Importantly, our setting of political polarization satisﬁes the two condi-
tions necessary for information diﬀerences to sustain investor disagree-
ment and thereby generate trade: (1) polarization leads news sources to 
diﬀer systematically in their coverage and, (2) people consume diﬀerent 
news sources that match their political views.

We also make the following unique contributions to the literature on 
polarization in the news, which has shown that readers seek out news 
sources  that  conﬁrm  their  political  views  (Iyengar  and  Hahn,  2009; 
Gentzkow and Shapiro, 2011; Bakshy et al., 2015).2 First, our paper pro-
vides novel evidence on the production side of news, shedding unique 
light into decisions about the intensive and extensive choices of cov-
erage. Second, we contribute to our understanding of polarization by 
showing that the trend in media polarization extends beyond political 
topics into corporate ﬁnancial news, which is expected to be objective 
(Gentzkow and Shapiro, 2006).

Lastly,  we  contribute  to  the  literature  on  ﬁnancial  news  and  the 
stock market.3 Unlike the coverage of political news (Mullainathan and 
Shleifer,  2005;  Groseclose  and  Milyo,  2005;  Gentzkow  and  Shapiro, 
2006, 2010; Boxell et al., 2020), corporate ﬁnancial news is not ex-
pected to be covered with political slant because the core purpose of 
this news is to inform the ﬁnancial decisions of readers. In fact, a large 
literature has studied the eﬀect of ﬁnancial news on stock markets with-
out considering the political slant of this news.4 We add to this literature 
by showing the novel result that the same corporate ﬁnancial news is 
covered diﬀerently based on the political alignment between the ﬁrm 
and the news source, which impacts trading behavior.

To identify the impact of political polarization on ﬁnancial news we 
need to ﬁrst measure political alignment between the newspaper and 
ﬁrm, and second, distinguish political alignment from other newspaper 
and ﬁrm characteristics that may also aﬀect coverage. To do this we 
compare three decades of articles published in the conservative Wall 
Street  Journal (WSJ)  and  the  liberal  New  York  Times (NYT)  covering 
ﬁnancial news on the 100 largest listed ﬁrms in the United States. These 
are the two most widely circulated national newspapers with business 
news sections, and their editorial pages occupy opposite ends of the 
political spectrum (Gentzkow and Shapiro, 2010).5 We use campaign

To isolate the eﬀect of political alignment on coverage and trading 
from other ﬁrm and newspaper characteristics, we follow the method-
ology in Fracassi et al. (2016) and Kempf and Tsoutsoura (2021) and 
include interacted Firm × Time and Paper × Time ﬁxed eﬀects, which 
absorb time-varying ﬁrm and newspaper characteristics. We also con-
trol for article topic ﬁxed-eﬀects, ﬁrm-newspaper-speciﬁc advertising 
expenditures, ﬁrm size, age, and employment, and compare coverage 
of the same ﬁrm-speciﬁc news events.

We  observe  diﬀerences  in  both  the  intensive  and  extensive  mar-
gins of coverage based on the political alignment between the ﬁrm and 
the newspaper. On the intensive margin, we ﬁnd that newspapers use 
more positive words and tone in articles about politically aligned ﬁrms. 
For example, the WSJ uses 15% more positive words than the NYT in 
articles about ﬁrms that donate only to Republican Party candidates 
compared to ﬁrms that donate only to Democratic Party candidates. On 
the extensive margin, we show that a newspaper is more likely to pub-
lish good rather than bad ﬁnancial news and write longer articles about 
politically aligned ﬁrms.

Additionally, we ﬁnd that the Wall Street Journal is more likely to 
publish an article and use more positive tone about Republican-leaning 
ﬁrms, following its acquisition by the more conservative News Corpo-
ration. Using a sub-sample of articles written by journalists who switch 
jobs between the two newspapers, we ﬁnd that journalists change their 
tone to reﬂect the ideology of the newspaper, which shows that the tone 
of coverage depends on the ﬁrm’s political alignment with the newspa-
per, not the journalist.

To identify the impact of politics-induced disagreement on trading 
we need to ﬁrst measure disagreement in coverage, and second, distin-
guish the impact of disagreement in the news from that of newsworthy 
events. To measure disagreement, we compare articles written on the 
same day about the same ﬁrm in both newspapers, and use three mea-
sures of diﬀerential coverage. These include the absolute value of the 
diﬀerence in the tone of coverage; absolute value of the diﬀerence in 
positive words; and, cosine similarity, which measures similarity in the 
words used in the articles.

To distinguish disagreement from omitted variables that may also 
aﬀect volume, we study the diﬀerential impact of disagreement on the 
abnormal volume of the most politically extreme ﬁrms (top 20th per-
centile of campaign contributions to either political party) compared to 
less politically extreme ﬁrms. This mitigates the identiﬁcation concern 
that news events rather than disagreement in coverage drives volume 
because such events are not more likely to occur for politically extreme 
ﬁrms. Lastly, we control for observable and unobservable, time-varying 
ﬁrm and news characteristics that may aﬀect abnormal volume.

We ﬁnd that the greater the disagreement in coverage between the 
two newspapers, the greater the increase in abnormal trading volume 
for the most politically extreme ﬁrms. For example, comparing days 
with no diﬀerence in the tone of coverage to days with the maximum 
diﬀerence in tone, daily abnormal trading volume is 30% higher for 
a top political donor compared to a non-top donor. We observe simi-
lar results using the diﬀerence in positive words and cosine similarity 
between articles in both newspapers about the same ﬁrm. In addition, 
we show that these results are not driven by speciﬁc news topics or by 
the largest ﬁrms. For example, disagreement in the coverage of corpo-
rate earnings announcements has the same impact on abnormal volume 
as disagreement about other news topics. Overall our results show that 
there is political bias in the reporting of corporate ﬁnancial news, and

2.  Data

1.1.  Related literature

In addition to the literature discussed above, our paper is related to 
studies that examine the responses of ﬁrms and investors to political 
bias in the media. Luo et al. (2020) ﬁnd that following the acquisition 
of Dow Jones by News Corporation, stock prices of Republican ﬁrms re-
spond less to Dow Jones Newswires sentiment although they do not ﬁnd 
an increase in bias, which they interpret as a ‘fake news’ eﬀect. Balo-
ria and Heese (2018) assume that Fox News is politically biased against 
Democratic ﬁrms, and show that ﬁrms aﬃliated with the Democratic 
Party  that  are  located  in  markets  with  Fox  News  channels  suppress 
bad news, and Knill et al. (2019) show that ﬁrms led by Republican-
leaning CEOs headquartered in regions where Fox News was introduced 
increased  their  investment  expenditures  during  the  Bush  presidency. 
While these studies assume there is political slant in a single media out-
let, we ﬁnd strong evidence of political polarization in the coverage of 
corporate ﬁnancial news across media outlets, and show that this can 
generate disagreement in the information sets of investors.

Second, our paper is broadly related to studies that examine whether 
connections between ﬁrms and the media aﬀect coverage. For example, 
Reuter and Zitzewitz (2006) and Gurun and Butler (2012) show that 
coverage may be correlated with advertising, and Dyck et al. (2008)
ﬁnd that public relations by an investment fund increased coverage of 
corporate governance violations by Russian ﬁrms. Our results are robust 
to  controlling  for  advertising  expenditures  by  ﬁrms  in  each  newspa-
per.

Third, our paper is related to the literature on the impact of ﬁnan-
cial news on markets. For example, Huberman and Regev (2001) and 
Tetlock (2011) observe that stock market returns respond to stale news; 
Barber and Odean (2008) ﬁnd that individual investors buy stocks re-
ported in the news; Fang and Peress (2009) show that media coverage 
reduces information frictions; Engelberg and Parsons (2011) ﬁnd that 
local media coverage predicts local trading; Dougal et al. (2012) show 
that short-term returns can be predicted using ﬁxed-eﬀects for colum-
nists at the WSJ; Garcia (2013) ﬁnds that the predictability of stock 
returns using news content is concentrated in recessions; Hillert et al. 
(2014) show that the media can exacerbate investor biases; Ben-Rephael 
et al. (2017) show that the impact of news on ﬁnancial markets de-
pends  on  the  nature  of  the  readership;  and,  Goldman  et  al.  (2022)
provide  a  theory  of  the  informational  role  of  ﬁnancial  media  in  se-
lectively disseminating ﬁrm announcements. The above studies do not 
take into account political slant in the reporting of ﬁnancial news about 
ﬁrms.

Lastly, our paper contributes to the politics and ﬁnance literature 
that ﬁnds positive eﬀects of political connections, campaign contribu-
tions, and lobbying on ﬁrm value (see, for example, Fisman, 2001; Fac-
cio, 2006; Borisov et al., 2016; Akey, 2015), and documents the eﬀect 
of political bias on economic expectations (Mian et al., 2017; Meeuwis 
et al., 2019; Kempf and Tsoutsoura, 2021; Cookson et al., 2020). For ex-
ample, Mian et al. (2017) ﬁnd that Republican party aligned households 
had more optimistic economic expectations following the 2016 election 
than Democratic party aligned households; Meeuwis et al. (2019) show 
that political party aﬃliation aﬀected household investment behavior 
following the 2016 presidential election; Kempf and Tsoutsoura (2021)
show that credit rating analysts behave in a partisan fashion based on 
the party of the president, which aﬀects their broad economic outlook; 
and, Cookson et al. (2020) ﬁnd that during the COVID-19 crisis Repub-
lican investors were more optimistic about stocks that had suﬀered the 
most. While these papers study how political biases aﬀect the broad eco-
nomic outlook of individuals, we study how political biases aﬀect the 
production of information about a cross-section of ﬁrms and the impact 
on investors.

Based on market capitalization in 2017, we conduct our analysis on 
the largest 100 publicly traded U.S. ﬁrms, with at least 10 articles that 
mention the ﬁrm published in either the Wall Street Journal or the New 
York Times between 1991 and 2016.7 We focus on these larger ﬁrms be-
cause they are likely to receive more news coverage. We choose the New 
York Times and Wall Street Journal for three reasons: First, these are the 
two most widely circulated national newspapers in the United States 
that are also important sources of business news; Second, their edito-
rial positions occupy opposite ends of the political spectrum (Gentzkow 
and Shapiro, 2010), which allows us to study the eﬀects of political po-
larization on ﬁnancial news coverage; Third, the ﬁnance literature has 
used these newspapers to study the eﬀect of ﬁnancial news on mar-
kets (Huberman and Regev, 2001; Tetlock, 2007; Dougal et al., 2012; 
Garcia, 2013).

From Factiva we gather all articles from the print editions of the New 
York Times and the Wall Street Journal that mention any of the ﬁrms in 
our sample between 1991 and 2016. We collect the text of each article, 
section and page numbers, topic codes that classify articles, and the 
name of the journalist when identiﬁed. To clearly identify the content 
of the article with a speciﬁc ﬁrm, we focus on articles that only mention 
one ﬁrm.

We use the standard Loughran and McDonald (2011) ﬁnancial dic-
tionary to classify the tone of a ﬁnancial news article. We count the 
number of positive and negative words in each article to create our mea-
sures of tone. Speciﬁcally we measure the tone of an article using the 
following variables: Positive Words is equal to Positive Words/(Positive + 
Negative Words); and Tone is equal to (Positive-Negative Words)/(Positive 
+ Negative Words). Both variables are expressed in percentages.

To construct an additional direct measure of good and bad news 
we search news articles for sentences describing a ﬁrm’s stock returns, 
share price, etc., and determine whether the sentence describes posi-
tive returns (e.g., “share prices rose”, “prices soared”, “positive return”, 
etc.), or negative returns (e.g., “stock price fell”, “prices were down”, 
“negative return”, etc.). We then aggregate the number of times a news-
paper reports positive returns and negative returns for each stock during 
each 2-year election cycle, and create the variable %Good News as the 
number  of  articles  that  mention  positive  returns  divided  by  the  to-
tal number of articles mentioning either positive or negative returns 
about a stock in each newspaper.8 To reduce noise, we require a min-
imum of 1 mention per quarter, on average, during a given election 
cycle.

To measure a ﬁrm’s political alignment, we collect data on cam-
paign contributions made by employees and Political Action Commit-
tees  (PACs)  of  ﬁrms  from  the  Center  for  Responsive  Politics,  which 
obtains the data from the Federal Election Commission. Donations are 
available for every two-year election cycle between 1990 and 2016. We 
aggregate donations to the ﬁrm level and construct the fraction of total 
campaign contributions by employees and ﬁrm-level PACs to Republi-
can Party candidates in an election cycle (% Contributions to Republican 
Party), which is approximately equal to 1-%Contributions to Democratic 
Party since almost all ﬁrm contributions go to the two parties. We match 
campaign contributions data from the previous two-year election cy-
cle for each ﬁrm to each year of the ﬁnancial news data. Lastly, we 
obtain ﬁrm-level market data from CRSP and ﬁnancial characteristics 
from Compustat.

The third measure of disagreement in the news is Cosine Dissimilarity, 
which captures the extent to which the words used by the two newspa-
pers in articles about the same ﬁrm are diﬀerent. For each day on which 
there is equal coverage in both newspapers, we create a measure of the 
dissimilarity of the words used in the articles between the two newspa-
pers. For consistency with our other measure of disagreement in tone, 
we deﬁne Cosine Dissimilarity as 1-Cosine Similarity of the words across 
articles in the two newspapers. Cosine Similarity is the angle between 
the two newspapers’ “word vectors”, which are n-dimensional numer-
ical vectors where each element is the number of times a given word 
appears in the article(s) and n is the number of unique words across all 
articles.9 More formally, Cosine Similarity is the dot product of the two 
word vectors normalized by the vector length. When there is no over-
lap in words in the two newspapers, Cosine Dissimilarity is equal to 1. 
When both newspapers use exactly the same words, Cosine Dissimilarity
is equal to 0.

We deﬁne Abnormal Volume as the daily dollar trading volume of a 
ﬁrm divided by the average daily dollar trading volume over the previ-
ous 30 days. We deﬁne politically extreme ﬁrms with the variable Top 
Donor, which is an indicator variable that is equal to one if the ﬁrm is in 
the top 20th percentile of campaign contributions to either Republican 
or Democratic Party candidates in a given cycle.

6.1.  Volume, tone, and ﬁrm size

We  check  whether  the  results  for  volume  and  tone  diﬀer  for  the 
largest ﬁrms in the sample. We create an indicator variable that is equal 
to one for the largest 50 ﬁrms based on market capitalization in a given 
quarter-year  (Above  Median  Size).  We  interact  this  variable  with  Top 
Donor × Diﬀerence in Tone in speciﬁcation (4). The results are reported 
in Table 8A. We report results without ﬁxed eﬀects and with robust 
standard errors in column (1) and progressively saturate the model with 
higher-order ﬁxed eﬀects in columns (2) and (3). We ﬁnd that while the 
coeﬃcient of the interaction between top donor ﬁrms and diﬀerence 
in tone of coverage remains positive and statistically signiﬁcant, the 
coeﬃcient of the triple interaction term is not statistically signiﬁcant. 
These results suggest that the eﬀect of politics- induced disagreement 
on volume is similar for the largest and smallest ﬁrms in the sample.

In Table 8B we interact Above Median Size with WSJ × % Contribu-

7.  Conclusion

Comparing 25 years of corporate ﬁnancial news articles in the lib-
eral New York Times to the conservative Wall Street Journal, we ﬁnd 
evidence of political polarization in the reporting of this news. Specif-
ically, we ﬁnd that the tone and content of coverage of ﬁrm-speciﬁc 
ﬁnancial news varies based on the political alignment between the ﬁrm 
and newspaper.

Based on this ﬁnding, we directly measure disagreement between 
primary news sources in the production of corporate ﬁnancial news, 
and show that this generates disagreement among investors. In partic-
ular, we ﬁnd that for the most politically extreme ﬁrms, disagreement 
in the tone and content of coverage of corporate ﬁnancial news leads 
to higher abnormal trading volume. Since political polarization implies 
that market participants may be exposed to diﬀerent news, this empiri-
cal setting satisﬁes the conditions necessary for information diﬀerences 
to sustain investor disagreement.
