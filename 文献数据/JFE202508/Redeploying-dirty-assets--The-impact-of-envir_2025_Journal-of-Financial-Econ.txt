A B S T R A C T

This paper investigates how firms’ pollution incentives are influenced by their ability to divest polluted assets. 
My empirical setting is a major reform that exempts purchasers from liability for past contamination. Using 
a difference-in-differences framework, I find that the reform reduces toxic emissions, lowers bankruptcy risk, 
and increases firm value. Cross-sectional tests show that the decline in emissions is driven by firms with 
weaker financial health and fewer assets. These findings highlight a novel net worth channel: by limiting 
ex-post liability, the reform enhances landowners’ net worth ex-ante, reducing their incentives to engage in 
risky behavior, such as excessive emissions.

1. Introduction

Corporations are under growing pressure to divest polluting assets, 
with major firms offloading billions of dollars worth of high-emission 
assets in response to rising demands for climate action.1 While policies 
designed to improve the liquidity of polluting assets may facilitate such 
divestitures, their environmental implications remain unclear. Recent 
research suggests that these policies might be ineffective, as emissions 
are transferred from sellers to buyers following the divestiture of pol-
luting assets (Duchin et al., 2024). However, understanding the full 
environmental impact of such policies requires examining their ex-ante
effects — how firms adjust their environmental behavior when they 
anticipate being able to sell polluting assets more easily or at higher 
prices. In this paper, I address this gap by investigating the environ-
mental implications of improving the liquidity of polluting assets, with 
a particular focus on their ex-ante effects.

Estimating the environmental effects of improved liquidity poses 
two challenges: first, accurately measuring the liquidity of brown as-
sets, and second, addressing potential correlations between this mea-
sure and unobservable firm characteristics that influence environmen-
tal behavior. To overcome these challenges, I exploit the passage of 
the  Brownfields  Act  in  2002  as  a  natural  experiment.  This  reform 
facilitated industrial land transactions by exempting purchasers from 
prior cleanup liabilities and disproportionately impacting certain firms, 
enabling  a  difference-in-differences  research  design.  I  find  that,  in 

response to the reform, affected landowners reduce toxic emissions, 
experience a decline in bankruptcy risk, and observe an increase in 
firm value. I attribute these effects to the reform increasing landown-
ers’ net worth, thereby reducing their incentive to take risks through 
higher emissions. These findings suggest that while policies promoting 
divestiture  might  be  ineffective  ex-post,  they  can  generate  positive 
environmental outcomes ex-ante, especially when driven by reductions 
in purchaser liability.

In  the  United  States,  land  purchasers  can  inherit  environmental 
liabilities, becoming responsible for contamination caused by previ-
ous owners after a transaction. These liabilities are often substantial 
and unpredictable, with cleanup costs sometimes reaching billions of 
dollars (Eisen, 1996; Greenstone and Gallagher, 2008). As a result, 
purchasers have been hesitant to acquire industrial land, leading to 
an  illiquid  land  market  where  many  industrial  sites  remain  aban-
doned (Ramseur, 2008; Hayes and Dinkin, 1988). To address this issue, 
Congress enacted the Small Business Liability Relief and Brownfields 
Revitalization Act (Brownfields Act hereafter) in 2002, which reduced 
purchasers’ exposure to cleanup liabilities caused by previous owners.
I  utilize  the  Brownfields  Act  as  an  empirical  setting,  exploiting 
its  unique  features  to  construct  treated  and  control  groups.  In  the 
U.S., environmental cleanups are governed by two primary statutes: 
the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Lia-
bility Act (CERCLA) and the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act

(RCRA). A key aspect of the Brownfields Act is that it only exempts 
purchasers from liability under CERCLA (EPA, 2022). As a result, plants 
regulated solely by CERCLA are particularly affected by the reform. 
In contrast, plants regulated by both CERCLA and RCRA serve as a 
natural comparison group, as the risk of incurring RCRA cleanup costs 
continues to deter purchasers (Burnham-Howard, 2004). Leveraging 
this institutional detail, I classify plants regulated solely by CERCLA 
as the treated group and RCRA-regulated plants as the control group.

I perform three distinct tests to validate this classification. First, 
I  examine  whether  treated  plants  exhibit  higher  exposure  to  CER-
CLA prior to the reform, as this would indicate that their purchasers 
benefited more from the reform’s liability protection. Consistent with 
this,  I  find  that  the  EPA  is  more  likely  to  use  CERCLA  to  address 
contamination at treated plants compared to control plants prior to 
the  reform.  Second,  I  show  that  treated  plants  incurred  almost  no 
RCRA cleanups prior to the reform, indicating that RCRA liabilities 
had minimal impact on their redeployment. Finally, I demonstrate that, 
following the reform, the treated group increased its propensity to sell 
assets relative to the control group. These results further validate my 
treatment classification, as they align with existing research showing 
that reducing purchaser liability enhances the liquidity of industrial 
land (Sigman, 2010; Howland, 2000; McGrath, 2000).

After validating my empirical design, I analyze the reform’s impact 
on industrial pollution. Theoretically, the reform’s effect on emissions 
is ambiguous. On the one hand, reducing purchasers’ liability increases 
landowners’ net worth by enhancing the value and liquidity of their 
land. This increased net worth mitigates incentives to take risks through 
excessive pollution, a mechanism I term the net worth channel. Addi-
tionally, if total cleanup liability remains fixed, reducing purchasers’ 
liability shifts a greater burden onto the current landowner, poten-
tially leading to lower emissions, as the current owner would bear 
a larger share of the cleanup costs in the event of contamination. I 
refer to this as the liability-shifting channel. On the other hand, the 
reform could also lead to higher emissions by enabling firms to sell 
treated plants more easily and at higher prices, even when they become 
contaminated.

I use granular data from the EPA’s Toxic Release Inventory (TRI) 
database to analyze the environmental effects of the Brownfields Act. 
Treatment  is  defined  at  both  the  plant  and  firm  levels,  with  firms 
classified  as  treated  if  the  majority  of  their  plants  are  affected  by 
the  reform.  My  most  stringent  specification  includes  chemical-year, 
plant-chemical, firm, industry-year, and state-year fixed effects. This 
approach identifies the effect of the Brownfields Act by comparing 
emissions from plants within the same industry, using the same chem-
ical, in the same state, but with different levels of exposure to the 
reform. I find that treated firms reduce emissions by approximately 7% 
compared to control firms following the Brownfields Act. While treated 
plants also exhibit some reduction in emissions, the effects are more 
modest, with point estimates indicating a decline between 2.8% and 
4.1%, accompanied by weak statistical significance. In a ‘‘horse race’’ 
specification that includes both plant and firm treatment variables, the 
results indicate that the reduction in emissions is driven entirely by 
firm-wide decreases. Although both the net worth and liability-shifting 
channels suggest a decline in emissions, the firm-wide reduction is more 
consistent with the net worth channel. This is because an increase in a 
firm’s net worth reduces emissions across all plants, whereas the shift 
in liability affects only the treated plants.

To  further  distinguish  between  the  two  mechanisms,  I  conduct 
several additional analyses. First, I examine the reform’s impact on 
net worth, a variable for which the two mechanisms yield contrasting 
predictions. The net worth channel suggests that the reform positively 
affects the net worth of treated landowners by increasing land value, 
while the liability-shifting channel predicts that transferring purchaser 
liability  to  landowners  would  negatively  impact  their  net  worth.  I 
find evidence consistent with the net worth channel. After the reform,

treated firms exhibit improved financial health, as indicated by in-
creases in Altman’s Z score, and higher firm value, measured by Tobin’s 
Q. These improvements in net worth are economically significant, with 
Altman’s Z score and Tobin’s Q increasing by approximately 10% to 
15% relative to their sample mean.

Next, I perform cross-sectional tests to further pinpoint the under-
lying mechanisms. The net worth mechanism, which reduces emissions 
by mitigating incentives to take risks through higher emissions, should 
have the greatest impact on firms where pollution-shifting incentives 
are strongest — such as firms with high bankruptcy risk or fewer assets. 
In contrast, the liability-shifting mechanism has a smaller effect on 
these firms, as liabilities transferred from purchasers are more likely to 
be discharged in bankruptcy. The evidence shows that emissions reduc-
tions are more significant among firms with stronger pollution-shifting 
incentives, providing further support for the net worth channel.

Finally, I examine the reform’s impact on economic activity. Since 
real estate can serve as collateral, an increase in land value should 
alleviate the financing constraints of landowners, thereby boosting their 
economic activity (Ersahin and Irani, 2020; Chaney et al., 2012). Ad-
ditionally, improved liquidity in the land market facilitates a more ef-
ficient allocation of resources, enhancing the productivity of landown-
ers (Gavazza, 2011). Thus, if the net worth channel drives the results, 
we should observe increased economic activity following the reform. 
Conversely, a negative effect on economic activity would support the 
liability-shifting mechanism, where redirected liability raises the cost 
of capital for landowners and reduces their economic activity. Using 
plant-chemical-level  production  data  from  the  EPA  and  plant-level 
employment data from the National Establishment Time Series (NETS), 
I find that the reform has a modest but positive impact on economic 
activity. These findings are consistent with the net worth channel and 
contradict the liability-shifting mechanism.

This paper contributes to the literature on the divestiture of brown 
assets. Prior research shows that the divestment of financial assets, such 
as equity and debt, is generally an ineffective strategy for improving so-
cial outcomes and may even be counterproductive (Edmans and Levit, 
2022; Berk and Van Binsbergen, 2025; Broccardo et al., 2022; Becht 
et al., 2023; Kahn et al., 2023; Kacperczyk and Peydró, 2022; Sachdeva 
et al., 2023; Heath et al., 2023; Green and Roth, 2024; Hartzmark and 
Shue, 2023; Bellon and Boualam, 2024). An exception to this occurs 
when there is an inelastic supply of capital, making it difficult for 
firms targeted by divestiture campaigns to substitute into alternative 
sources of financing (Green and Vallee, 2024). In contrast, research on 
real asset divestitures remains limited. Duchin et al. (2024) and Berg 
et al. (2003) show that when firms are pressured to improve their 
environmental performance, they often sell their polluting assets to 
buyers facing less environmental scrutiny. Duchin et al. (2024) further 
track emissions from divested plants and find no significant reduction, 
interpreting this as evidence of greenwashing, where firms divest dirty 
assets to appear cleaner without achieving meaningful environmental 
improvements.

This paper contributes to the literature in three key ways. First, 
I demonstrate that divestitures policies that increase the demand for 
brown assets can yield positive social outcomes, particularly in the 
context of reducing purchaser liability. Second, I show that such di-
vestitures are effective because they enhance the net worth of current 
owners, thereby reducing their incentives to pollute. This contrasts with 
policies that encourage the divestiture of financial assets, which may 
negatively impact the target firm’s financial health and worsen envi-
ronmental behavior (Hartzmark and Shue, 2023; Bellon and Boualam, 
2024). Third, to my knowledge, this paper is the first to explore the 
ex-ante effects of divestiture policies. Given the increasing interest in 
tackling environmental issues, understanding such ex-ante effects helps 
inform policymakers on how to design effective divestiture policies.

This paper also contributes to the literature on environmental liabil-
ity. This line of work studies the consequences of imposing environmen-
tal liability on shareholders and lenders (Akey and Appel, 2021; Bellon,

affecting environmental behavior.

2. Institutional background

2.1. Background on environmental cleanup

In the U.S., environmental cleanups are governed by two statutes: 
the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Lia-
bility Act (CERCLA) and the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act 
(RCRA).

CERCLA, enacted in 1980, was designed to fund the cleanup of 
contaminated sites by granting the EPA authority to impose liability 
on a broad range of parties, known as Potentially Responsible Parties 
(PRPs). These PRPs include past and current property owners, as well 
as  parties  involved  in  the  transportation  or  disposal  of  hazardous 
substances (Weissman and Sowinski Jr, 2015). Sites subject to CER-
CLA cleanups are listed on the National Priorities List (NPL) and are 
commonly referred to as NPL sites.

RCRA,  enacted  in  1976,  grants  the  EPA  the  authority  to  regu-
late hazardous waste. Initially, RCRA focused on the management of 
hazardous waste at operating facilities, with limited provisions for envi-
ronmental cleanups. However, after 1984, RCRA’s cleanup provisions, 
known  as  corrective  actions,  became  more  comprehensive,  aligning 
more closely with those of CERCLA (Stoll, 1990). For example, the 1984 
amendments expanded corrective actions to cover all hazardous waste 
releases, regardless of when the waste was disposed. Like CERCLA, 
RCRA’s corrective action also empowers the EPA to require owners and 
operators to remediate contaminated sites.

While both CERCLA and RCRA impose cleanup costs on landown-
ers, an important distinction is that CERCLA has a broader jurisdic-
tion (Stoll, 1990).2 Specifically, CERCLA governs the cleanup of any 
site contaminated by hazardous substances, whereas RCRA’s correc-
tive actions affect mainly facilities that treat, store, dispose of haz-
ardous waste, commonly known as TSDFs (Bromm, 1998; Eisen, 1996; 
Burnham-Howard, 2004).3 The importance of this distinction becomes 
apparent in Section 2.3, where I describe how it is used to construct 
the treated and control groups.

When CERCLA and RCRA overlap, they often serve as substitutes, 
as cleanups under either statute typically result in similar outcomes. 
The EPA generally prefers to use RCRA when a facility is subject to 
both statutes (EPA, 2011).4 If, during the assessment of a potentially 
contaminated site, the EPA determines that RCRA corrective action can 
be used to enforce cleanup, it would generally not add the site to the 
NPL list (Fig.  A.1).

The cleanup liability imposed by these statutes is significant. The 
average  cleanup  cost  for  a  contaminated  site  is  approximately  $43 
million (Greenstone and Gallagher, 2008), with costs for larger, more 
complex sites reaching billions of dollars (Blair, 2011; Ohlrogge, 2024). 
Cleanup costs are also difficult to estimate (Eisen, 1996). On average, 
the evaluation phase alone takes about a decade, and the cleanup itself 
requires an additional ten years (GAO, 1997). Furthermore, cleanup 
liability is joint and several, meaning that any single party can be 
held responsible for the entire cleanup cost, regardless of their specific 
contribution to the contamination.

Because purchasers assume ownership after acquiring land, they 
face  substantial  and  uncertain  remedial  costs  (Witkin,  2004).  As  a 
result,  environmental  liability  imposed  by  the  two  statutes  signifi-
cantly hinders the transaction of industrial property (Bromm, 1998; 
Burnham-Howard, 2004). This barrier has led to the abandonment of 
many ‘‘potentially’’ contaminated industrial sites, commonly referred 
to as brownfields (Higgins, 2001; Bromm, 1998; Weissman and Sowin-
ski Jr, 2015). According to the EPA, there are currently approximately 
450,000 brownfields in the United States.

2.2. Small business liability relief and Brownfields Revitalization Act

To address the brownfields problem, Congress enacted the Small 
Business Liability Relief and Brownfields Revitalization Act (Brown-
fields Act hereafter) in 2002, which introduced two significant changes 
to purchaser liability.

First, the Brownfields Act clarified the ‘‘innocent landowner’’(ILO) 
defense,  an  existing  liability  defense  that  absolves  purchasers  from 
cleanup liability if they had no reason to know about past contamina-
tion. To successfully invoke this defense, a purchaser must demonstrate 
that she did not discover any contamination during due diligence prior 
to the purchase. However, before the Brownfields Act, the effectiveness 
of this defense was undermined by uncertainty about the due diligence 
requirements (Beless, 1997). The Act resolved this by establishing clear 
standards for environmental assessment (Meltz, 2002).

Second, the reform introduced the new ‘‘bona fide prospective pur-
chaser  (BFPP)’’  defense.  Unlike  the  ILO  defense,  the  BFPP  defense 
protects the purchaser from cleanup liability if they are aware of the 
release  or  potential  release  of  a  hazardous  substance  on  the  prop-
erty (Meltz, 2002). This provision plays a crucial role in facilitating 
property transactions by reducing the environmental risks associated 
with  acquiring  contaminated  or  potentially  contaminated  land.  To 
qualify for the BFPP defense, the transaction must have occurred after 
January  11th,  2002,  and  purchasers  must  comply  with  the  criteria 
outlined in the statute, including conducting a pre-acquisition environ-
mental assessment and appropriately managing hazardous substances 
after the purchase. Internet Appendix A describes these requirements 
in more detail.

In addition to providing liability protection for potential purchasers, 
the Act also offers liability relief to small businesses and introduces 
grants to assess and clean up brownfields (Collins, 2002). However, 
these provisions are unlikely to affect the interpretation of my results.

2.3. Measuring exposure to the Brownfields Act

This section outlines the construction of treated and control groups. 
I begin with my primary treatment measure, which classifies plants 
based on their exposure to RCRA regulation. I also develop three al-
ternative treatment measures designed to address potential weaknesses 
of the primary measure.

2.3.1. Main exposure measures

A key aspect of the Brownfields Act is that it offers liability relief 
from CERCLA to purchasers but does not extend these protections to 
RCRA  liability  (EPA,  2022).5  Consequently,  plants  subject  solely  to 
CERCLA  are  more  significantly  impacted,  as  their  purchasers  bene-
fit from liability protections without concern for RCRA liabilities. In 
contrast, plants subject to both CERCLA and RCRA serve as a natural 
comparison group, as RCRA liabilities continue to pose barriers to land 
acquisition even after the reform (Burnham-Howard, 2004).

I  exploit  this  statutory  distinction  to  construct  the  treated  and 
control  groups.  Since  CERCLA  cleanups  apply  to  any  contaminated 
plants, while RCRA’s corrective actions primarily target plants involved 
in the treatment, storage, or disposal of hazardous waste (TSDFs), I 
classify the treated and control groups based on whether plants qualify 
as TSDFs using the EPA’s RCRAinfo database. The specific steps are 
outlined below, with additional details provided in Internet Appendix 
B.

Given that TSDFs must demonstrate their ability to safely manage 
hazardous waste by obtaining RCRA operating permits, I first identify 
TSDFs using the RCRA permits database (PM_EVENT). Next, I use the 
handler dataset (HD_HANDLER), which includes a flag for plants that 
handle hazardous waste, to identify additional TSDFs. Lastly, I use the 
Biennial Hazardous Waste Report, which has been available since 2001 
and requires plants generating large volumes of hazardous waste to 
report the quantities treated, disposed of, or recycled on site. I classify 
plants as TSDFs if they report handling hazardous waste on site.6 Since 
TSDF status may be endogenous (i.e., influenced by the reform), I use 
pre-reform information, including the 2001 Biennial Report and permit 
events that occurred before 2002, to classify plants into treated and 
control groups.

After identifying the TSDFs, I match this information with plants in 
my primary dataset. Treated plants are those not classified as TSDFs 
(i.e., plants likely regulated by CERCLA only), while the control group 
consists of the remaining plants (i.e., plants more likely regulated by 
CERCLA and RCRA). To measure treatment at the firm level, I classify 
a firm as treated if the majority of its plants were treated one year 
before the Brownfields Act.

Fig.  1 plots the location of non-TSDFs (Treated) and TSDFs (Control) 
plants. The figure demonstrates that both types of plants are similarly 
distributed nationwide. Given the substantial geographic variation in 
environmental regulations, this suggests that the treated and control 
groups are likely subject to comparable regulatory environments.

2.3.2. Alternative exposure measures

To address these concerns, I implement three alternative treatment 
intensity measures. The first method leverages variations in environ-
mental liability across states. In addition to CERCLA, which is admin-
istered by the federal government, all states impose their own environ-
mental liabilities on purchasers (Sigman, 2010). Similar to RCRA, these 
state-level liabilities continue to deter property transactions even after 
the Brownfields Act (Burnham-Howard, 2004). Consequently, plants 
in states with weaker environmental liability regimes are more signif-
icantly impacted by the Brownfields Act, as purchasers benefit from 
reduced CERCLA liability while facing lower risks from state-imposed 
environmental obligations.

I measure the stringency of state-level environmental liability based 
on whether states adopt joint and several liability regimes, as deter-
mined by a 2001 survey conducted by the Environmental Law Institute. 
Under joint and several liability, any party can be held responsible 
for the entire cleanup cost, regardless of their specific contribution to 
the contamination. Fig.  2 illustrates the states with and without joint 
and several liability. This approach is grounded in previous research 
that  indicates  that  such  regimes  reduce  property  values  and  deter 
transactions (Sigman, 2010).

Second,  I  implement  an  industry-level  treatment  intensity  mea-
sure, classifying a firm as treated if it operates in an industry with 
a  significant  number  of  treated  plants.  This  method  permits  treat-
ment designation even when plants are not explicitly categorized as 
non-TSDFs.

The final approach measures exposure based on the thickness of 
the  local  real  estate  market.  The  idea  is  that  reducing  purchasers’ 
liability would have a more substantial effect on plants with more 
potential buyers. Given that firms typically sell property to neighboring 
industry peers (Bernstein et al., 2019), I define a plant as affected if 
a large number of its industry peers (top decile) are located in the 
same county. Fig.  3 highlights counties with thick real estate market for 
plants operating in the most common industries in my sample: Chem-
ical Manufacturing (Pane A), Fabricated Metal Product Manufacturing 
(Panel B), and Transportation Equipment Manufacturing (Panel C).

The figure reveals some overlap between the industries but also

3. Economic predictions

The Brownfields Act’s effect on emissions is theoretically ambigu-
ous. On one hand, exempting purchasers from cleanup liability may 
reduce emissions by increasing landowners’ net worth.  In the U.S., 
limited  liability  protections  allow  firms  to  discharge  environmental 
and  other  debts  through  bankruptcy.  These  protections  distort  the 
behavior of firms in hazardous industries, incentivizing them to take 
excessive  environmental  and  public  health  risks,  knowing  that  lia-
bilities  can  be  avoided  in  the  event  of  insolvency  (Shavell,  1986; 
Boomhower, 2019). By reducing purchaser liability, the reform miti-
gates such risk-shifting incentives by enhancing landowners’ net worth. 
Several economic forces explain how a reduction in purchaser liability 
positively impacts net worth:

First,  reducing  purchaser  liability  increases  the  demand  for  in-
dustrial  land  by  lowering  the  perceived  environmental  risks  asso-
ciated  with  its  acquisition.  This  heightened  demand  raises  real  es-
tate prices (Sigman, 2010; Howland, 2000; McGrath, 2000), directly 
boosting the net worth of current landowners.

Second, greater demand improves the liquidity of industrial land, en-
abling firms to allocate resources more efficiently. In thin asset markets, 
firms may be forced to retain unproductive assets or miss opportunities 
to  acquire  more  productive  ones.  Improved  liquidity  facilitates  the 
efficient reallocation of assets across firms, enhancing productivity and 
profitability (Gavazza, 2011; Bernstein et al., 2019; Shleifer and Vishny, 
1992; Hsieh and Klenow, 2009). Additionally, enhanced liquidity in-
creases the reversibility of investments in industrial land. Knowing that 
land can be redeployed in the future, firms are more inclined to invest 

5 

in production equipment ex-ante, thereby increasing land utilization 
rates and firm value.

Finally, reducing purchaser liability increases the liquidation value 
of land, enabling firms to pledge assets with higher collateral value. 
This reduces lenders’ risk in the event of borrower default, thereby 
lowering the cost of debt and enhancing firm value (Williamson, 1988; 
Shleifer and Vishny, 1992; Benmelech et al., 2005; Cvijanović, 2014; 
Benmelech and Bergman, 2009).

The reform could also reduce emissions through a liability-shifting 
mechanism:  as  purchaser  liability  decreases,  a  greater  share  of  the 
cleanup  burden  shifts  to  current  landowners,  incentivizing  them  to 
reduce pollution. Unlike the net worth channel, this mechanism lowers 
current landowners’ net worth, as increased environmental risk leads 
to a higher cost of capital and hinders landowners’ ability to obtain 
financing.

Conversely, the reform may increase emissions by enabling treated 
plants to be sold more easily, even when contaminated. This could 
encourage current landowners to pollute more, knowing the cleanup 
burden can be shifted to future purchasers exempt from liability.

4.2. Summary statistics

Table  1 presents the descriptive statistics for the dataset. Continuous 
variables are winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles to mitigate the 
influence of extreme observations. The main sample comprises 279,284 
plant-chemical observations, covering 498 unique chemicals and 9,970 
unique plants owned by 1,012 firms. Of these, 7,592 plants and 867 
firms belong to the treated group, defined using the TSDF status of 
plants. The average plant emits approximately 35.65 thousand pounds 
of toxic pollutants per chemical annually. Within the sample, 2.6% 
of plants report investments in abatement efforts aimed at prevent-
ing spills and leaks. Additionally, 5.7% of plants have implemented 
abatement efforts related to good operating practices, and 3.3% have 
modified their processes to reduce emissions. The average production 
ratio is 0.96.

The typical firm has a Z-score of 3.2, a Tobin’s Q of 1.6, a leverage 
ratio of 0.32, and total assets amounting to $6.1 billion. Approximately 
16.6% of firms report at least one divestiture annually. The average 
plant employs 471 people, with a 1.4% probability of closure and a 
0.9% probability of relocation. On average, 0.8% of plants sell their 
real estate through closure and relocation each year. 

4.3. Balancing test

Table   2  compares  pre-Brownfields  Act  characteristics  between
treated and control firms. Panel A reports results using my primary 
treatment measure (TSDF status). The findings show that treated and 
control firms exhibit no significant differences in emissions, produc-
tion,  Z-score,  Tobin’s  Q,  leverage,  or  total  assets.  Both  groups  also 
demonstrate similar propensities to invest in spill and leak prevention 
technologies and to modify production processes to reduce pollution. 
However,  treated  firms  display  higher  levels  of  operational-related 
abatement  activities,  potentially  due  to  their  marginally  higher  net 
worth, which may incentivize greater pollution reduction efforts. While 
observing similar characteristics between treated and control groups 
before the reform are not a necessary condition for identification in 
a difference-in-differences framework, these similarities enhance the 
credibility of a causal interpretation of the results.

The remaining panels repeat this analysis for alternative treatment 
measures. Panel B defines treatment based on whether firms have a

The results indicate that, while treated and control groups show no

In Panel C, firms are classified into treated and control groups based 
on  the  thickness  of  the  real  estate  markets  in  which  they  operate.

firms emitting fewer toxic chemicals than control firms. This difference 
may be attributed to the characteristics of thick real estate markets, 
which are often more populous. Higher population densities attract 
greater regulatory scrutiny, likely exposing treated firms to increased 
environmental oversight, thereby reducing toxic emissions.

Combined, these results demonstrate the advantage of using TSDF 
status  to  classify  treated  and  control  groups  compared  to  location-
based measures. Environmental regulations vary significantly across 
locations, leading to substantial differences in environmental activity. 
In contrast, TSDFs and non-TSDFs are more evenly distributed across 
the United States, resulting in a more homogeneous classification of 
treated and control groups.

To assess the impact of purchaser liability on toxic emissions, I

at both the firm and plant levels. 𝑇 𝑟𝑒𝑎𝑡𝑒𝑑𝑖 is a binary indicator set

exhibit minimal or no RCRA cleanups. The results, reported in Panel B

control plants exhibit similar numbers of CERCLA cleanups, control 
plants incur substantially more RCRA cleanups. The average control 
plant incurred 0.641 RCRA cleanups, whereas treated plants experi-
enced only 0.045. This difference is statistically significant at the 1% 
level. The near-zero exposure to RCRA among treated plants supports 
the  validity  of  my  treated  and  control  group  classification  in  two 
ways.  First,  it  suggests  that  the  redeployment  of  treated  plants  is 
unlikely to be hindered by RCRA liability. Second, it indicates that the 
misclassification of TSDFs as non-TSDFs is minimal.

Finally, the results also indicate that approximately two-thirds of 
the RCRA cleanups at treated plants stem from the EPA invoking emer-
gency cleanup authority (Section 7003) or addressing contamination 
caused by neighboring TSDFs (Section 3008(h)), consistent with the 
conditions under which RCRA applies to non-TSDFs.

6.3. Additional tests

In this section, I perform additional tests to address potential con-
cerns with my main findings. A plausible concern is that the results 
might be driven by a secular decline in emissions due to technolog-
ical progress or increased regulation, which could vary in intensity 
for larger or smaller emitters. If the treated and control group have 
different pre-reform emissions, the observed decline in emissions after 

2002 could reflect this broader trend rather than the impact of the 
Brownfields Act. While the no-pre-trend result in Fig.  4 suggests that 
this is unlikely the case, I further address this issue by controlling 
for changes in pollution trends based on pre-reform emission levels 
by adding Toxic Emission𝑝𝑟𝑒×Post to the regressions in Table  5. Toxic 
Emission𝑝𝑟𝑒 represents the average emissions by plant 𝑝 for chemical 
𝑐  before  2002.  The  results  reported  in  Table  O.A.1  show  that  the 
main findings remain robust under this specification. Importantly, the 
point estimates for Treated×Post remain largely unchanged, indicating 
that differential pollution trends among high- and low-emitters do not 
explain the results.

Another concern is that my main findings could be driven by con-
temporaneous industry shocks that differentially impact emissions of 
treated and control firms. To evaluate this possibility, I conduct placebo 
tests by randomly assigning treatment status across plants with parent 
firms in the same industry. For each iteration, I re-estimate the baseline 
specification and plot the distribution of the resulting coefficients in 
Fig.  5. If my main findings were driven by contemporaneous industry 
shocks, these placebo tests would be expected to produce estimates 
similar to those in my main specification (−0.073). However, the results 
suggest otherwise: the placebo point estimates are centered around 
zero (mean = −0.003), and 196 out of 200 estimates are to the right 
of the coefficient in my main specification (red line). These findings 
indicate that contemporaneous industry shocks are unlikely to explain 
my results.

7. The channel

Previous  results  suggest  that  the  reform  reduces  emissions  and 
increases abatement efforts, particularly among treated firms. As dis-
cussed in Section 6.1, this outcome is more consistent with the net 
worth channel than with the liability-shifting mechanism. An increase 
in  net  worth  affects  emissions  across  all  plants,  while  the  shift  in 
cleanup responsibility applies only to treated plants. In this section, I 
conduct additional tests to further disentangle the mechanisms under-
lying my main findings.

7.2. Cross-sectional heterogeneity

Next, I investigate whether the decrease in emissions varies based 
on the extent to which firms internalize the cost of pollution. Firms 
in  poor  financial  health  may  not  internalize  the  full  cost  of  pol-
lution  because  they  can  discharge  environmental  liabilities  through 
bankruptcy (Akey and Appel, 2021; Bellon and Boualam, 2024; Ohlrogge, 
2023). Similarly, smaller companies tend to engage in riskier environ-
mental practices because they do not internalize cleanup costs that 
exceed the value of their assets (Shavell, 1986; Boomhower, 2019). 
I refer to these firms as having strong pollution-shifting incentives. 
According to the net worth mechanism, firms with strong pollution-
shifting incentives are expected to reduce emissions the most, as the 
reform  alleviates  these  incentives.  In  contrast,  the  liability-shifting 
mechanism should have minimal effects on such firms, as they can 
discharge the redirected purchaser liability through bankruptcy.

To test these opposing predictions, I segment the sample based on 
measures of pollution-shifting incentives and re-estimate the baseline 
specification. Additionally, I employ a triple-differences framework as 
an alternative approach:

7.3. Economic activity

In my next set of analyses, I examine the effects of the reform 
on economic activity. Real property often serves as collateral for debt 
financing, and an increase in real estate prices can alleviate financing 
constraints, thereby stimulating economic activity (Ersahin and Irani, 
2020; Chaney et al., 2012). Additionally, improved liquidity in the 
land market can enhance productivity by facilitating a more efficient 
allocation of resources (Gavazza, 2011; Hsieh and Klenow, 2009). Since 

the net worth channel is driven by an increase in real estate value 
and liquidity, observing a rise in economic activity would lend support 
to this channel. Conversely, if the observed reduction in emissions is 
accompanied by a decline in economic activity, this would indicate the 
liability-shifting channel drives my main findings. Under the liability-
shifting mechanism, the transfer of purchaser liability to landowners 
raises their liability risk, which could increase their cost of capital and 
reduce economic activity.

To  assess  this,  I  use  two  measures  of  production  from  the  EPA 
to measure economic activity: the production ratio and the (log of) 
normalized production. Columns (1)–(6) of Table  10 reports the results. 
The outcome variable in columns (1)–(3) is the production ratio. The 
estimates show that the production ratio of treated firms increased by 
2.6% to 3.6% relative to control firms after the reform. Columns (4)–(6) 
present  the  results  for  normalized  production.  The  point  estimates 
are similar to those in the first three columns but only statistically 
significant in one of the specifications.

I also use employment data from NETS to measure economic activ-
ity. For this analysis, I replace plant×chemical with plant fixed effects 
and chemical ×year fixed effects with year fixed effects because the 
analysis is carried out at the plant level. The outcome variable is the log 
of plant employment. The findings are reported in columns (7)–(9). The

7.4. Economic magnitudes

In my final set of tests, I assess whether the increase in net worth 
is substantial enough to account for the observed decline in emissions. 
Specifically, the estimated elasticity between emissions and the Altman 
Z score in Fig.  6, implies that a one-unit increase in the Z score is 
associated  with  a  17%  decrease  in  emissions.  Given  the  estimated 
increase in the Z-score post-reform ranges from 0.433 and 0.514, this 
implies a 7.4% to 8.7% decrease in toxic emissions.19 This range closely 
matches the estimated 7% reduction in emissions.

However,  this  back-of-the-envelope  calculation  has  some  limita-
tions. For instance, the relationship between the Z-score and emissions 
may not be linear; as companies approach bankruptcy, their emissions 
might  increase  exponentially.  Furthermore,  Ohlrogge  (2023)  shows 
that emissions drop significantly after companies file for bankruptcy.20 
Keeping  these  caveats  in  mind,  the  calculations  provide  suggestive 
evidence that the increase in net worth is sufficient to explain the 
estimated decline in emissions among treated firms.

Overall, the results in this section indicate that the reform reduced 
emissions when the net worth effect was expected to be most significant 
and increased the net worth and economic activity of affected firms. 
These findings provide evidence that the net worth channel is the key 
mechanism driving the observed reduction in emissions.

8. Conclusion

What are the ex-ante consequences of promoting the divestment of 
polluting assets? I address this question by studying the passage of the 
Brownfields Act, a major reform designed to increase the liquidity of 
industrial land by reducing purchaser liability. Leveraging the unique 
aspects of the Brownfields Act, I find that reducing purchaser liability 
leads to a reduction in toxic emissions by the current landowners. This

I conduct several tests to explore the underlying mechanisms. First, I 
demonstrate that the reform positively affects net worth. Second, cross-
sectional analyses reveal that the reduction in emissions is more pro-
nounced among firms with strong pollution-shifting incentives. Third, 
I find evidence that the reform positively impacts productivity and 
employment. Together, these results suggest that an increase in net 
worth drives the decline in emissions.

Overall, my findings suggest that policies promoting divestitures 
by increasing the demand for brown assets can reduce emissions from 
current landowners by enhancing their net worth. To my knowledge, 
this study provides the first empirical evidence on the ex-ante effects 
of divestiture policies.

It is important to acknowledge that my analysis does not allow for 
definitive welfare conclusions regarding purchaser liability. A potential 
drawback of reducing purchaser liability is that it may limit the funds 
available to the government for cleaning up contaminated sites. I leave 
it to future research to quantify this potential cost.
