Liquidity picking and fund performance

A R T I C L E  I N F O

A B S T R A C T

Using global mutual fund and American Depositary Receipt (ADR) data, we test if funds strategically trade cross- 
listed firms’ equity shares in the most liquid trading location. We find that especially funds that score high on 
traditional skill measures exhibit a liquidity-based trading venue preference. We identify an informed trading 
motive  as  the  most  likely  driver  for  such  behaviour  rather  than  preference  based  on  geographic,  economic, 
cultural, or governance motives. Thus, liquidity picking is associated with fund outperformance and stock se-
lection ability that is not limited to only cross-listed firms. Our tests directly support theories of informed trading 
in a multi-market setting.

1. Introduction

The recent decades have witnessed two parallel processes related to 
the ongoing financial and economic globalization. The first one is the 
expansion  of  the  mutual  fund  industry  in  many  countries  around  the 
world.  The  second  is  the  proliferation  of  stock  cross-listings  in  many 
countries, especially in the United States.1 Motivated by these two de-
velopments, we raise the following questions. Given that investing in the 
same equity is possible in the firm’s home market or its chosen foreign 
listing location, do funds (skilled or unskilled) show any location pref-
erence for holding or trading the equity of a cross-listed firm? Given the 

near-perfect  return  correlation  of  cross-listed  stocks  with  their  home 
market counterparts (Gagnon and Karolyi, 2010), what are the reasons 
for such a location preference, if it exists?

There  are  many  potential  benefits  of  cross-listings  for  firms,  espe-
cially those placed in the United States.2 However, most of these bene-
fits, including cost of capital reduction, visibility enhancement, equity 
offerings,  foreign  acquisitions, information  generation, and  disclosure 
stringency are firm-specific and not specific to the trading location: they 
may persuade investors to start investing in the equity of a cross-listed 
firm in general but have little relevance to investors’  location choices 
for trading a firm’s equity in its domestic market or on a US exchange.

For instance, since the returns of cross-listed stocks are nearly identical 
across  trading  venues  by  the  no-arbitrage  principle,  risk-based  expla-
nations cannot justify a specific location preference. Even on the matter 
of improved governance or investor protection when holding an ADR 
versus the firm’s equity in the home market, Licht et al. (2018) point out 
that in 2010 the US Supreme Court decided that the US securities laws 
would not apply to investment activities outside the United States, thus, 
effectively  excluding  US  traded  cross-listed  firms  from  US  antifraud 
enforcement.3 We  note  that,  in  spite  of  lower  potential  liabilities  of 
US-listed foreign firms, their bonding preference for the US market may 
still prevail among some funds (Gagnon and Karolyi, 2018).

Of the remaining cross-listing placement considerations, some could 
still conceivably induce a location preference for funds. Those include a 
preference  based  on  proximity  or  familiarity  differences  between  the 
fund and firm’s trading venues (e.g., geographic, economic, and cultural 
proximities, as in Sarkissian and Schill (2004)4 and a preference based 
on security-level liquidity differences between trading venues.

In this paper, we focus on the aforesaid subset of cross-listing moti-
vations  and  test  the  hypothesis  that  when  investing  in  a  cross-listed 
stock, funds display a location preference rooted in the desire to trade 
the  firm’s  equity  in  its  most  liquid  trading  venue.  Such  a  preference 
should be particularly pronounced for skilled funds that seek to better 
exploit  their  informational  advantages  without  significant  effect  on 
stock prices. This is the central hypothesis of our paper.

Our hypothesis is motivated by theory and studies that examine the 
distribution of trading volume of cross-listed stocks. A central prediction 
common to theories of multi-market trading is liquidity concentration in 
one trading venue as especially informed (or “skilled”) investors stra-
tegically  trade  in  the  location  where  the  market  is  the  “thickest”  to 
exploit  their  information  with  minimal  price  impact  (Admati  and 
Pfleiderer,  1988);  (Chowdhry  and  Nanda,  1991).5 Also,  empirical 
studies  attest  to  the  large  heterogeneity  and  dynamics  in  the  foreign 
share  of  trading  volume  of  cross-listed  stocks  (Baruch  et  al.,  2007); 
(Halling et al., 2008).

We  test  our  hypothesis  using  data  on  mutual  funds  with  a  global 
investment mandate from 35 countries (or territories) and firms from 40 
countries (or territories) with their share listings as American Depositary 
Receipts (ADRs) on regular US exchanges.6 This is a uniquely powerful 
setting  to  test  our  main  theoretical  prediction  and  contrast  it  against 
alternatives that predict a location preference based on differences in 
various  proximity  metrics  or  investor  protection.  First,  focussing  on 
funds  with  global  investment  mandates  rules  out  that  any  location 
preferences  are  implicitly  driven  by  geographic  mandate  or  style  re-
strictions. Second, for those funds, cross-listed firms constitute over 20% 
of  their  average  portfolio  allocation,  allowing  us  to  measure  and 
examine  liquidity  preferences  for  a  meaningful  part  of  funds’  overall 
investment decisions. Third, we show that funds do change the trading 

venue for cross-listed stocks over time. Fig. 1 shows the proportion of 
global funds per year that change the location of at least one stock in 
their  portfolio  between  stock’s  domestic  trading  venue  and  the  US 
market. The proportion is quite sizable ranging from 31% in 2001 to 
about 12% in 2021. Fourth, a large literature on fund performance has 
identified a variety of proxies that allow us to identify “skilled”  funds 
and contrast their investment behaviour when investing in cross-listed 
firms against the behaviour of “unskilled” funds. Fifth, our alternative 
motives for the selection of a specific trading venue (geographic, eco-
nomic,  and  cultural  proximities  and  investor  protection)  are  already 
known to affect fund portfolio choice.

To  measure  the  fund  managers’  preference  for  security-level 
liquidity, i.e., trading of a given firm’s stock in its more liquid venue, 
we  first  construct  a  security  liquidity  picking  (SLP)  measure  as  the 
average fraction of the more liquid security (stock or ADR) over total 
equity  holdings  (stock  and  ADR)  across  all  cross-listed  stocks  in  the 
portfolio of the fund.7 SLP is computed over cross-listed stocks in the 
United States that are held in funds’ portfolios, since we can determine 
the  trading  locations  of  those  stocks  based  on  differences  in  security 
identifiers.8 The SLP measure is the key to our research question: We are 
interested in, not whether a fund invests in cross-listed stocks per se, but, 
conditional  on  investing,  in  where  the  fund  trades  the  stock.9 We 
conjecture that funds that consistently select to trade in the most liquid 
venue exhibit strategic behaviour that theory associates with informed 
(or “skilled”) investing.

Our empirical analysis is composed of several parts. We start with 
examining the determinants of a fund’s perfect SLP score to understand 
what type of funds exclusively trade in the most liquid trading venue. We 
sort  funds  based  on  various  established  skill  metrics  including  active 
share (Cremers and Petajisto, 2009), the industry concentration index 
(“ICI”, Kacperczyk et al., 2005), and others and find that the fraction of 
funds with perfect SLP is the highest among the quintile of “most skilled” 
funds.  For  most  measures,  the  representation  of  perfect  SLP  funds  is 
monotonically increasing in the skill measure and two- to three-times 
higher  in  the  top  versus  the  bottom  quintile.  Multivariate  tests  that 
control for many fund characteristics confirm this observation: measures 
of  skill  are  consistently  positively  correlated  with  liquidity  picking, 
suggesting that more skilled funds are particularly careful in selecting 
their trading location. Empirically, our estimated effects indicate that 
for every 1.0% increase in the various measures of skill, the odds of a 
fund to exclusively trade in the most liquid trading venue increases by 
2.3% to 4.3%. We find that this effect is similar for both US and non-US 
managed funds.

indeed  reflecting  a  preference  for  trading  in  the  most  liquid  trading

However, when conditioning on various “skill”  measures, liquidity 
proves  the  most important  determinant for  skilled  funds: the effect of 
liquidity  on  trading  location  choice  is  up  to  50%  stronger  for  skilled

effect for skilled funds for any other motive that could induce a trading 
venue preference.

Next, we examine the importance of liquidity picking for gross and 
risk-adjusted fund returns. For instance, based on univariate tests, we 
find that funds with perfect SLP (SLP = 1) outperform those with lower 
SLP (SLP < 1) by 85.2 bps per year in gross returns and between 40.8 bps 
and  82.8 bps  per  year  in  risk-adjusted  returns  depending  on  the  per-
formance  evaluation  model.  Multivariate  panel  regressions  with  risk- 
adjusted returns and control variables support this evidence. Interest-
ingly, the positive effect of SLP on fund performance survives controlling 
for established skill measures, indicating that liquidity picking is infor-
mative about fund manager skill beyond what is captured by existing 
proxies. A large battery of robustness tests based on multiple interna-
tional factor models, US and non-US fund sub-samples, and net of fee 
fund returns confirm our finding. More importantly, we repeat our tests 
in  the  presence  of  alternative  trading  venue  preferences  (geographic, 
economic, and cultural proximities as well as investor protection). Even 
in the presence of these additional considerations, we find strong evi-
dence of the relation between consistent security liquidity picking and 
fund performance. Among the alternatives, only geographic proximity 
shows some positive association with high returns among non-US funds.
To  sharpen  identification,  we  conduct  a  series  of  event  studies 
around instances when the more liquid trading location of a cross-listed 
stock switches from the home market to the United States or vice versa. 

While  we  identify  283  such  instances  attributable  to  98  cross-listed 
firms, we find that for most of those cases, the distribution of liquidity 
across the two venues is far more homogenous than in the rest of the 
sample  (i.e.,  while  the  ranking  of  the  most  liquid  venue does  switch, 
liquidity tends to remain fairly equally shared between both venues). 
Nevertheless, we still find that a small fraction of funds (2.0%) make an 
active  decision  to  trade  in  the  most  liquid  venue  consistent  with  our 
hypothesis – their holdings of the cross-listed firms’ equity are partially 
or fully exchanged from the now less-liquid security to the now more 
liquid  security  trading  in  the  alternative  venue.  This  fraction  is  more 
than  three  times  larger  compared  to  the  same  statistic  computed  for 
other  cross-listed  stocks  whose  most  liquid  trading  venue  does  not 
change.  Consistent  with  the  notion  that  liquidity  chasing  funds  are 
skilled, we observe that funds that score above the median in standard 
skill metrics are more likely to engage in this practice than those with 
below median values. Then expectedly, we also find that funds that do 
follow  stock  liquidity  switches  register  positive  risk-adjusted  out-
performance between 59 bps to 123 bps the following year, supporting 
the conjecture that some funds make an active decision to trade in the 
most liquid venue and consistent with the central theoretical prediction 
that informed traders strategically select to trade a security in its most 
liquid venue to best capitalize on their information (Admati and Pflei-
derer, 1988; Chowdhry and Nanda, 1991).

In the final part, we conduct additional analysis using holdings-based 
fund  returns.  First,  we  show  that  liquidity  picking  is  associated  with 
stock selection ability (but not market timing ability) of fund managers 
and  is  not  a  sole  effort  to  minimize  transaction  costs.  Furthermore, 
perfect SLP funds also outperform in their holdings of non-cross-listed as 
well as foreign stocks, suggesting that such funds are “skilled” overall, 
not only specialized in trading cross-listed firms, and that our results are 
not  driven  by  a  preference  for  investing  in  geographically  proximate 
firms (i.e.,  “home bias”). Second,  we find  a stronger  positive  relation 
between SLP and fund returns for large funds as well as for funds with a 
large share of small stocks in their portfolios. This result is consistent 
with the idea that selecting the most liquid trading venue is particularly 
useful  for  skilled  funds  that  are  more  likely  to  suffer  from  capacity 
constraints in their trading strategies.

We  make  two  broad  contributions  to  the  literature.  First,  we  link 
asset management research with the literature on cross-listed securities,

taking  advantage  of  the  uniqueness  of  a  global  environment,  where 
money  managers  around  the  world  can  select  different  markets  for 
trading the same firm’s shares. This setting allows us to examine in detail 
the  preferences  of  fund  managers  to  invest  in  the  firm’s  equity  in  its 
domestic stock market or in the United States through ADRs.10 In doing 
so, we contribute empirically to the literature on informed trading in 
multi-market settings. While there are many papers in this field, they 
mainly focus on aggregate, market-level tests linking, for example, the 
market  for  a  stock  to  trading  in  its  derivatives  and  vice  versa  (e.g., 
Manaster and Rendleman, 1982; Easley et al., 1998; Chakravarty et al., 
2004; Augustin et al., 2019). Few (if any) studies link strategic venue 
selection based on liquidity differences for the same security to invest-
ment performance at the investor or portfolio levels. Indeed, contrary to 
other related securities such as stocks and options that differ in multiple 
aspects including liquidity but also embedded leverage or the market 
environment in which they are traded, an ADR is practically identical to 
its underlying stock in all aspects but its liquidity. This greatly attenu-
ates concerns about confounding factors that could lead to a location 
preference. The unique setting of global mutual funds selecting to trade 
the equity or the ADR of a cross-listed firm therefore allows us to directly 
test a key prediction of the theoretical literature: informed traders will 
strategically select the most liquid market for a security to best capitalize 
on their information.

Second, we contribute to the literature that examines the investment 
behaviour  of  international  mutual  funds  particularly  that  associated 
with informed trading. Such studies frequently identify specific invest-
ment biases and relate those to observed portfolio decisions and fund 
performance.11 Contrary to those studies, we are not interested in any 
portfolio biases or which funds over- or under-weight particular firms or 
markets.  Instead,  we  focus  on  the  choice  of  trading  venue  based  on 
liquidity characteristics conditional on the fund investing in the equity 
of the cross-listed firm. That is, we do not seek to identify any possible 
source of information advantage but instead focus on testing a central 
prediction of theories of informed trading in a multi-market setting.

2. Hypothesis development

Our hypothesis is based on theories of informed trading in a multi- 
market  setting  (e.g.,  Admati  and  Pfleiderer,  1988;  Chowdhry  and 
Nanda,  1991;  Baruch  et  al.,  2007).  The  specific  theories  differ  in  the 
information structure that investors are endowed with, the location re-
strictions that investors face, or the drivers for emergence of a specific 
trading  venue  as  the  most  liquid  one.  However,  they  all  share  one 
insight: informed investors in particular care about trading any given 
security in the most liquid market to exploit information advantages. In 
other words, informed investors are particularly attracted to trading in 
venues where the market is “thickest”  because it allows them to capi-
talize on their information without generating large price impact.

We  highlight  that  we  do  not  seek  to  differentiate  between  these 
specific theories but focus on testing their common key prediction. In 
doing so, we are agnostic as to which trading venue emerges as the more 
liquid one for any given cross-listed firm or why occasionally the most 
liquid venue switches. Instead, we exploit our unique setting as an op-
portunity to measure if funds trade in a manner consistent with the in-
formation advantage line of reasoning, and if so, what the characteristics 
of those funds are. This is our hypothesis:

Hypothesis: Skilled funds trade in the most liquid venue to exploit in-

formation advantages.

We translate this hypothesis into testable predictions adapted spe-
cifically to our setting. The key observation is that global mutual funds 
hold  (and  trade)  both  cross-listed  and  non-cross-listed  stocks.  This  af-
fords us an opportunity to measure liquidity picking for the subset of 
their  cross-listed  firm  holdings.  Furthermore,  past  studies  identified 
various measures that allow for an ex-ante differentiation into “skilled” 
and “unskilled” funds, providing us a mapping to the “informed” versus 
“uninformed”  investors  that  feature  in  the  abovementioned  theories. 
This  allows  for  a  direct  translation  of  our  hypothesis  into  our  first 
empirical prediction:

Prediction #1: Skilled funds trade in the most liquid venue for a cross- 

listed firm’s stock.

Second,  our  hypothesis  is  cast  in  terms  of  a  liquidity  preference 
driving  the  venue  choice  of  skilled  funds.  We  recognize  alternative 
drivers that can induce a venue choice and that can be correlated with a 
liquidity preference. We identify two such alternative motives – gover-
nance and  proximity (familiarity) –  and contrast our liquidity motive 
against them. On the topic of governance, it is generally believed that 
the United States provides the best minority investors’ protection (e.g., 
Doidge et al., 2004; 2007, 2009; Gagnon and Karolyi, 2018). If so, global 
funds,  both  within  and  outside  of  the  United  States,  should  always 
choose investing in ADRs rather than the actual stock in its domestic 
market, a possibility we discuss throughout our tests.

Proximity considerations are supported by the extensive past cross- 
listing and international portfolio choice literatures. The proximity di-
mensions include geographic, economic, and  cultural proximities  and 
are  frequently  interpreted  in  the  context  of  familiarity-based  versus 
information-based investing.12 We provide explicit tests to differentiate 
our liquidity hypothesis from these alternatives. We note that the liter-
ature relates familiarity-based motives to a fund’s decision to invest (or 
not) in the firm’s equity in general leaving the choice of a trading venue 
aside. As highlighted in the introduction, our hypothesis focuses on the 
latter  preference.  Nevertheless,  if,  for  example,  geographic  proximity 
affects investors’  portfolio choice, it is  natural to expect that it could 
equally affect the funds’ trading venue selection due to their larger fa-
miliarity  with  the  “closest”  stock  exchange.  This  is  our  second 
prediction:

Prediction #2: Venue choice of skilled funds is not driven by proximity 

or governance motives.

Third, the theories also agree that, in principle, all funds – skilled or 
not – would like to trade in the most liquid venue if only to minimize 
transaction costs. However, compared to “unskilled” funds (i.e., “noise 
traders”), “skilled” funds (i.e., “informed traders”) have an extra motive 
– exploiting their information advantage to minimize the price impact of 
their  trades.  Empirically,  this  means  that  the  fund’s  propensity  to 
consistently  trade  in  the  most  liquid  market  should  reflect  its  strong 
performance in general based on various fund performance metrics, not 
only  in  the  cross-listed  portfolio  of  stocks.  Moreover,  it  should  be 
detected through those measures of fund performance that control for 
the  simple  motive  of  minimizing  transaction  costs  such  as  holdings- 
based returns. This is our third prediction.

Prediction #3: Skilled funds outperform beyond the holdings of cross- 

listed stocks.

Having laid out our empirical predictions, we discuss several related 
and important points. To start, we iterate that, since returns on ADRs 
and their underlying stocks are practically the same by the no-arbitrage 
principle  (Gagnon  and  Karolyi,  2010),  the  outperformance  of  funds 
under  our  hypothesis  does  not  result  from  liquidity  chasing  per  se. 
Instead, we argue that skilled funds care much more about trading in a 
more liquid venue, where they can exploit their informational advan-
tage without large price impact concerns on their transactions.

the  past  empirical  work  on  this  subject  (Baruch  et  al.,  2007,  Halling 
et  al.,  2008)  and  aligns  directly  with  some  other  liquidity  measures, 
particularly  that  of  Amihud  (2002).  Indeed,  since  the  returns  of 
cross-listed firms’  stocks and their ADRs are practically identical, any 
difference  in  the  Amihud  (2002) liquidity  between  the  stock  and  the 
ADR of a cross-listed firm is driven by the differences in their respective 
trading volumes.13

Next, our tests focus on the fund’s choice to trade the stock of a cross- 
listed firm on its home exchange or through its ADR on a US exchange. 
Yet,  investors  can  trade  certain  cross-listed  firms  in  more  than  two 
venues: some firms have a tertiary listing of their stock on another ex-
change,  while  others  also  have  a  Global  Depositary  Receipt  (GDR) 
program. However, since very few firms in our sample are affected by 
this and the vast majority of fund holdings is attributable to either the 
stock or the ADR, our main results are not impacted by the existence of 
alternative  trading  platforms  for  some  of  our  cross-listed  firms.  For 
clarity,  we  maintain  our  general  exposition  as  a  choice  of  trading  a 
firm’s stock on its domestic exchange or as an ADR.

Finally, we acknowledge one element where our empirical strategy 
departs from the theory: our setting does not offer us a true equivalent of 
competitive market makers between exchanges. Indeed, there are other 
differences in market structure (e.g., different investor protection rules) 
that could induce a location choice. Our tests cannot differentiate be-
tween these possibilities but are instead designed around the established 
result that the most liquid trading venue differs across firms – the home 
exchange for some, the US for others –  and, therefore, have power to 
identify  (and  reject)  our  main  hypothesis  that  is  based  on  liquidity 
differences.

3. Data and main variables

3.1. Data sources

The dataset we employ in this study merges a sample of cross-listed 
firms  with  holdings  data  for  international  mutual  funds.  We  describe 
both main data sources in turn. First, we construct a cross-listing firm 
sample from several sources and restrict it to placements on regular US 
exchanges  (i.e.,  AMEX,  NYSE,  and  NASDAQ)  and  from  countries  not 
classified as tax havens. We require that a cross-listed firm has both a 
domestic listing and an ADR, so that each fund always has a choice of 
trading a given firm in its domestic market or on a US exchange. We 
collect ADR data using the Citigroup ADR database and cross-check the 
data with foreign listing codes from CRSP and leave only those foreign 
firms that have identifiable SIC codes. We collect cross-listing informa-
tion  until  2019  because  our  fund  holding  and  return  data  ends  in 
December  2021  and  we  measure  the  trading  venue  choices  of  funds 
based on 1-year lagged holdings for cross-listed firms that have existed 
for that entire year. This procedure yields a final sample of 502 cross- 
listings (direct placements, Level II, and Level III ADRs) from 40 coun-
tries. Table A.1 in the Internet Appendix shows the distribution of cross- 
listed firms in the United States across countries over time and Table A.2 
provides descriptive statistics of these firms.14

December)  for  funds  from  the  FactSet  Ownership  database.  We  com-
plement  this  holdings  data  with  additional  fund  information  such  as 
monthly  fund  performance  and  other  fund  characteristics  from  Mor-

perfect SLP funds, funds even with relatively high but non-perfect of SLP 
do not consistently trade in the more liquid of the two markets (domestic 
or the United States) for a given firm security. Therefore, such funds do 
not  seem  to  prioritize  liquidity  consistently  across  all  decisions  when 
exploiting possible informational advantages. In the context of our hy-

traders.

4. Liquidity and venue choice

In this section, we test the first and second empirical predictions that 
skilled fund managers prefer to trade the equity of cross-listed firms in 
the  most  liquid  trading  venue rather  than make  their location  choice 
based  on  proximity  or  governance  considerations.  We  first  conduct 
univariate and multivariate tests linking perfect SLP to various known 
skill measures of fund managers as well as to other fund characteristics. 
Then we horse-race security liquidity picking with other possible mo-
tives for specific trading venue selection of cross-listed stocks.

4.2. Do skilled funds value liquidity, proximity, or governance?

We now turn to our second prediction and examine other factors that 
could induce a location preference for trading. These other candidate 
factors are our three proximity measures and the governance proxy. Our 
goal is two-fold: First, we want to establish that it is indeed liquidity 
characteristics  that  are  important  for  the  choice  of  trading  venue  as 
liquidity  could  conceivably  be  correlated  with  the  alternative  de-
terminants. Second, we want to test if liquidity is particularly important 
for skilled funds when selecting their trading venue. Our first tests in 
Tables  2  and  3 indicate  as  much  but  do  not  have  strong  power  to 
separate  the  liquidity  motive  from  alternative  ones.  Our  next  test  re-
solves this.

To achieve our goal, we expand our dataset from the fund-period to 
the fund-position-period level. In other words, instead of measuring SLP 
at  the  aggregate  fund  level,  we  examine  every  position  choice  sepa-
rately, asking if a funds’  propensity to trade the position in the cross- 
listed  firm’s  home  stock  market  or  the  US  exchange  is  related  to 
liquidity characteristics or any of the above alternatives. We estimate 
the following fractional logistics model:

5. Security liquidity picking and fund performance

In this section, we provide several tests for our third empirical pre-
diction.  That  is,  we  show  that  the  main  motive  behind  skilled  funds’

general  fund  performance.  We  first  analyse  overall  fund  performance 
based on gross and risk-adjusted returns without and with alternative 
trading  venue  preferences.  Then,  we  examine  fund  performance  and 
managerial characteristics when funds switch their trading of a given 
firm between its domestic market and the United States in response to 
changes  in  the  dominant  trading  volume  and  liquidity  venue  for  its

returns using the estimated loadings. We require at least 24 fund return 
observations. The exact definition of each factor model is provided in the 
Appendix. We employ a large variety of risk adjustments, recognizing 
that a consensus on the appropriate factor model for international funds 
has yet to emerge in the literature. Our approach is consistent with past 
studies that examine risk-adjusted performance for international funds 

that also rely on a variety of factor corrections (e.g., Chuprinin et al., 
2015; Cremers et al., 2016; Schumacher, 2018; Massa and Schumacher, 
2020; Jagannathan et al., 2022).

5.2. Security liquidity picking and alternative trading venue preferences

We now augment Model (5) by adding proxies that capture venue 
preferences based on the alternative motives introduced earlier. We run 
a horse-race between our measure and the alternative motives to com-
plement the results from Section 4.2. For each alternative, we construct 
a fund-level measure that indicates if the fund consistently selects the 
trading venue based on any of these alternatives. For example, a variable 
Geog Prox Pickingi,t(cid:0) 1 = 1 is an indicator equal to unity if the fund al-
ways holds the security that is geographically closest to the fund and 
0 otherwise and likewise for the economic or cultural proximity mea-
sures or the measure on rule of law picking. All other specifications are 
as in Table 6, Regression (4).

Table 7 presents the estimation results. We can see that the addition 
of other trading location preferences does not substantially affect our 
main result – the SLP indicator is still significant at the 1% level across 
all estimations and the magnitude of the estimate barely changes across 
regressions. For instance, the point estimate on SLP in Regression (4) is 
4.3 bps  per  month  versus  4.4 bps  per  month  for  the  same  regression 
specification in Table 6. This confirms our findings from Section 4.2 that 
liquidity  picking  is  indeed  a  measure  that  likely  captures  informed 
trading by skilled funds.

At the same time, we do see support for some alternative motiva-
tions, in particular for funds that consistently trade in the geographically 
closest venue: the effect of Geog_Prox_Picking is positive and significant 
in  both  Regression  (2)  and  the  joint  specification  of  Regression  (6), 
although  its  statistical  significance  is  largely  driven  by  non-US  funds 
(compare  Regressions  (7)  and  (8)).  This  result  is  consistent  with  past 
studies on the positive effects of proximity-based investing (e.g., Coval 
and Moskowitz, 2001; Ivkovich and Weisbenner, 2005).

Overall,  even  in  the  presence  of  alternative  location  preference 
considerations, we again find strong evidence of the relation between 
superior security liquidity picking ability and fund performance. Com-
plementary tests in Tables A.6 and A.7 further confirm this finding for 
different  performance  benchmarks  and  a  bid-ask  spread  based  SLP 
measure.  Since  fund  performance  is  based  on  the  entire  portfolio  of 
stocks  rather  than  cross-listed  firms  alone,  our  tests  provide  the  first 
substantial support of the relevance of our third prediction. More direct 
tests  require  detailed  fund  holdings  data,  which  is  the  focus  of  our 
follow-up Section 6.

identification,  we  examine  situations  when  the  more  liquid  trading

their preferred trading venue from the now less-liquid to the now more- 
liquid one, and (ii) if such changes relate to fund performance.

6. Additional tests with holding period fund returns

Our finding that the ability of fund managers to select a more liquid 
venue for the same company’s stock is positively related to funds’ gross 
and risk-adjusted returns leaves the question on the mechanism of these 
funds’  outperformance  open.  Also,  it  is  unclear  whether  this  out-
performance is solely (or largely) due to direct transaction cost mini-
mization  as  highlighted  in  Section  2 rather  than  to  trading  on 
information advantages as postulated in our third empirical prediction. 
Therefore, in this section we provide additional evidence to substantiate 
our support for the third prediction using holdings-based fund returns.
First, we examine various holding return measures to understand in 
what  dimensions  perfect  SLP  funds  outperform.  In  particular,  we 
determine  whether  such  funds  have  superior  stock  selection  and/or 
market timing ability and whether they also outperform in their hold-
ings of non-cross-listed stocks. Second, we test which funds benefit more 
from liquidity picking by relating SLP to capacity constraints that certain 
funds may face.

6.1. Liquidity picking and fund performance decomposition

To understand whether the security liquidity picking reflects stock 
selection  ability  of  fund  managers  or  simply  reveals  their  efforts  to 
minimize  transaction  costs  without  exploiting  any  firm-specific  infor-
mation, we use holdings-based returns. The test results in the previous 
section  are  based  on  the  overall  fund  returns  (before  fees)  but  after 
transaction costs. In contrast, holdings-based returns do not incorporate 
transaction  costs  and  therefore  allow  us  to  assess  if  the  overall  fund 
returns are higher because of superior stock selection (or market timing) 
ability or simply because high SLP funds are more efficient at lowering 
transaction costs.

R(AH),  is  the  semi-annual  holding  period  return  constructed  from  the 
holdings of all stocks held in the portfolio of a fund in a given period and 
the subsequent semi-annual stock return of each stock. The second and 
third measures, R(FH) and R(DH), decompose overall holding returns into 
foreign  and  domestic  stock  holding  returns,  respectively.  We  refer  to 
holdings  as  domestic  (foreign)  if  a  fund  managed  in  a  given  country 
holds a firm (its stock or ADR) domiciled in the same (different) coun-
try.25 The next two measures are R(CL) and R(Non-CL) that correspond to

6.2. Liquidity picking and capacity constraints

Finally,  we  analyse  whether  liquidity  picking  of  fund  managers  is 
particularly  beneficial  for  funds  that  likely  face  capacity  constraints. 
Indeed, the importance of proper liquidity management increases when 

16 

portfolio  managers  handle less  liquid  securities.  Moreover,  additional 
liquidity issues could arise with trading by large funds, the actions of 
which may impact security prices, thus, lowering the liquidity of their 
transactions.

Two dimensions that are likely to correlate with capacity constraints 
are funds trading in small-cap stocks that are generally less liquid and 
large  funds  that  may  be  more  concerned  about  their  trades  having  a 
price impact. Therefore, to address the issue of interest, in each period, 
we divide the full sample of funds into four groups by fund TNA and 
average stock size held in the fund’s portfolio. We create two indicator 
variables that serve as capacity constraints proxies. The first variable, 
Small_Cap, is an indicator that equals one if the average lagged stock size 
is  in  the  lowest  quintile,  and  zero  otherwise.  The  second  variable, 
Large_Fund, is an indicator that equals one if the lagged fund TNA is in 
the largest quintile, and zero otherwise. We then re-estimate Model (5) 
and augment it with additional interaction terms between the SLP = 1 
indicator and our two proxies for capacity constraints.

7. Conclusion

In this study, using data on global mutual funds from 35 countries 
and firms with ADRs on US exchanges from 40 countries, we examine 
the preference of fund managers to trade the same firm’s stock in the 
United States versus its local market in the more liquid trading venue. 
This setting allows us to test a central prediction common to theories of 
informed  trading  in  multiple  markets  (Admati  and  Pfleiderer,  1988; 
Chowdhry  and  Nanda,  1991)  namely  that,  ceteris  paribus,  informed 
investors strategically choose to trade in the most liquid trading venue to 
best  capitalize  on  their  information.  Using  the  setting  of  stocks  and 
ADRs, we are able to link strategic venue selection based on liquidity 
differences for the same security to investment performance at the fund 
portfolio level.

In our analysis, we construct and use a novel measure of managers’ 
trading  venue preference –  a firm-specific liquidity selection metric – 
security liquidity picking (SLP). We show that funds with perfect SLP, i. 
e.,  funds that always trade the stock of a given company in the more 
liquid market of the two possible are predominantly skilled funds based 
on  previously  established  measures  of  skill  and  consistent  with  the 
theoretical  prediction  that  such  behaviour  is  of  particular  value  to 
skilled investors. Consequently, we find that liquidity picking is asso-
ciated with superior gross, net, risk-adjusted, and holdings-based fund 
returns as well as in both US and non-US fund sub-samples. Importantly, 
our  results  survive  after  controlling  for  standard  fund  characteristics, 
existing manager ability metrics, as well as funds’ alternative possible 
preferences for trading their firm’s security in a specific market such as 
geographic, economic, and cultural biases or better investor protection.
We  also  examine  fund  liquidity  chasing  and  performance  around 
events  when  the  more  liquid  trading  location  of  a  cross-listed  stock 

switches from the home market to the United States or in the opposite 
direction. Skilled funds are more likely to correctly switch their holdings 
from the previously more liquid to the now more liquid venue and when 
doing so, register strong outperformance in the next year, indicating that 
these funds indeed acted to capitalize on superior information. Further 
tests reveal that funds with perfect SLP possess genuine stock selection 
skills rather than simply minimizing their transactions costs. We observe 
that such SLP is associated with superior fund returns not only for cross- 
listed  firms  but  also  for  firms  that  are  traded  only  in  their  domestic 
markets.  Finally,  consistent  with  the  common  understanding  of  more 
importance for proper liquidity management for potentially less liquid 
securities, we observe a stronger relation of perfect SLP to fund returns 
among funds with large proportion of small cap stocks in their portfolios 
and among large funds. Our study provides strong support to a group of 
theories of informed trading in a multi-market setting and our empirical 
measure  that  captures  this  behaviour  incorporates  information  about 
skill that goes beyond existing skill measures.
