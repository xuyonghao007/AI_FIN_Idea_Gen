Do negative housing shocks lead to persistent changes in household attitudes toward housing and homeown-
ership? We use the residential destruction of Germany during World War II (WWII) as a quasi-experiment 
and exploit the reasonably exogenous region-by-cohort variation in destruction exposure. We ﬁnd that WWII-
experiencing cohorts from high destruction regions are signiﬁcantly less likely to be homeowners decades later, 
controlling for regional diﬀerences and household characteristics. Underlying this eﬀect are changes in house-
hold attitudes toward homeownership that also extend to preferences for housing consumption, with little or no 
support for risk preferences, income and wealth eﬀects, or supply-side factors.

1.  Introduction

Housing is typically the largest component of household wealth. Af-
ter  reaching  69%  in  the  mid-2000s,  the  homeownership  rate  in  the 
United  States  has  experienced  a  signiﬁcant  decline  in  the  following 
decade, falling to its lowest level in half a century. One explanation 
for the steep fall in homeownership rates, which is often cited by the 
popular press and policymakers, is changes in household attitudes to-
ward homeownership fueled by negative housing experiences around 
the ﬁnancial crises and the Great Recession (Bracha and Jamison, 2012; 
Simmons, 2014; Goodman and Mayer, 2018; Heimer and Fritsch, 2020). 
While this explanation has recently received a great deal of attention, 
empirically distinguishing its eﬀect from other contemporaneous eco-

nomic  factors,  such  as  stagnant  incomes,  tight  credit  conditions,  or 
declining household wealth has proven to be diﬃcult.

In this paper, we provide novel empirical evidence on how negative 
housing experiences can shape long-term household attitudes towards 
housing and homeownership. Speciﬁcally, we use the housing destruc-
tion caused by the bombing in Germany during World War II (WWII) as 
a quasi-experimental framework. This setting allows us to more eﬀec-
tively distinguish the experience eﬀects from other channels, compared 
to other contexts like foreclosures or other adverse economic shocks. 
Our results reveal that exposure to extensive housing destruction leads 
to lower housing market participation decades later, after controlling 
for regional diﬀerences and a broad set of household characteristics. 
We ﬁnd that this variation in participation is to a large extent driven by

The  WWII  destruction  in  Germany,  which  leveled  approximately 
4 million ﬂats and left 13 million Germans homeless (Hampe, 1963), 
provides a natural setting to study the impact of negative housing expe-
riences on household attitudes and preferences toward homeownership. 
While the Allied Air Forces bombing led to a total destruction of 22% 
of Germany’s housing stock,1 destruction intensity varied substantially 
across cities. Exploiting both the variation in destruction rates across 
German cities and the WWII exposure of diﬀerent cohorts, we ﬁnd that 
a one standard deviation increase in destruction rate leads to an approx-
imately 3 percentage point decrease in the likelihood of homeownership 
for WWII-experiencing cohorts. We verify the robustness of these ﬁnd-
ings  by  performing  several  additional  sensitivity  and  validity  checks 
such as imposing various restrictions on the sample or using alternative 
cohort or destruction deﬁnitions.

Our identiﬁcation strategy relies on a diﬀerence-in-diﬀerences type 
design  that  exploits  the  reasonably  exogenous  regional  variation  in 
destruction intensity and cross-cohort variation in exposure to WWII 
destruction. In particular, we measure the eﬀect of destruction on the 
diﬀerences in housing decisions between war-exposed cohorts and post-
war cohorts in heavily destructed areas relative to less destructed areas. 
This allows us to introduce region × year ﬁxed eﬀects to control for any 
unobserved (time-varying) regional characteristics.2 Our identiﬁcation 
relies on the parallel trends assumption that, had WWII exposure not 
occurred, the diﬀerence in housing decisions between cohorts would be 
the same in regions with diﬀerent levels of destruction. We validate this 
assumption by conducting a falsiﬁcation test on a sample of immigrants 
who arrived in Germany after 1955 (i.e., after the per capita housing 
stock had been restored to pre-war levels) and ﬁnd no eﬀects.

Our nationally-representative household panel data are drawn from 
the German Socio-Economic Panel (SOEP), covering the period from 
1985 to 2017. Since SOEP coverage starts four decades after the end 
of WWII, we study the long-term eﬀects of the destruction experienced 
by households who were children or young adults at the time. SOEP 
includes  detailed  information  on  household  demographic  and  ﬁnan-
cial  characteristics  such  as  age,  income,  education,  occupation,  and 
household size. Beyond these variables, SOEP provides a broad set of 
self-reported “subjective” variables, such as the perceived importance 
of homeownership. Responses to these questions help us to elicit house-
hold attitudes and preferences toward housing, thereby shedding light 
on the mechanisms underlying observed housing outcomes.

It is a-priori not clear how exposure to war destruction would aﬀect 
household attitudes toward homeownership. On the one hand, a bud-
ding literature documents that personal experiences of past economic 
shocks such as stock market realizations, economic downturns or high 
inﬂation have long lasting eﬀects on the economic behavior and choices 
of individuals (Malmendier and Nagel, 2011, 2016; Malmendier and 
Steiny, 2020; Malmendier et al., 2021).3 Since residential war destruc-
tion was a major historical event that represented a signiﬁcant, direct 
shock to the housing market, it is reasonable to conjecture that such 
an experience could lead to negative attitudes toward homeownership. 
On the other hand, survey evidence shows that many households asso-

Our analysis provides strong support for the interpretation that the 
experience  of  wartime  destruction  negatively  aﬀects  the  attitudes  of 
exposed households toward homeownership. Speciﬁcally, we ﬁrst con-
ﬁrm that the response to the importance of homeownership question 
is a strong determinant of housing tenure and reasonably captures the 
homeownership preferences of households. We then show that house-
holds that experienced wartime destruction express signiﬁcantly weaker 
preferences for homeownership decades later, and that the shift in their 
attitudes toward homeownership is the key underlying mechanism un-
derlying our core ﬁndings.

The  analysis  of  household  attitudes  toward  homeownership  also 
provides  insight  into  an  alternative  mechanism:  wartime  destruction 
may aﬀect the housing decisions of exposed households by reducing 
wealth  accumulation.  If  our  results  were  merely  a  product  of  unob-
served wealth shocks, it is not clear why exposed households would 
not desire homeownership as much as others, even when facing con-
straints  that  prevent  them  from  becoming  homeowners.  Thus,  it  is 
unlikely that our results are simply an artifact of unobserved wealth 
eﬀects. In addition, we explore several other alternative explanations, 
including supply-side factors, labor market channels like labor income, 
unemployment risk, or investment in human capital, diﬀerences in risk 
preferences, and access to credit, and ﬁnd little or no supportive evi-
dence for them.

Interestingly,  we  also  document  that  the  eﬀects  of  exposure  to 
wartime  destruction  extend  beyond  the  housing  tenure  decisions  to 
the housing consumption of households. Speciﬁcally, we observe that 
war-exposed cohorts from highly destructed regions tend to inhabit sig-
niﬁcantly smaller dwellings, implying weaker preferences for housing 
consumption many decades later.5 Overall, these results suggest that 
wartime destruction induces a shift in the attitudes of households to-
ward housing in general, encompassing both its role as an investment 
and as a consumption good.

To gain a deeper understanding of the scope of transmission of expe-
rience eﬀects, we examine the impact of wartime destruction on other 
forms of private property ownership or utilization. Our analysis shows 
that the eﬀects of (negative) attitudes toward homeownership do not 
translate into a decreased desire to purchase other goods, nor do they 
signiﬁcantly aﬀect the purchases of durable goods such as furniture or 
vehicles. Furthermore, when we examine the household consumption 
preferences through subjective questions, we ﬁnd that households that 
experienced wartime destruction have a stronger preference for non-
housing consumption goods and services, with travel being a notable 
example.6 Overall, these ﬁndings are consistent with previous literature

Another avenue of transmission we investigate is the inﬂuence of 
speciﬁc housing conditions following WWII. In areas heavily aﬀected 
by the war, households might have found themselves residing in tem-
porary or government-provided housing, which could have potentially 
shaped habits favoring renting and a reluctance to become homeowners 
later in life. To investigate this, we analyze data on early social housing 
construction in the 1950s, ﬁnding no signiﬁcant impact on the home-
ownership attitudes or decisions of war-exposed cohorts. These results 
suggest that government-funded housing facilities and potential habits 
formed  through  them  are  not  the  primary  drivers  behind  our  base-
line  ﬁndings.  Furthermore,  to  diﬀerentiate  homeownership  attitudes 
from habit-based explanations, we explore ownership of properties not 
occupied by the owner, speciﬁcally real estate held purely for invest-
ment purposes. Our analysis suggests that pure real estate ownership 
exhibits an even stronger negative response to wartime destruction ex-
posure, underscoring the domain-speciﬁc nature of these attitudes and 
dispelling the notion that they stem solely from a preference for renting.
The consequences of housing attitudes go beyond the homeowner-
ship rate. They also have implications for household wealth and its com-
position. We ﬁnd that, on average, the ﬁnancial wealth of destruction-
exposed  households  is  similar  to  others,  but  these  households  have 
lower total wealth, possibly due to their lower homeownership rate. 
However, among households with positive total (gross) wealth,7 there is 
no signiﬁcant diﬀerence between the total gross wealth of destruction-
exposed households and others, while destruction-exposed households 
have signiﬁcantly higher ﬁnancial assets. Consequently, their portfolios 
are tilted away from housing toward ﬁnancial assets: a one standard de-
viation increase in war destruction results in a more than 3 percentage 
point higher share of ﬁnancial assets for WWII-experiencing cohorts.

The potential reasons underlying the unusually low homeownership 
rate in Germany (44% as of 2010, which was the lowest rate in the Eu-
ropean Union at that time) have attracted interest both from academics 
and  policymakers.  For  example,  Kaas  et  al.  (2021) solve  a  life-cycle 
model of consumption and housing with endogenous homeownership 
and attribute the observed low levels of homeownership to high trans-
fer taxes on home purchases, no mortgage interest tax deductibility, and 
the social housing sector in Germany. Malmendier and Steiny (2020), 
on the other hand, relate cross-country diﬀerences in homeownership 
rates to past inﬂation experiences such that the low inﬂation experience 
of current German households is consistent with their lower tendency 
to own a house. We contribute to this discussion by providing a novel 
additional channel: the widespread destruction Germany experienced 
during  WWII  had  long-lasting  eﬀects  on  household  attitudes  toward 
homeownership, contributing to a lower homeownership rate. Our es-
timates  imply  that  the  homeownership  rate  of  West  German  natives 
would be approximately 5 percentage points higher in 1985, the ﬁrst 
year of our sample period, in the absence of exposure to war destruc-
tion. Furthermore, the declining share of the war-exposed cohort in the 
population can account for half of the increase in the aggregate home-
ownership rate since 1985.

2.  Historical background

2.1.  Residential destruction during WWII

The majority of German cities experienced widespread destruction 
through the aerial bombardment by the Allied Air Forces during WWII. 
Over the course of the bombing period from 1939 until 1945, more than 
1.6 million tons of bombs were dropped on German ground that, in to-
tal, erased more than 20% of the German housing stock (Diefendorf, 
1993). Until early 1942, the British Royal Air Force (RAF) focused its 
bombing campaign on key industrial targets essential for the German 
war eﬀort (e.g., metal production plants, military aircraft factories, and 
oil reﬁneries). This strategy turned out to be ineﬀective to reduce the 
German war eﬀorts. The Area Bombing Directive, issued in March 1942 
by the British Government’s Air Ministry, ordered bombers to attack 
city centers as new principal targets. The main idea of the new bomb-
ing strategy was that the destruction of urban areas would depress the 
morale of the general population and destroy Germany’s will to ﬁght. 
The US Air Force joined the British RAF in their aerial bombing pur-
suit. The vast majority of these second-wave attacks were broad area 
bombardments rather than focused attacks on economic targets.

Speciﬁcally, the selection of target cities was mainly driven by build-
ing density, presence of visible landmarks, and navigational capacity 
rather than the economic importance of those cities (Friedrich, 2002). 
For example, Vonyó (2012) ﬁnds no statistically signiﬁcant relation be-
tween pre-war industrialization levels and the degree of destruction in 
a given region. Similarly, historical accounts of the bombardment sug-
gest that wartime destruction also depended on random factors such as 
weather conditions, pilot errors, and technological malfunctions in ad-
dition to the ﬁxed regional characteristics (e.g., proximity to the British 
airﬁelds) (Akbulut-Yuksel, 2014).

Overall, WWII destruction in Germany leveled approximately 4 mil-
lion ﬂats and left 13 million Germans homeless (Hampe, 1963), provid-
ing  a  natural  laboratory  to  examine  the  long-run  eﬀects  of  negative 
housing  experiences  on  household  attitudes  toward  homeownership. 
Importantly, almost all major German cities suﬀered to some degree 
from aerial bombings, though, the intensity of physical destruction var-
ied substantially across regions. Panels A and B of Fig. 1 illustrate the 
heterogeneity of destruction across West German regions as measured

2.2.  Post-war policies

The dire housing situation in West Germany in the period follow-
ing WWII was further aggravated by the arrival of millions of refugees 
from Eastern German territories and the Soviet Occupation Zone, lead-
ing to a housing shortage of around 5 million housing units by 1950 
(Wertheimer, 1958).8 Several policies were introduced by the German 
federal government in an attempt to address the economic and housing 
challenges. First, the 1948 currency reform exchanged ten Reichsmark
for one Deutsche Mark, reducing ﬁnancial assets and liabilities. This was 
followed by a substantial levy in 1952, Lastenausgleich or “equalization 
of burdens act,” which entailed a quasi-ﬂat 50% tax on the net wealth 
of households and companies as assessed in 1948. The tax was levied on 
households whose wealth survived the war or beneﬁted from the cur-
rency reform, annuitized to be paid over 30 years, and redistributed to 
refugees and households whose assets were destroyed in the war, albeit 
many years after the destruction.9 While Lastenausgleich did not provide

for renting purposes, which led to a rise in the number of housing units

1961 (Bundesamt, 1955, 1964). Most social housing was built by the 
private sector using subsidized government loans. Units were oﬀered

gradually declined.11 Finally, the housing beneﬁt, Wohngeld, was intro-

to aﬀord housing due to low income or high rents.12 As presented in 
section 4.4, we ensure the robustness of our results by excluding house-
holds living in social housing or receiving housing beneﬁts.

3.  Data and empirical strategy

In this section, we ﬁrst introduce the data sources and provide de-
tailed information on our key variables of interest. We then discuss the 
econometric challenges in the empirical analysis and explain how we 
tackle them.

3.1.  Data and sample construction

We hand collect information on the percentage of ﬂats destroyed 
during WWII from the statistical yearbooks of German municipalities 
(Kästner, 1949) and use it as our preferred measure of WWII destruction 
in a given region.13 The yearbooks provide information on the Western 
German municipalities with more than 20,000 inhabitants as of 1946, 
which  accounted  for  47%  of  the  historical  West  German  population 
(Burchardi and Hassan, 2013). Approximately 10% of the municipal-
ities did not report the share of ﬂats destroyed during WWII. Following 
Kästner (1949), we treat those municipalities with missing destruction 
information as little- or no-destruction regions. We validate our mea-
sure by using auxiliary data on the total tonnage of bombs dropped in 
each region reported by the British and American air forces from Davis 
(2006) and set the destruction level of the municipalities with missing 
information to zero if the tonnage of bombs dropped on a municipal-
ity is zero or very low relative to its number of ﬂats.14 We aggregate 
the municipal destruction rates to the region (Raumordnungsregion) level 
by calculating the mean percentage of ﬂats destroyed, weighted by the 
pre-war population of municipalities, to match them to the location of 
households. Regions are analogous to metropolitan statistical areas in 
the  U.S.  but  also  include  rural  areas.  West  Germany  is  divided  geo-
graphically into 75 such regions, while the WWII destruction data are 
available for 73 of these regions.

In  addition,  we  make  use  of  the  German  Socio-Economic  Panel 
(SOEP) (Goebel et al., 2019), which is a nationally representative house-
hold panel data, collected annually since 1984. SOEP provides detailed 
information  on  demographic  and  ﬁnancial  characteristics  of  respon-
dents such as age, education, marital status, place of residence, income, 
as well as about their attitudes and beliefs. We focus on the sample 
period from 1985 to 2017 and merge the regional WWII destruction 
measures with the SOEP data using each respondent’s ﬁrst region loca-
tion recorded when included in the panel.15

3.2.  Identiﬁcation strategy

This section describes our empirical framework to identify the ef-
fect of exposure to housing destruction during WWII on households’ 
housing tenure and consumption decisions. We conjecture that the ex-
perience of wartime destruction would lead to lower housing market 
participation and housing consumption in areas with higher levels of 
destruction. The key econometric challenge we face is potential omit-
ted  variable  bias  when  examining  the  relationship  between  regional 
wartime destruction and present day housing outcomes. In particular, 
there may be unobserved (time-varying) regional characteristics that 
simultaneously aﬀect the destruction in a given area and the housing 
choices of households several decades later. For example, destruction 
during WWII may have inﬂuenced economic or ﬁnancial development 
in a given region that can have persistent eﬀects today (e.g., local con-
struction cycles or trends in social housing supply).

4.  The eﬀects of war destruction on homeownership and housing 
preferences

In this section, we ﬁrst present the results of the baseline analysis of 
the long-term eﬀects of exposure to wartime destruction on individual 
housing decisions. We then report the results of a control experiment 
with a focus on a sample of post-war immigrants to Germany. Finally, 
we discuss several economic mechanisms through which the experience 
of housing destruction during World War II aﬀects individual housing 
decisions.

The sign and statistical signiﬁcance of other controls in our estima-

household ﬁnance literature. For example, the income and education 
level of household heads and household size consistently exhibit posi-

for living in a rural area and being a male are not precisely estimated. 
It’s worth emphasizing that the introduction of birth year ﬁxed eﬀects in 
our regressions enables us to isolate the impact of exposure to regional 
destruction from the broader lifetime economic factors that households 
encounter, such as stock market returns (Malmendier and Nagel, 2011), 
inﬂation (Malmendier and Nagel, 2016; Malmendier and Steiny, 2020), 
or economic growth, all of which are well-documented as relevant and 
signiﬁcant determinants of household economic behavior.

Beyond its role as an investment good and asset class, housing also 
serves as a major consumption good (Piazzesi et al., 2007). Notably, in 
Germany, housing expenditures constitute approximately one-quarter 
of net household income.20 Therefore, the war-induced destruction of 
housing had a dual impact — it not only impacted housing investment 
returns but also signiﬁcantly disrupted housing consumption. This im-
pact was keenly felt by the war-exposed cohorts in our sample, most of 
whom were children at the time, irrespective of whether their families 
owned or rented their housing units.21 Given this context, we now turn 
our attention to investigating whether the eﬀects of experiencing hous-
ing destruction extend beyond homeownership decisions to inﬂuence 
households’ housing (consumption) preferences.22

It is worth noting, as a preliminary consideration, that the German 
rental market is highly developed, with the majority of rental housing 
in Germany being owned by private households.23 Consequently, the 
stock of housing units available for rent or ownership is well-integrated, 
allowing  households  to  ﬁnd  rental  or  owner-occupied  housing  in  all 
location and quality segments. This ﬂexibility enables them to adjust 
their housing consumption, regardless of their choice of tenure.24

To assess households’ preferences for housing, we consider the quan-

in  square  meters.25 For  a  given  quality,  dwelling  size  would  be  the

to housing destruction has a “long shadow” adverse eﬀect on both the 
extensive and intensive housing margins of exposed households. These

4.2.  Control experiment: immigrant households

In our empirical analysis, we identify the long-run eﬀects of wartime 
destruction by leveraging regional disparities in the severity of housing 
damage, coupled with variations across cohorts in exposure to WWII 
destruction. This strategy enables us to incorporate region × year ﬁxed 
eﬀects, thereby mitigating potential inﬂuences from unobserved, pos-
sibly  time-varying  regional  characteristics  like  the  level  of  regional 
(ﬁnancial) development. Such characteristics could be simultaneously 
linked to both the wartime destruction rate and the outcomes for house-
holds residing in those areas. Furthermore, our causal analysis hinges 
on the assumption of parallel trends, suggesting that if WWII exposure 
had not occurred, the disparities in homeownership rates and housing 
consumption across cohorts would have remained consistent across re-
gions with diﬀering levels of destruction. To validate this assumption, 
we conduct a control experiment. Speciﬁcally, we focus on the housing 
tenure and consumption decisions of a sample of ﬁrst-generation im-
migrants who arrived in Germany after 1955 (i.e., after the per capita 
housing stock had been restored to prewar levels), and thus, were not 
directly exposed to the wartime destruction in their area of residence.
The immigrant sample comprises around 40,000 household-year ob-
servations. As outlined in Table O.A.5 in the Online Appendix, 43% of 
the sampled immigrants arrived in Germany prior to 1975, with over 
50% of immigrant households having resided in the country for at least 
22 years at the time of observation. On average, an immigrant in the 
sample is 48 years old and has a monthly household income of €2,035, 
a ﬁgure comparable to that of native individuals who are, on average, 
52 years old with a monthly household income of €2,150.26

4.3.  Household attitudes toward homeownership

The analysis so far shows that the experience of wartime destruction, 
a major historical event that represents a signiﬁcant, direct shock to the 
housing market, has robust, long-lasting eﬀects on households’ housing 
tenure and consumption decisions. These ﬁndings are consistent with 
personal experiences of past events, particularly during formative years, 
being an important determinant of individuals’ economic behavior and 
choices later in life (Malmendier and Nagel, 2011, 2016; Malmendier 
and Wachter, 2022).

This interpretation is clouded, however, by the question of whether 
our ﬁndings might simply be an artifact of diﬀerences in wealth accu-
mulation between households in high and low destruction regions. In 
particular, (unobserved) negative wealth shocks caused by the bomb-
ing of Germany during WWII could also lead to lower housing market 
participation and housing consumption in places with higher levels of 
destruction, rather than the experience of housing destruction per se. To 
partially address this concern, we oﬀer an detailed discussion of several 
tests examining wealth eﬀects in Section B.2 of the Online Appendix. 
For instance, when we exclude households that report inheriting their 
current primary residence, receiving a gift or inheritance, or losing a 
parent due to the war from the sample, and subsequently re-run the re-
gressions for homeownership and housing consumption, we ﬁnd that 
the loss of parental wealth and the inheritance channel have little to no 
impact on our baseline results.29

Nevertheless,  we  acknowledge  the  possibility  that  wealth  eﬀects 
may  operate  through  other  channels  that  are  unrelated  to  parental 
wealth.  In  response  to  lingering  concerns  regarding  these  potential 
wealth eﬀects, we adopt an alternative approach that capitalizes on the 
depth of our dataset, which furnishes information on households’ be-
liefs and preferences, including their attitudes toward homeownership. 
Speciﬁcally, we directly elicit households’ homeownership preferences 
from their responses to a survey question about the importance of being 
a homeowner. The SOEP administers this attitude question in selected 
years beginning in 1990. Responses to this question range from 0 (“not 
at all important”) to 3 (“very important”). Utilizing this ordinal vari-
able, we then construct an indicator variable that takes the value of 1 if 
the respondent rates the importance of being a homeowner with “very 
important” or “important” and zero otherwise. Our rationale for this ap-
proach is that if the baseline results were driven entirely by unobserved

principle, memories of past experiences can have an impact on the eco-

preference  channel.  However,  as  noted  by  Malmendier  and  Wachter

this section underscore the signiﬁcance of the preference channel, as 
we  demonstrate  that  the  experience  of  wartime  destruction  prompts 
households to develop adverse attitudes toward homeownership. Con-
sequently, this leads to reduced participation in the housing market and 
diminished housing consumption in the decades that follow. This mech-
anism aligns with the theoretical model put forth by Bernheim et al. 
(2021), which posits that such experiences can have long-lasting eﬀects 
through shifts in preferences.32 In Section 5, we probe into the nature 
of various transmission mechanisms to gain a better understanding of 
the transmission process of experience eﬀects.

4.4.  Robustness to alternative interpretations

In  this  section,  we  examine  other  mechanisms,  alternative  to  the 
attitudes and preferences channel, that can potentially produce similar 
eﬀects and discuss how we address them.

The ﬁrst mechanism we consider is the inﬂuence of supply-side fac-
tors. It’s imperative to account for potential variations in these factors, 
such as the ownership structure within the housing market, housing 
supply dynamics, or subsidies, which could diﬀer between regions with 
high and low destruction levels. Notably, our estimations consistently 
incorporate region × year ﬁxed eﬀects, eﬀectively encompassing all un-
observed aspects related to a region, including the factors mentioned 
earlier, that could potentially impact individual housing decisions. Fur-
thermore, our control experiment involving the immigrant sample, as 
detailed and discussed in Section 4.2, furnishes additional evidence that 
our results do not simply arise from disparities in supply-side factors 
across  local  housing  markets.  We  do  not  observe  any  systematic  ef-
fects among immigrant households of the same cohorts residing in the 
same regions and sharing the same local environment as native individ-
uals who were directly exposed to wartime destruction. Nevertheless, 
we continue to conduct additional tests to assess the robustness of our 
ﬁndings in the presence of potential variations in the housing market’s 
ownership structure, as well as the availability of social housing and 
subsidies to households across regions.

Government subsidies, such as Wohngeld (direct payments to low-
income  households),  and  the  availability  of  social  housing  (below-
market-rate rental housing) may inﬂuence the housing tenure choices

Another  factor  that  can  inﬂuence  housing  supply  and  household 
tenure choices is the regulation of rental markets. Particularly, strin-
gent rent control policies, if in place, may favor existing tenants and 
potentially discourage homeownership. Such policies might also distort 
the rental supply and diminish the quality and quantity of available 
rental units. However, it’s important to note that, after resolving the 
acute post-war housing shortage, West Germany introduced the Tenure 
Security Act of 1971 (Wohnraumkündigungsschutzgesetz), which permit-
ted free negotiation of initial rents and rent increases in accordance 
with comparable properties. This eﬀectively deregulated the rental mar-
ket (Hubert, 1998). Consequently, housing supply and tenure choices 
in  West  Germany  were  not  subject  to  distortion  due  to  rent  control 
policies. In summary, our ﬁndings do not support a signiﬁcant role for 
supply-side factors in explaining our results.

Another mechanism we explore is the potential impact of wartime 
destruction on labor market outcomes. It is plausible that residing in an 
environment with higher wartime destruction could lead to less favor-
able labor market outcomes, subsequently inﬂuencing lower housing 
market  participation  and  housing  consumption.  Wartime  destruction 
may  introduce  additional  constraints  and  obstacles  for  war-aﬀected 
households, hindering their capacity to invest in human capital. More-
over, it could directly aﬀect regional labor demand through adverse 
repercussions on local development.

We take several steps to address potential labor market eﬀects. First, 
we acknowledge that wartime destruction is associated with lower ed-
ucational  attainment  among  aﬀected  cohorts,  as  evidenced  by  their 
reduced years of schooling, as shown in Table O.A.10 of the Online 
Appendix. This result is consistent with the ﬁndings of Akbulut-Yuksel 
(2014). We also examine the likelihood of obtaining a college education 
and ﬁnd signiﬁcant negative eﬀects of exposure to wartime destruction 
on this outcome. To further account for labor market eﬀects, we incor-
porate controls for education, occupation, industry of employment, and 
labor income, such as earnings or years of schooling, in all our regres-
sions. This approach explicitly considers their inﬂuence on households’ 
decisions regarding homeownership and housing consumption.

Additionally, we directly estimate the eﬀects of residential destruc-
tion on the labor income and unemployment risk of war-exposed house-
holds.  Strikingly,  the  regression  estimates,  presented  in  Panel  B  of 
Table 5, reveal a positive and signiﬁcant eﬀect of exposure to destruc-
tion on the earnings of war-experiencing cohorts, after controlling for 
other household characteristics. Speciﬁcally, a one standard deviation 
increase in destruction in the region leads to a 2.2% increase in the 
earnings of the exposed cohort. We also examine unemployment risk

and  ﬁnd  no  signiﬁcant  eﬀects.  Furthermore,  we  investigate  whether

Another mechanism we examine is the role of risk preferences. It is 
possible that the eﬀects we document operate through household risk 
preferences rather than preferences for homeownership. Major shocks, 
such as experiencing a ﬁnancial crisis, can impact household risk pref-
erences and subsequently inﬂuence their willingness to take risks, as 
demonstrated  by  Guiso  et  al.  (2018).  To  explore  this  alternative  ex-
planation, we directly link wartime destruction to various measures of 
household attitudes toward risk.  Following the approach of Dohmen 
et  al.  (2012),  we  use  two  measures  of  risk  aversion  directly  elicited 
through the SOEP survey: the ﬁrst measures the general willingness to 
take risks, and the second assesses the willingness to take ﬁnancial risks. 
The results, reported in columns (1) and (2) of Panel C of Table 5, reveal 
no signiﬁcant eﬀect of wartime destruction on the qualitative indicators 
of risk preferences. Furthermore, we examine the stock market partici-
pation decisions of households in column (3) of Panel C. Once again, we 
ﬁnd no signiﬁcant relationship between exposure to destruction and the 
actual ﬁnancial risk-taking behavior of households. Notably, this ﬁnd-
ing aligns with another key feature of experience eﬀects discussed in 
the literature, which suggests that these eﬀects are speciﬁc to a particu-
lar context or domain, as highlighted by Malmendier and Nagel (2011)
and Malmendier and Wachter (2022). In our case, the speciﬁc domain 
is housing, as discussed in more detail in Section 5.

The ﬁnal mechanism we investigate relates to the inﬂuence of ﬁnan-
cial institutions. The impact of war destruction on homeownership and 
housing preferences may be mediated through its eﬀect on access to 
local ﬁnancial institutions. Brown et al. (2019) demonstrate that early 
exposure to ﬁnancial institutions results in long-term improvements in 
ﬁnancial literacy and consumer credit outcomes. Similarly, D’Acunto et 
al. (2019) have found that households residing in regions with a history 
of higher antisemitism exhibit a reduced tendency to utilize mortgage 
ﬁnancing. Disparities in access to debt could consequently lead to vari-
ations in both housing market participation and housing consumption. 
To explore this potential channel, we examine direct participation in 
the debt market and ﬁnd no signiﬁcant eﬀect on household debt behav-
ior, as detailed in Online Appendix Table O.A.11.

5.  On the process of transmission

In Section 4.3, we have demonstrated that households who experi-
enced housing destruction during WWII developed negative attitudes 
toward homeownership. These attitudes, in turn, resulted in decreased 
rates of homeownership and housing consumption decades later. In this 
section, we delve deeper into the mechanisms of transmission to gain 
a better understanding of the scope of transmission and to explore the 
channels through which it may operate.

As we measure the extent of destruction through residential damage, 
our investigations thus far have focused on the impact of destruction ex-
perience on housing consumption and investment. However, it is worth 
noting that WWII housing destruction may have encompassed not just 
the loss of the housing stock itself but also a wide range of other pri-
vately owned physical assets and objects, whether related or unrelated 
to  housing  (such  as  means  of  transportation,  furniture,  and  various 
types of physical property). Therefore, we now turn our attention to 
examining whether wartime destruction similarly impacts the owner-
ship or utilization of private property beyond housing. If such eﬀects 
exist, they would suggest that the experience eﬀect of destruction has 
a broader reach, extending to various forms of private property rather 
than being conﬁned solely to housing.

previous evidence indicating that the inﬂuence of past experiences is 
domain-speciﬁc.  Personal  experiences  within  a  particular  asset  class, 
such as stock or house price movements, tend to aﬀect expectations or 
economic behavior only within the same domain. For instance, experi-
ences with stock prices primarily aﬀect stock prices and stock market 
participation, while experiences related to house price movements pri-
marily inﬂuence house price expectations. This domain-speciﬁc pattern 
has  been  extensively  documented  in  studies  such  as  those  by  Mal-
mendier and Nagel (2011), Kuchler and Zafar (2019), and Malmendier 
and Wachter (2022). In our case, the observed adjustments are not in-
dicative of a broad change in attitudes towards property ownership but 
rather pertain to the speciﬁc domain in which a positive or negative 
outcome has been experienced, namely housing.

Another potential avenue of transmission could have been the spe-
ciﬁc housing conditions following WWII. Following the extensive de-
struction  of  housing,  numerous  households  in  heavily  aﬀected  areas 
may  have  been  compelled  to  reside  in  temporary  housing  or  hous-

external circumstances might have contributed to a lack of familiar-
ity  with  owner-occupied  housing  (or  a  taste  for  renting)  during  the 
formative years for households exposed to wartime destruction. This 
lack of habit, rather than direct exposure to housing destruction, could 
have inﬂuenced households’ development of negative attitudes towards 
homeownership,  potentially  leading  them  to  be  reluctant  to  become 
homeowners later in life.

6.  Implications of the ﬁndings

6.1.  From homeownership to wealth accumulation

The shift in attitudes toward homeownership can also have impli-
cations  for  the  composition  and  accumulation  of  wealth.  Housing  is 
known to be the primary saving instrument for most households (Good-
man and Mayer, 2018), and high realized returns to housing, especially 
when purchased with leverage, can lead to signiﬁcant asset accumu-
lation.  The  ﬁndings  in  a  contemporaneous  paper  by  Halbmeier  and 
Schröder  (2021) showing  that  war-exposed  cohorts  in  more  destruc-
ted regions tend to accumulate less wealth would be consistent with 
missing out on such eﬀects.

A full-ﬂedged analysis of wealth eﬀects is beyond the scope of our 
study, mainly due to several data-related constraints. First, SOEP starts

to collect wealth information in the second half of our sample period 
beginning from 2002, and this information is only collected at a ﬁve-
year frequency. This implies that we observe household portfolios only 
in four out of 33 waves of our data. Second, based on the available 
data, we observe that around one-tenth of the respondents report having 
zero  gross  total  wealth,  which  is  deﬁned  as  the  sum  of  all  real  and 
ﬁnancial assets. We acknowledge that while those responses may reﬂect 
the true value of household wealth, some may be due to recall bias 
(Vissing-Jorgensen, 2002). Keeping these caveats in mind, we provide 
some interesting indicative evidence on the composition of wealth in 
Table 9.

Column (1) reports the estimates for the eﬀect of exposure to war 
destruction on the logarithm of gross total wealth of households. We 
see that war-exposed households who reside in regions with higher de-
struction accumulate signiﬁcantly less gross wealth, consistent with the 
ﬁndings in Halbmeier and Schröder (2021). Interestingly, as presented 
in column (2), the coeﬃcient estimate for the log ﬁnancial wealth is 
positive but not precisely estimated.

Our ﬁndings in columns (3) to (4) point to a more nuanced inter-
pretation of the wealth results. When we restrict the sample to house-
holds with positive gross wealth, we see that exposure to destruction 
no longer signiﬁcantly aﬀects gross wealth while it signiﬁcantly posi-
tively contributes to the level of ﬁnancial wealth. These results imply 
that destruction-exposed households with positive wealth tend to tilt 
their  portfolios  toward  ﬁnancial  assets.  Accordingly,  as  presented  in 
column (5) of Table 9, we ﬁnd that a one standard deviation increase 
in war destruction results in a more than 3 percentage point higher 
share  of  ﬁnancial  assets  for  WWII-experiencing  cohorts.  Overall,  our 
ﬁndings imply that consequences of housing attitudes extend beyond 
homeownership and also have implications for household wealth and 
its composition.

6.2.  Aggregate homeownership

Our analysis so far shows that exposure to extensive residential de-
struction results in an economically large reduction in housing market 
participation  decades  after  experiencing  the  shock.  We  came  to  this 
conclusion using a diﬀerence-in-diﬀerence type methodology by com-
paring the homeownership outcomes of diﬀerent cohorts in areas that 
diﬀer in their destruction rates. We can also think about the implica-
tions of exposure to destruction for the aggregate homeownership rates 
in Germany. Given that almost all cities experienced some destruction, 
exposure may have a sizable impact on aggregate homeownership rates 
and oﬀer an additional underlying reason for the low historical home-
ownership rates in Germany (Kaas et al., 2021). Furthermore, the share 
of war-exposed cohorts in the population has changed signiﬁcantly over 
the sample period, so the aggregate eﬀect would reﬂect this composi-
tion change over time.

Using the ﬁtted model of column (3) in Table 2 (our main speciﬁ-
cation), we predict a counterfactual homeownership rate had the war 
exposure not occurred (i.e., assuming a hypothetical destruction level 
of zero) and plot it in Panel A of Fig. 2 along with the actual home-
ownership rate in the data. We calculate that war exposure led to a 
3.8 percentage points lower homeownership rate in 1985 compared to 
our  counterfactual  of  no  war  destruction.  The  eﬀect  declined  to  1.1 
percentage points by 2017. The average eﬀect over the years is 2.3 per-
centage points.39 The decline in the aggregate eﬀect over time is due

7.  Conclusion

In this paper, we explore the potential link between negative hous-
ing  experiences  and  long-lasting  attitude  shifts  toward  housing  and 
homeownership, utilizing the residential destruction resulting from the 
bombing of Germany during WWII as a quasi-experiment. Exploiting 
the reasonably exogenous region-by-cohort variation in exposure to de-
struction, we ﬁrst document that residential destruction caused by the 
bombardment  of  Germany  during  WWII  leads  to  lower  participation 
in the housing market and lower housing consumption among exposed 
households decades later. This negative eﬀect is present after control-
ling for regional diﬀerences and a rich set of relevant household char-
acteristics. We verify these ﬁndings by performing several additional 
sensitivity and robustness tests.

We  investigate  the  economic  mechanisms  responsible  for  the  en-
during impact of wartime destruction exposure on household housing 
decisions. Our analysis reveals that variations in homeownership and 
housing consumption are primarily attributed to shifts in household at-
titudes. In contrast, alternative explanations, including wealth eﬀects, 
supply-side inﬂuences, labor market dynamics, and household risk pref-
erences, receive minimal or no empirical support. Importantly, the ad-
verse eﬀects observed in homeownership attitudes do not spill over into 
ownership  of  other  tangible  assets,  underscoring  the  domain-speciﬁc 
nature  of  the  war  destruction’s  inﬂuence.  Furthermore,  these  eﬀects 
are not a result of an indirect impact through changes in consumption 
habits.

Exposure to wartime destruction could have had a substantial im-
pact on the overall homeownership rate in Germany, oﬀering insights 
into the observed low homeownership rates. We estimate that war ex-
posure reduced the homeownership rate in 1985 by about 4 percentage 
points relative to a counterfactual scenario without wartime destruc-
tion. Moreover, the declining presence of the war-exposed cohort since 
1985 can account for approximately half of the subsequent increase in 
the homeownership rate over the next three decades.

Finally,  it  is  worth  noting  that  the  interpretation  of  our  results 
requires some nuance, as we study a major historical event that sig-
niﬁcantly  aﬀected  the  housing  market.  On  the  one  hand,  the  eﬀects 
we document might stem from the catastrophic nature of the shock we 
study – similar eﬀects may or may not emerge in the case of a less 
intense shock. On the other hand, the extreme nature of the housing 
destruction could act as a “magnifying glass” for the experience eﬀects 
that would also alter household attitudes and decisions, albeit to a lesser 
extent, in the case of a less severe shock. Both interpretations hold rele-
vance and carry important implications. We remain agnostic about the 
dominance of one explanation over the other and leave it to future re-
search to determine which of these forces is at play.
