This paper studies how common institutional ownership (CIO) aﬀects information diﬀusion in the stock market. 
My ﬁndings suggest that CIO can exacerbate the slow spread of information across ﬁrms. With over 50% of 
institutional investors holding concentrated stock portfolios, I infer a fundamental connection among ﬁrms 
with  CIO.  These  ﬁrms  exhibit  cross-predictability  in  monthly  stock  returns,  leading  to  a  CIO-based  peer 
momentum  strategy  that  outperforms  Ali  and  Hirshleifer’s  (2020) shared-analyst  momentum  strategy.  This 
anomaly  stems  primarily  from  institutional  investors  with  fewer  stock  holdings,  who  employ  passive  asset 
management characterized by lower portfolio turnover and more delegated investment.

1.  Introduction

Researchers test the hypothesis of gradual information diﬀusion in 
the stock market by examining the stock returns of economically re-
lated  ﬁrms.  A  positive  serial  cross-correlation  of  stock  returns1 has 
been found among ﬁrms that are related by industry and product mar-
ket segmentation (e.g., Hou, 2007; Cohen and Lou, 2012; Hoberg and 
Phillips,  2018;  Eisdorfer  et  al.,  2022),  supplier-customer  chain  (e.g., 
Cohen and Frazzini, 2008; Menzly and Ozbas, 2010), production tech-
nology (e.g., Lee et al., 2019) and geographic proximity (e.g., Addoum 
et al., 2020; Parsons et al., 2020). Investor inattention or information 
processing constraint is often considered as the friction that slows down 
the information diﬀusion process. Previous studies show that institu-
tional investor ownership mitigates the pricing friction and expedites 
information diﬀusion, particularly when institutional investors simulta-
neously hold stocks that are economically related. For example, Cohen 

and Frazzini (2008) and Menzly and Ozbas (2010) ﬁnd that stock re-
turn cross-predictability fades as common institutional ownership (CIO) 
emerges.

Does common institutional ownership always lead to faster dissemi-
nation of information in the stock market? To answer this question, this 
paper examines the stock return cross-predictability of ﬁrms that are 
commonly  owned  by  institutional  investors.  Using  the  holdings  data 
extracted from Security and Exchange Commission (SEC) 13(f) ﬁlings 
over the 1980-2017 period, I show that ﬁrms with CIO tend to share 
more similar fundamentals than those in the same industry. This is be-
cause over half of institutional investors tend to hold concentrated stock 
portfolios (i.e., less than 60 domestic common stocks), which is consis-
tent with van Nieuwerburgh and Veldkamp (2009, 2010), who argue 
that investors should focus on a subset of stocks in which they have 
a  comparative  advantage  of  information.  These  ﬁrms,  despite  being 
owned by the same group of institutional investors, show signiﬁcant

The analysis of economic mechanisms demonstrates that the gradual 
dissemination of information among ﬁrms that share common owner-
ship  is  largely  due  to  passive  portfolio  management  by  institutional 
investors. The calculation of CIO peer momentum gives more weight 
to the returns of stocks held by institutional investors who hold fewer 
stocks. These investors tend to have lower portfolio turnover and del-
egate  more  assets  to  external  asset  managers  like  exchange-traded 
funds (ETFs). The abnormal returns from the cross-predictability de-
cline  when  institutional  investors  with  more  concentrated  portfolios 
are removed from the sample. My research suggests that institutional 
investors with more diversiﬁed portfolios expedite the information dif-
fusion process. However, the anomaly persists as institutional investors 
tend to only pay attention to the peer momentum within their own port-
folios. I also ﬁnd that institutional investors increase (reduce) holdings 
of undervalued (overvalued) stocks while disposing the stocks that are 
no longer fundamentally related.

Compared to industry, market sector, and other businesses, the eco-
nomic link among commonly owned ﬁrms is much more inconspicuous. 
Conceptually, most if not all institutional investors are bound by bench-
mark  policy  to  diversify  their  portfolios.  Common  ownership  should 
indicate no economic link if institutional investors hold a large num-
ber of stocks to achieve diversiﬁcation or execute an index investing 
strategy. Nonetheless, SEC 13(f) ﬁlings show that the number of stocks 
directly held by institutional investors is far below what a diversiﬁed 
portfolio requires. As illustrated in Fig. 1, about a quarter of institu-
tional investors in the United States held less than 20 domestic common 
stocks  on  average  during  the  1980–2017  period,  while  only  another 
quarter held more than 140 stocks.

The  concentration  of  institutional  portfolios  can  be  explained  by 
theory  and  recent  developments  in  ﬁnancial  markets.  Theoretically, 
as  argued  by  van  Nieuwerburgh  and  Veldkamp  (2009,  2010),  ratio-
nal  investors  should  concentrate  on  a  set  of  assets  whose  value  can 
be better assessed with the information advantage they possess. Insti-
tutional investors would incur more costs to cover a large bundle of 
unrelated ﬁrms than focusing on a small set of related ﬁrms, because 
they may need to hire more security analysts and conduct more indus-
try research to invest in more unrelated ﬁrms. Institutional investors 
using passive investment vehicles also favor concentrated portfolios. For 
instance, exchange-traded funds (ETFs) oﬀer institutional investors an 
eﬀective yet less costly option for diversiﬁcation and benchmark track-

ing. Fig. 2 shows a decline in the number of stocks held by institutional 
investors against their growing population. The number of institutional 
investors grows more than 8 times from 1980 to 2017, while the median 
number of stocks they hold declines from 100 to 60. Meanwhile, the 
average percentage of ETF holdings in institutional portfolios plunged 
from 1997 to 2017.

One important consequence of institutional investors concentrating 
on a small set of stocks is that economically related ﬁrms or ﬁrms that 
operate  in  similar  sectors  or  industries  are  commonly  owned  by  the 
same group of investors. For example, from 2007 to 2017, 76.3% of 
the institutional investors who invested in Spirit AeroSystems (one of 
Boeing’s main suppliers) also held shares of Boeing, while only 39.1% 
held shares of Papa John’s.2

To empirically test the implication of CIO for ﬁrms’ fundamental 
connection, I use a novel measure of common ownership as the weight-
ing scheme to examine the correlation between the fundamentals of a 
ﬁrm and the weighted composite of other commonly owned ﬁrms. I 
estimate the common ownership of any two ﬁrms using the weighted 
count of institutional investors of both ﬁrms with the weight being in-
versely proportional to the number of stocks each institutional investor 
holds. The weighting scheme puts a higher weight on common owner-
ship by institutional investors who concentrate on fewer stocks. Two 
stocks  with  common  ownership  by  more  diversiﬁed  institutional  in-
vestors are less likely to be related. The common ownership by indexers 
inevitably adds noise to the identiﬁcation of economic links but does not 
bias the results substantially.3 With the weighting scheme, the common 
ownership measure between Spirit AeroSystems and Boeing (0.368) be-
comes four times higher than the common ownership between Spirit 
AeroSystem and Papa John’s (0.070).

I ﬁnd a series of signiﬁcant correlations and lead-lag relations be-
tween  commonly  owned  ﬁrms  in  regard  to  annual  growth  in  sales, 
gross proﬁt, research and development (R&D) expense, and total assets.

The superior cross-predictability of fundamentals and stock returns 
among  commonly  owned  ﬁrms  suggests  several  methodological  ad-
vantages of using CIO for peer identiﬁcation. First, CIO captures the

multidimensional  and  time-varying  nature  of  the  connection  of  peer 
ﬁrms. Institutional investors manage their portfolios based on their up-
to-date information, which potentially covers multiple themes of the 
connection between ﬁrms. Hence, compared to industry classiﬁcation 
and other static peer identiﬁcations, CIO is more comprehensive and dy-
namic. I show that stocks held by institutional investors on average have 
worse information asymmetry than stocks covered by analysts, which 
suggests  that  ownership  by  institutional  investors  is  more  attracted 
by the proﬁtability of mispriced stocks than analyst coverage. Retro-
spectively, institutional investors’ indexing and diversifying appear to 
induce less noise for peer ﬁrm identiﬁcation than analyst coverage bi-
ases.5 Lastly, institutional investors can exercise their voting rights to 
reinforce the connection among commonly owned ﬁrms.

In this study, I investigate the economic mechanism behind the grad-
ual  information  diﬀusion  among  commonly  owned  ﬁrms  from  three 
diﬀerent angles. First, I examine whether all institutional investors con-
tribute equally to the cross-predictability of stock returns; if not, I try 
to determine which institutional investors play a more essential role. 
Second, following Cohen and Frazzini (2008) and Menzly and Ozbas 
(2010), I look into changes in institutional ownership in response to 
the CIO momentum and test the hypothesis that institutional investors’ 
hold-ups of trading delay the information diﬀusion process. Lastly, I 
inspect the direct impact of institutional investors by conditioning the 
arbitrage strategy on ﬁrms’ percentage of institutional ownership.

I  ﬁnd  that  the  cross-predictability  of  commonly  owned  stocks  is 
primarily driven by institutional investors who concentrate on fewer 
stocks, under 150 in particular. Those more focused institutional in-
vestors are typically smaller asset managers who delegate more invest-
ments to ETFs and closed-end funds. The number of directly held com-
mon stocks is negatively associated with institutional investors’ active 
weight (Doshi et al., 2015), which measures the deviation of portfolio 
allocation from the market index, the industry Herﬁndahl-Hirschman 
Index (HHI), and is positively correlated with portfolio turnover, mea-
sured by the churn rate (Gaspar et al., 2005; Yan and Zhang, 2009). 
Furthermore, mutual fund managers are less focused than other insti-
tutional investors. Focused institutional investors are more likely to be 
dedicated investors, as deﬁned by Bushee and Goodman (2007), than 
transient investors and quasi-indexers.

Institutional investors as a whole increase (decrease) their owner-
ship of a ﬁrm with the presence of a good (bad) peer momentum. The 
change in institutional ownership is particularly sensitive to the mo-
mentum of other stocks that institutional investors also own, which is 
consistent with the ﬁndings in Cohen and Frazzini (2008) and Men-
zly and Ozbas (2010). However, my regression analysis based on the 
three-dimensional data structure (investor–date–stock) shows that in-
stitutional investors only trade a stock based on the momentum of peer 
stocks they also hold in their own portfolio, neglecting the peer mo-
mentum  based  on  the  common  ownership  of  the  other  institutional 
investors. The selective attention of institutional investors likely causes 
delays in the full diﬀusion of relevant information carried in the peer 
momentum.

In contrast to more focused institutional investors, I show that those 
who  hold  more  stocks  (i.e.,  less  focused)  are  shown  to  expedite  the 
information diﬀusion process among peer ﬁrms by changing their own-
ership in response to the peer momentum. I ﬁnd that the long-short 
strategy yields signiﬁcantly lower alpha from stocks with a higher per-
centage of ownership by less focused institutional investors. This ﬁnding 
reconciles this paper to prior studies that document the mitigation ef-
fect of CIO to the gradual information diﬀusion.

ings to form the CIO momentum strategy for the next quarter is that

Lastly, for robustness, I compare the baseline CIO momentum with 
several alternatives that are built with diﬀerent weighting schemes and 
measures of peer ﬁrms’ leading returns. For instance, I compare the ar-
bitrage portfolio performance using CIO momentums calculated with 
equal  and  dollar  weighting  and  gross  returns  instead  of  benchmark-
adjusted returns. A spanning test shows that the baseline CIO momen-
tum retains robust predictive power for the subsequent stock returns 
after being orthogonalized to the alternatives, but most of the alterna-
tives’ predictive power diminishes after I control for the baseline CIO 
momentum.

This  paper  makes  two  main  contributions  to  the  literature.  First, 
this is the ﬁrst study showing that common ownership by some insti-
tutional investors could delay information diﬀusion among peer ﬁrms. 
Previous studies typically consider institutional investors to be more 
attentive and better informed than retail investors, thus a higher per-
centage of institutional ownership is expected to mitigate the delays of 
pricing value-relevant information (e.g., Yan and Zhang, 2009; Baik et 
al., 2010; Bartram et al., 2015; Boone and White, 2015). Consistent with 
this argument, researchers ﬁnd common ownership by institutional in-
vestors expedites the information diﬀusion between peer stocks. For ex-
ample, Hou (2007), Menzly and Ozbas (2010), and Lee et al. (2019) ﬁnd 
a negative relationship between the magnitude of cross-predictability 
and the percentage of institutional ownership. More speciﬁcally, Cohen 
and Frazzini (2008) and Hoberg and Phillips (2018) show that com-
mon ownership of economically related ﬁrms by mutual funds expedites 
the information diﬀusion process. My ﬁndings demonstrate that only 
the less focused institutional investors (i.e., the ones diversifying with 
a larger number of stocks) mitigate the gradual information diﬀusion 
issue.  Meanwhile,  common  ownership  by  more  focused  institutional 
investors worsens gradual information diﬀusion since those investors 
delay their trading.

The second contribution of this paper is to provide new empirical 
evidence  showing  that  a  large  portion  of  institutional  investors  con-
centrate on fundamentally related ﬁrms. There is a growing body of 
literature  that  reveals  investors’  preference  and  pecuniary  incentives 
for simultaneously holding stocks that are connected by, for instance, 
industry  (e.g.,  Kacperczyk  et  al.,  2005,  2007;  Fedenia  et  al.,  2013) 
and  location  (e.g.,  Coval  and  Moskowitz,  1999,  2001;  Ivkovi´c  and 
Weisbenner, 2005; Seasholes and Zhu, 2010; Choi et al., 2017). van 
Nieuwerburgh and Veldkamp (2009, 2010) propose the information-
driven  concentrated  portfolio  theory,  which  rationalizes  institutional 
investors’ concentrating behavior. Institutional investors’ need for di-
versiﬁcation is fulﬁlled by delegating passive investments to external 
managers such as ETFs, which is consistent with the ﬁnding in DeVault 
et al. (2021).

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, I 
describe the data and methodology. In Section 3, I present the main re-
sults regarding the ﬁrms’ fundamental correlation and the stock return 
cross-predictability inferred from their CIO. In Section 4, I scrutinize 
the economic mechanism. In Section 5, I describe the robustness checks. 
Concluding remarks are in Section 6.

2.  Data and methodology

In this section, I discuss the data source and describe how the sam-
ple  of  stocks  and  institutional  investors  are  selected.  Additionally,  I 
introduce a new measure of common ownership, which will serve as 
a weighting scheme to calculate the CIO-linked peer trend and the CIO 
momentum.

2.1.  Data and sample

I merge historical stock data from the Center for Research in Se-
curity  Prices  (CRSP)  with  ﬁnancial  statement  data  from  Compustat. 
The Form 13F institutional investors holdings data comes from Reﬁni-
tiv/Thomson Reuters. I extract analysts’ coverage and forecast informa-
tion from the Institutional Brokers Estimate System (IBES) maintained 
by Reﬁnitiv/Thomson Reuters. I download the asset-pricing factors and 
Fama-French industry deﬁnitions from Kenneth French’s online data li-
brary.6 The text-based industry classiﬁcation comes from the Hoberg 
and Phillips Data Library.7 I download the institutional investor clas-
siﬁcation from Brian Bushee’s personal website.8 I identify the mutual 
fund managers using the s12type5 ﬁle from the mutual fund holdings 
data provided by Inﬁnitiv/Thomson Reuters. I download the data of 
the Probability of Informed Trading (PIN) computed using the Venter 
and DeJong model from Stephen Brown’s website.9

I  examine  institutional  holdings  from  March  1980  to  December 
2017.  institutional  investors  with  less  than  two  valid  common  stock 
holdings are excluded from the sample. I sample ﬁrms monthly from 
April 1980 to March 2018 since I ﬁrst estimate the return predictor at 
the end of April 1980 based on the previous quarter-end institutional 
holdings. The ﬁrm sample is restricted to the U.S.-based issuers of com-
mon stocks (with share codes 10 and 11) that are traded on the NYSE, 
NYSE MKT (formerly AMEX), and NASDAQ, and held by at least one 
institutional investor. Stocks are removed from the sample in the re-
gression analyses if their previous month-end price is missing or less 
than $5 or their book value of equity is missing from Compustat. In all 
the portfolio tests, I further exclude the micro-cap and nano-cap stocks 
that are in the bottom (10%) of the NYSE breakpoints.

2.2.  Measuring common ownership

In this study, I use the common ownership of two ﬁrms by insti-
tutional  investors  to  infer  the  fundamental  correlation  of  these  two 
ﬁrms. van Nieuwerburgh and Veldkamp (2009, 2010) theorize that it 
is optimal for investors to hold a concentrated portfolio that consists of 
ﬁrms whose value-relevant information the investors have a compara-
tive advantage over acquiring and processing. Due to the information 
constraints and learning costs, only ﬁrms with similar fundamentals are 
included in the concentrated portfolio. Depending on the speciﬁc in-
formation advantage, an investor’s concentrated portfolios may include 
ﬁrms that are connected in various ways, such as industry membership, 
supplier-customer  relation,  geographic  proximity,  etc.  Therefore,  the 
common ownership aggregated across all of the concentrated portfolios 
should represent a relationship network of ﬁrms from the investors’ per-
spectives.10 Further, the extent of similarity in fundamentals between 
two ﬁrms should increase with the number of their common owners. To 
mitigate the measurement noises from institutional investors’ practice 
of indexing and diversiﬁcation, which makes institutional investors si-
multaneously hold fundamentally unrelated ﬁrms, I assign more weight 
to the common ownership by institutional investors who focus on fewer 
common stocks. In Section 5, I inspect several alternative common own-
ership measures that assign weight to institutional investors based on 
the dollar value they hold, the under-/over-weighting relative to the 
market index portfolio, and the past trading direction.

The  example  above  can  also  be  used  to  illustrate  the  diﬀerences 
between my common ownership measure and some others in the liter-
ature. Anton and Polk (2014, p. 6) consider two stocks having greater 
common ownership if the common investors own a higher percentage 
of the total market value of both stocks. Consider the case in which ﬁrm 
D has the largest market value and is held almost entirely by investor 
2 while the other three ﬁrms have much smaller market value and are 
equally held by their shareholders. Firm A thus has the greatest com-
mon ownership with ﬁrm D. Bartram et al. (2015, Eqns. 10 and 11)
measure the common ownership by calculating the scaled Manhattan 
distance between both ﬁrms’ institutional investors and then weighting 
it by their relative institutional holdings in dollars. According to the au-
thors, ﬁrm C would be most connected to ﬁrm A if investor 2 holds most

of ﬁrm A and puts more portfolio weight on ﬁrm C than she does on 
ﬁrm D. Gao et al. (2017) simply counts the number of common institu-
tional investors, which results in the same extent of common ownership 
of ﬁrm B and ﬁrm C with ﬁrm A.

I argue that my common ownership measure is more appropriate 
for studying institutional investors’ inﬂuence on information diﬀusion 
among connected ﬁrms for the following reasons. First, my measure 
mitigates  the  adverse  eﬀect  of  institutional  investors’  incentives  for 
diversiﬁcation. Second, the inferred connection does not depend on in-
stitutional portfolio weights and the market value of commonly owned 
shares, thus is less impacted by contagion-related factors, such as exoge-
nous fund ﬂow shocks and portfolio rebalancing. Lastly, the asymmetry 
of the inferred connections better resembles reality.

3.  Main results

In this section, I present the main ﬁndings of the study. Firstly, I 
examine the fundamental connection between commonly owned ﬁrms 
using panel regressions with ﬁxed eﬀects. After that, I investigate the 
performance  of  a  CIO-based  peer  momentum  strategy  to  determine 
whether  cross-predictability  exists.  Finally,  I  compare  the  predictive 
power of CIO momentum with other peer momentums, speciﬁcally Ali 
and Hirshleifer’s (2020) shared-analyst momentum.

3.1.  Firms’ fundamental connection

Despite  being  supported  by  the  theoretical  developments  in  van 
Nieuwerburgh and Veldkamp (2009, 2010), it is not immediately clear 
how well the CIO resembles ﬁrms’ fundamental connection. Neither can 
the literature conclude whether the documented portfolio concentration 
is driven by institutional investors’ information advantage or some sys-
tematic behavioral bias. Korniotis and Kumar (2013) explore the same 
question with a sample of retail investors and ﬁnd the existence of both 
driving forces.

To empirically verify the fundamental connection among commonly 
owned ﬁrms, I run both a contemporaneous and a predictive regres-
sion of a series of ﬁrms’ annual ﬁnancial statement variables on their 
respective CIO-linked trends, the estimation procedure is described in 
Section 2.3. I focus on the growth of four key fundamental indicators: 
sales, gross proﬁt, R&D expenses, and total assets. All of the regressions 
include the growth variables to avoid the persistence issue of the level 
variables.

I also regress annual fundamental growth onto two industry trends 
and Ali and Hirshleifer’s (2020) analyst-linked trend. The ﬁrst industry 
trend is based on Fama-French 48 industry classiﬁcation and the sec-
ond one is based on Hoberg and Phillips’s (2016) text-based industry 
classiﬁcation. The Fama-French 48 industry trend is the equal-weighted 
average of the fundamental growth of ﬁrms in the same industries ex-
cluding the focal ﬁrm. The Hoberg-Phillips text-based industry trend 
uses the similarity scores between two ﬁrms as the weight of the con-
nection. Following Ali and Hirshleifer (2020), the analyst-linked trend 
is the weighted average of peer ﬁrms’ fundamental growth with the 
weight being the number of analysts who issue at least one FY1 or FY2 
earnings forecast for both the focal ﬁrm and the peer ﬁrms in the same 
year. Due to the data availability, the text-based industry trend starts in 
1989 and the analyst-linked trend starts in 1984.

The results are reported in Table 2. I ﬁnd that the CIO-linked trend 
signiﬁcantly correlates with the focal ﬁrm for all four indicators of fun-
damental growth. For example, as shown in column (1) of Panel A, a 1% 
increase in the weighted average sales growth of the CIO-linked peers 
is associated with a 3.75% increase in the focal ﬁrm’s sales growth. The 
positive  association  remains  signiﬁcant  after  I  control  for  the  indus-
try trends and analyst-linked trend. Columns (3) and (4) of each panel 
report the results from the predictive regressions. I ﬁnd that the CIO-
linked trend is the only one that robustly predicts the future growth of 
the focal ﬁrm’s sales, gross proﬁt, R&D expenses, and total assets.

The contemporaneous and predictive regression results suggest the 
following: (1) the CIO contains important information about ﬁrms’ fun-
damental connection that is not revealed in the industry membership 
and shared coverage by analysts; and (2) the CIO maps the network for 
the transmission of value-relevant shocks among the connected ﬁrms. 
Note that the strong predictability of ﬁrms’ future fundamental growth 
by the CIO-linked trend could be either endogenous due to institutional 
investors’ tendency to hold connected ﬁrms or exogenous due to the 
enhanced cross-ﬁrm collaboration induced by common ownership. My 
objective is not to distinguish these two possibilities. Both cases work 
in favor of using common ownership to infer the connection of ﬁrms.

3.2.1.  Baseline portfolio tests

I sort and group ﬁrms into quintiles at the beginning of each month 
based on their past CIO momentum estimated from the previous month. 
The top quintile group consists of the ﬁrms with the highest past CIO 
momentum and the bottom quintile group has the lowest past CIO mo-
mentum. I then construct either an equal-weighted or a value-weighted 
stock portfolio using the ﬁrms in each quintile. In addition, I form a self-
ﬁnancing arbitrage portfolio by long holding the top-quintile ﬁrms and 
short selling the bottom-quintile ﬁrms. All six portfolios are regrouped 
and rebalanced monthly. Each month, there are an average of 440 ﬁrms 
in each quintile portfolio over the formation period from April 1980 to 
March 2018.

I evaluate portfolio performance using the portfolio’s excess returns 
relative to the U.S. 3-month Treasury-bill rate, as well as the alphas 
from a batch of asset pricing factor models: CAPM, Fama and French’s 
(1993) three-factor model, Carhart’s (1997) four-factor model with and 
without short-term reversal factor, and Fama and French’s (2015) ﬁve-
factor model.

The results in Table 3 show that both the excess return and alphas 
demonstrate  that  the  ﬁrms  with  higher  past  CIO  momentum  signiﬁ-
cantly outperform the ﬁrms with lower past CIO momentum during the 
next month. The long-short value-weighted equity portfolio generates a 
monthly alpha of over 100 bps across all the asset pricing factor mod-

els. I ﬁnd that the sizable risk-adjusted returns are not solely driven by 
the short-sale constraint as the long legs already generate monthly al-
phas that are over 0.5%. The negligible diﬀerence in returns between 
the value-weighted and the equal-weighted portfolios suggests that the 
portfolio proﬁts I document are not biased by the inclusion of small-cap 
ﬁrms.

Anton  and  Polk  (2014) and  Gao  et  al.  (2017) ﬁnd  that  CIO  can 
cause the excessive comovement of the stock prices of the commonly 
owned ﬁrms due to the exogenous fund ﬂow shocks or portfolio reallo-
cation, followed by signiﬁcant price reversals. To make sure the alphas 
generated by the CIO momentum strategy are not transient contagion-
induced  mispricing,  I  track  the  cumulative  returns  of  the  long-short 
portfolios throughout the 12-month period after the initial formation. 
Fig. 4 illustrates that both the equal- and value-weighted portfolios con-
tinue  to  generate  upward  drifting  returns—the  one-year  cumulative 
return  is  between  2.5%  and  3%.  This  conﬁrms  that  the  return  pre-
dictability using the CIO momentum reﬂects the gradual diﬀusion of 
value-relevant information.

3.2.4.  Comparison to shared-analyst coverage

CIO is related to Ali and Hirshleifer’s (2020) shared-analyst cover-
age. These authors consider that ﬁrms are fundamentally related if they 
are followed by the same group of sell-side analysts. Both Ali and Hirsh-
leifer (2020) and this study implicitly identify the fundamentally related 
stocks through decisions made by investment professionals. There are 
two major diﬀerences in the motivation behind the decisions: (1) unlike 
institutional investors, analysts have little to no incentive to diversify 
the group of ﬁrms they cover; and (2) institutional investors have more 
straightforward pecuniary interests in the stocks they hold than ana-
lysts.

I ﬁrst examine the correlation between the peer momentums derived 
from CIO and shared-analyst coverage. Panel A of Table 6 illustrates 
the  correlation  between  CIO  momentum  and  shared-analyst  momen-

double-sorted portfolios. If CIO momentum and shared-analyst momen-
tum are perfectly correlated, all the stocks would be evenly sorted in the 
diagonal portfolios. There are noticeable discrepancies between the pre-
dictions of future stock performance based on non-extreme CIO momen-
tum and shared-analyst momentum, despite both being largely similar 

in the top and the bottom quintiles. I show the accuracy of those pre-
dictions by estimating the 4-factor alpha of the 25 portfolios. In Panel 
B, the alphas suggest that CIO momentum and shared-analyst momen-
tum have comparable predictive power for the worst-performing stocks; 
however, the CIO momentum is more accurate in predicting the best-
performing stocks; the shared-analyst momentum fails to predict any 
best-performing stocks except for those the CIO momentum identiﬁes 
as best performers. Panel C presents the results of conditional double 
sorting, in which sequential sorting is done within each initially sorted 
bin of stocks. The results are consistent with the ﬁndings from the un-
conditional double sorting in Panel B.

I next scrutinize whether and how the peer momentum eﬀect (i.e., 
stock return cross-predictability) changes conditional on diﬀerent ﬁrm 
characteristics, such as size, age, etc. To do so, I examine the perfor-
mance of the long-short arbitrage strategy within each quintile port-
folio sorted by the tested ﬁrm characteristic. As shown in Fig. 6, the 
4-factor alpha from the shared-analyst momentum arbitrage declines 
signiﬁcantly  with  ﬁrm  size  and  age,  and  grows  with  stock  volatility 
and extent of information asymmetry. Meanwhile, the 4-factor alpha 
from the CIO momentum arbitrage remains consistent across the infor-
mation asymmetry that surrounds the stock. These contrasting results 
suggest the diﬀerence in the frictions that cause the delayed diﬀusion 
of information implicated in CIO and shared-analyst momentums. The 
CIO  momentum  eﬀect  declines  with  institutional  ownership,  but  not 
with analyst coverage, which suggests that the friction leading to the 
CIO momentum eﬀect is more internally related to the institutional in-
vestors than other external information constraints.

By the construction of CIO momentum, institutional investors with 
fewer holdings are given more weight for the count of common own-
ership. Firms commonly owned by those investors are more likely to 
be fundamentally related, thus their stock returns contribute more to 
the CIO momentum. I identify which institutional investors essentially 
drive the CIO momentum eﬀect and their motivation behind holding a 
concentrated portfolio.

First, I employ a step-wise procedure to examine whether the num-
ber of holdings in institutional portfolios is relevant to the return pre-
diction of the CIO momentum. I repeat the long-short arbitrage strategy 
described  in  Section 3.2.1 using  stocks  commonly  owned  by  institu-
tional  investors  who  hold  less  than  20,  40,  60,  ...,  or  500  common 
stocks, respectively, and document the Carhart 4-factor (FF3M) alphas. 
As shown in Panel A of Fig. 7, the FF3M alpha peaks after I include the 
institutional investors who hold less than 150 common stocks. Common 
ownership by institutional investors who hold more than 150 stocks 
contributes little to the performance of the arbitrage portfolio. Exclud-
ing these more focused institutional investors gradually weakens the 
arbitrage performance. Panel B illustrates that the 4-factor alpha de-
clines noticeably after excluding the common ownership of institutional 
investors who hold less than 150 stocks. These patterns reveal that the 
previously documented CIO momentum eﬀect is primarily driven by the 
more focused institutional investors who invest in fewer stocks.

To identify the focused institutional investors, I regress the log of 
the number of common stocks they hold onto their portfolio size, in-
vestment  delegation,  active  asset  allocation,  industry  concentration, 
portfolio turnover, and other identiﬁcation categories. The results are 
in Table 7. After controlling for both institutional investor and quarter 
ﬁxed eﬀects, I ﬁnd the number of common stocks held by institutional 
investors is positively associated with portfolio size, as measured by the 
market value, indicating that the more focused institutional investors 
are more likely to have smaller assets under management (AUM) and 
be more constrained by capital and other resources. Columns (2) and (3) 
show that the more focused institutional investors delegate more funds 
to external managers, such as ETFs and CEFs. This result is consistent 
with institutional investors’ demand for diversiﬁcation and benchmark 
tracking.  Institutional  investors  can  more  closely  follow  fewer  ﬁrms 
when their diversiﬁed portfolios are externally managed. The results 
in column (4)–(6) demonstrate that the more focused institutional in-
vestors are likely more strategic with their asset allocations, as mea-
sured  by  the  deviation  of  their  portfolio  weighting  from  the  market 
portfolio following Doshi et al.’s (2015) active weight, maintain higher 
industry concentration, as measured by the Herﬁndahl-Hirschman In-
dex  (HHI),  and  have  lower  portfolio  turnover,  as  measured  by  the 
churn rate following Yan and Zhang (2009). In particular, the positive 
correlation between the number of institutional holdings and portfo-
lio turnover suggests that the delays in information diﬀusion among 
stocks  commonly  owned  by  the  more  focused  institutional  investors 
may be due to the lack of active trading. Column (7) shows that mutual 
funds hold more stocks than other institutional investors, and column

to be dedicated investors than quasi-indexers and transient investors.

4.2.  Institutional trading and peer momentum

Both  Cohen  and  Frazzini  (2008) and  Hoberg  and  Phillips  (2018)
argue  that  institutional  investors  expedite  the  information  diﬀusion 
process by trading in the same direction as predicted by the peer mo-
mentum. To reconcile with these prior ﬁndings, I examine the trading 
pattern of institutional investors in response to the industry peer mo-
mentums with and without common ownership of peer stocks.

For  institutional  investors  to  accelerate  the  information  diﬀusion 
among peer ﬁrms, they need to evaluate the contemporaneous news of 
the peer ﬁrms, especially when they also hold stock in the peer ﬁrms. I 
test this hypothesis by regressing the split-adjusted quarterly change in 
percentage ownership by the institutional investors who hold the focal 
ﬁrm at the beginning of the quarter onto the cumulative momentums of 
the entire industry and the momentums of the industry peers that these 
investors simultaneously hold.

To estimate the momentum of the commonly owned industry peers, 
I follow the same procedure as for the CIO momentum described in Sub-
section 2.3, except for using the gross returns of the ﬁrms in the same 
industry deﬁned by the Fama-French 48 industry classiﬁcation or the 
3-digit SIC code. I include the lagged momentums in the regressions to 
control for institutional investors’ momentum chasing behaviors. Since 
the institutional holdings are disclosed quarterly while the peer mo-
mentum eﬀect is documented on a monthly basis, I estimate the lagged 
momentum by compounding the monthly momentum from the third 
month of the previous quarter to the second month of the current quar-
ter. I also include the focal ﬁrm’s quarterly compounded gross return 
and its lagged gross return to account for the ownership changes due to 
the focal ﬁrm’s own news, as well as the ﬁrm and quarter ﬁxed eﬀects. 
I cluster the standard errors by both the ﬁrm and quarter.

The results are in Panel A of Table 8. Consistent with the ﬁndings 
in Cohen and Frazzini (2008) and Hoberg and Phillips (2018), institu-
tional investors, as a group, increase (decrease) their holdings of a ﬁrm 
when the ﬁrm’s industry peers are under positive (negative) valuation 
shocks. The ownership changes respond to the momentum of industry 
peers that institutional investors commonly hold, instead of the entire 
industry, as the coeﬃcients of the equal- or value-weighted industry 
momentum become insigniﬁcant once I include the commonly owned 
industry momentums in the regressions. These ﬁndings suggest that in-
stitutional investors trade on the news of the industry peer ﬁrms only 
if they hold them. The negative coeﬃcient of the lagged Fama-French 
48 industry momentum indicates that retail investors tend to follow the 
more coarsely deﬁned industry momentum.

If institutional investors unanimously trade in the same direction 
as predicted by commonly owned peer momentum, the return cross-
predictability  should  be  eliminated,  unless  some  frictions  cause  dis-
agreements among institutional investors. To further examine how each 
institutional investor responds to the news of commonly held industry 
peers, I run a three-dimensional panel regression at the investor-stock-
quarter level. The dependent variable is the change in percentage own-
ership of stock  𝑖 by institutional investor  𝑗 over quarter  𝑞. I regress 
the individual ownership changes onto the momentum of stock 𝑖’s in-

momentum) and the momentum of stock 𝑖’s industry peers that are com-
monly held by all other institutional investors (i.e., external CO-industry 
momentum). The separation of internal and external CO-industry mo-
mentums helps to identify what peer news elicits a response from insti-
tutional investors. In the three-dimensional panel regression, I control 
for investor-quarter ﬁxed eﬀects and stock-quarter eﬀects, in which the

5.  Robustness checks

In this section, I compare the return predictability by the baseline 
CIO momentum with several alternatives of the estimated CIO momen-
tum. I run a similar spanning test as in Section 3.2.3, which orthogonal-
izes the baseline CIO momentum and the alternative CIO momentums 
onto each other. I then compare the risk-adjusted returns of the long-
short value-weighted portfolio formed with the orthogonal residuals.

One major diﬀerence between the CIO momentum in this study and 
the other documented peer momentum, such as industry peer momen-
tum (Hou, 2007) and shared-analyst momentum (Ali and Hirshleifer, 
2020), is the measurement of peer returns. Most previous papers use 
the gross monthly returns while I use the benchmark-adjusted DGTW 
returns to alleviate the noises from institutional investors’ index invest-
ing and the potential alpha attenuation problem with the gross return, 
which is pointed out by Burt and Hrdlicka (2021). Nevertheless, I cre-
ate an alternative CIO momentum, ALT1, using the baseline weighting 
scheme and the gross returns.

I estimate a second set of alternative CIO momentums, ALT2 and 
ALT3, by assigning equal weights to all the institutional investors when 
measuring the common ownership and using the benchmark-adjusted 
DGTW return and gross return, respectively. With equal weights, two 
ﬁrms are considered to share more common ownership when there are

6.  Conclusion

Using SEC 13(f) ﬁlings between 1980 and 2017, I ﬁnd that ﬁrms 
with CIO share similar fundamentals, such as annual growth in sales, 
proﬁts, R&D expenses, and total assets. Institutional investors tend to 
concentrate their portfolios, which results in this similarity. It is more 
cost-eﬀective for them to delegate the diversiﬁed portfolio to external 
asset managers, rather than cover more market sectors themselves. Dur-
ing the sample period, a quarter of institutional investors held under 20 
domestic common stocks, while another quarter held more than 140.

Previous studies suggested that CIO expedites the dissemination of 
information across fundamentally related ﬁrms, but my research indi-
cates that the gradual information diﬀusion issue persists among ﬁrms 
that are commonly owned by institutional investors. I show that the 
stock  return  cross-predictability  is  more  prevalent  among  commonly 
owned ﬁrms than ﬁrms in the same industry and ﬁrms covered by the 
same sell-side analysts. The CIO momentum strategy outperforms the 
industry momentum strategy and Ali and Hirshleifer’s (2020) shared-
analyst momentum strategy.

The gradual information diﬀusion among commonly owned ﬁrms 
is primarily driven by more focused institutional investors who con-
centrate on fewer ﬁrms and delegate more funds to external managers. 
Common ownership by more focused institutional investors delays the 
information diﬀusion among peer ﬁrms by delaying trading in response 
to  the  peer  momentum.  Less  focused  institutional  investors  actively 
trade on the CIO momentum; however, their selective attention toward 
the fundamentally related ﬁrms within their portfolio fails to eliminate 
the CIO momentum eﬀect.

My results have the following implications for investors and policy-
makers: (1) investors could miss investment opportunities if they ne-
glect the fundamental similarity of the commonly owned ﬁrms; (2) the 
passive investment strategy employed by some institutional investors 
who concentrate on fewer ﬁrms may impact market eﬃciency. For fu-
