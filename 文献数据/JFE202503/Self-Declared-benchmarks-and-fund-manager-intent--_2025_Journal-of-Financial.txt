Using a panel of self-declared benchmarks, we examine funds’ use of mismatched benchmarks over time. Mis-
matching is high at the beginning of our sample (45 % of TNA in 2008), consistent with prior studies, but de-
clines significantly over time (27 % in 2020), driven by existing specialized funds changing benchmarks to match
their style. Market forces including investor learning, institutional governance, market competition, and product
positioning all play a role in benchmark correction decisions. For funds with difficult to categorize investment
strategies, mismatched benchmarks are less associated with performance bias. Our study highlights the value of
market solutions in aligning manager-investor interests.

1. Introduction

Relative performance evaluation (RPE) is a theoretically-motivated
solution to moral hazard that is commonplace in modern incentive
contracts.1 Both firm executives and fund managers typically have
bonus compensation based on performance relative to a group of peer
firms, peer funds, or other comparable benchmarks.2 At the same time, a
growing body of empirical evidence indicates that the incentivization
tools based on RPE are limited in their efficacy by executive or mana-
gerial influence. The incentivized parties often have sway over the

selection of the comparable sets. In the case of CEOs, while compensa-
tion committees often hold the responsibility for defining the peer
group, the choice of these peers is likely to be influenced by CEOs (Bizjak
et al., 2008), especially when relative power of the CEO is high (see,
also, Dikolli, Diser, Hofmann, and Pfeiffer, 2018). In the case of mutual
fund managers, Sensoy (2009) finds that 40.4 % of U.S. equity funds
select incorrect benchmarks which overstate their relative performance,
resulting in higher flows. Similarly, Cremers, Fulkerson, and Riley
(2022) document that 24 % of funds have a high degree of benchmark
the

In this paper, we explore both the nature of these distortions and the
economic forces that alleviate them by analyzing the evolution of fund
benchmarks. In contrast to the 2004 cross-section used in Sensoy (2009),
our dataset consists of multiple snapshots of U.S. equity fund bench-
marks collected between 2008 and 2020. Using the “mismatched
benchmark” Morningstar definition from Sensoy (2009), we find
consistent results that at the beginning of our sample, 34.2 % of funds
(45.4 % of fund assets) have mismatched benchmarks. However, over
our sample period, the percentages of mismatched funds (assets)
decreased steadily to 27.2 % (27.8 %). Moreover, the fraction of fund
assets benchmarked to the S&P 500 decreased from 52.4 % to 38.7 %.
The decrease in mismatched benchmarks is largely driven by the
intensive margin (i.e., changes in benchmarks for existing funds), not
the extensive margin (i.e., opening of matched funds and closing of
mismatched funds). Almost 60 % of funds with mismatched benchmarks
in 2008 that survive to 2020 switched to a matched benchmark over the
course of our sample. Formally, a linear model of the trend in mis-
matched benchmarks demonstrates that the equal-weighted likelihood
of mismatch decreases by 0.762 % per year within our sample period
(2008 to 2020).

Correctly interpreting the frequency and the trend in mismatching
requires accounting for the breadth of the fund’s investment strategy.
While Holmstr¨om (1982) assumes the presence of a perfectly matched
and sufficiently diversified RPE peer comparison set, such a peer group
may not exist.3 Because benchmarks are often defined within an in-
vestment category (e.g., Russell Mid Cap Growth), funds whose strategy
extends beyond a single category may have difficulty identifying a
“matched” benchmark. In contrast to the prior CEO and mutual fund
literature which suggests that such mismatching is consistent with
strategic distortions (or put more plainly, “cheating”) on the part of the
CEO/fund manager (e.g., the fund exhibits a positive bias relative to the
benchmark, see Garvey and Milbourn (2006)), this alternative expla-
nation suggests that the selection of such benchmarks is related to risk,
not performance.

To capture funds whose investment approach extends beyond an
easily categorized style, we calculate the degree to which a fund man-
ager invests outside her core investment objectives. This new measure,
“Investment Strategy Breadth”, or IS Breadth, is the percentage of a given
fund’s holdings outside its Morningstar assigned investment objective
category. We further demonstrate that IS Breadth exhibits a positive
correlation with other approximations for portfolio breadth and
flexibility.

The IS Breadth measure contributes to explaining both the trends and
the levels of mismatched benchmarks. First, when we allow the linear
trends to depend on the levels of IS Breadth, the rate of decline in mis-
matched benchmarks is 1.06 % per year for funds with IS Breadth one
standard deviation (SD) below the mean (the more specialized funds),
and 0.482 % for those with IS Breadth one SD above the mean (funds
with a broader investment strategy). This trend differential suggests the
potential for economic forces leading to more accurate benchmarks for
specialized funds, while broad funds are less likely to make “corrections”
due to the unavailability of appropriate benchmarks.

Second, we find that specialized funds with mismatched benchmarks
exhibit higher return bias than broad funds do: Their chosen benchmark

We examine potential economic forces that may explain the correc-

then go beyond this agency setting and consider product market

product features. Within these channel analyses, we separately account
for IS Breadth, reiterating the critical role of strategy breadth in the
effectiveness of RPE and funds’ responses to the economic factors.

Investor learning is the idea that as investors become more finan-
cially sophisticated, they are better able to assess both the correct
benchmark for the fund and the potential efforts of the manager to
obfuscate. How do investors learn? One possibility is that in competitive
markets, rival firms will educate consumers about potential obfuscation
to win over educated customers (Shapiro, 1995).4 The market for
investor education has been evolving. First, passive advisors such as
from
Blackrock, State Street, and Vanguard, who would benefit
debiasing, have invested heavily in investor education,
including
benchmarking and performance measurement.5 Second, third-party in-
formation providers such as Morningstar and Lipper often provide a
fund performance comparison to that of the average of the portfolio
category, which approximates for a “matched” benchmark. Third, the
rise of fiduciary financial advisors can steer investors towards more
transparent products. Together, these educational efforts may result in
more sophisticated investors, driving the shift in actively managed
firms’ behavior away from mismatched and towards correctly matched
benchmarks, because efforts to obfuscate are less effective. Yet, in the
case of broadly invested funds, whose initial mismatched benchmarks
may not be chosen for obfuscation, these efforts will likely not affect the
incidence of mismatch.

In examining investor flows before and after the change of bench-
marks, we show that for specialized funds, not only do the average in-
vestors assess manager performance relative to the correct benchmark
even before the switch, but that they appear to only respond to the new
benchmark if it is appropriate to the fund’s investment style. Though
this level of investor sophistication may be surprising for the literature,
it is consistent with the intensive investor education initiatives described
above. For broad funds, in contrast, while we find evidence of investors
responding both to the new and old benchmarks, we find no change in
flow sensitivity even after a switch to a matched benchmark. In response
to such investor learning, specialized funds are more likely to switch to

After examining the investor learning and monitoring channels, we
turn to an important factor in competitive equilibria – product differ-
entiation.6 Considering benchmarks as conveying information on the
differentiation of fund objectives, we find that as competition in a given
benchmark category increases (as measured by the number and market
share of index fund competitors), funds, especially those with special-
ized portfolios, are more likely to switch to benchmarks that match their
portfolio styles.7

In addition to the direct measures of price competition, we also
examine the product characteristics upon which the prior literature has
shown investors base their investment decisions. Sensoy (2009) has
shown, and we confirm in our sample, that investor flows are sensitive to
the performance of the fund relative to its benchmark. Additionally, the
prior literature has shown that more sophisticated investors are sensitive
to fund relative risk/tracking error (e.g., James and Karceski (2006), Del
Guercio and Tkac (2008)). Separately, from an RPE perspective, a lower
tracking error can help hedge risk for the risk-averse manager (Gibbons
and Murphy (1990)).8 We find that funds are more likely to switch to a
mismatched benchmark when relative performance improves, and more
likely to make a correction when tracking errors are reduced. In a related
paper by Mullaly and Rossi (2022), the authors find evidence consistent
with funds changing their benchmarks to look better in relative per-
formance terms. In contrast, we show that the economic channels of risk

In addition to exploring the application and effectiveness of RPE, our
study contributes to two strands of literature. First, the paper comple-
ments existing studies which highlight intermediaries as information
providers that guide retail investor decisions, and particularly those
demonstrating the improvement in the quality of such information
provision. On this topic, Blume (1998) underscores the importance of
selecting appropriate peer groups for evaluating mutual fund perfor-
mance, and Evans and Sun (2021) show a major refinement in the peer
groups used by Morningstar in 2002 has led to more sophisticated
investor risk adjustment. Our study, in contrast, concentrates on the
enhanced quality of self-declared benchmarks of mutual funds which
has occurred gradually over time. The similarity is that both the rating
change and the benchmark switches we document in this paper are
partially prompted by the demand of (relatively sophisticated) investors.
Second, while performance benchmarks can result in more informed
fund choices by retail investors and better alignment of fund manager
incentives, the broader literature shows that they can further influence
the asset demand by (incentivized) fund managers and ultimately asset
prices. Recent studies, for example by Buffa and Hodor (2022); Pavlova
and Sikorskaya (2023); Kashyap, Kovrijnykh, Li, and Pavlova (2023);
and Kim (2024) document the distortions caused by inelastic and
correlated demand that originates from benchmarking practices.
Further, Han, Roussanov, and Ruan (2022) shows that the risk-taking
incentives by fund managers who lag their benchmarks lead to risk
anomalies. Thus, if the trend we document in this paper continues,
distortions caused by the largest benchmarks, such as the S&P 500, may
continue to decline, but demand associated with more differentiated
benchmarks may create new return anomalies.

the data sample. Section 3 introduces the IS Breadth measure. Sections 4

2. Data

2.1. Databases and sample construction

Motivated by a desire to enhance investors’ ability to evaluate fund
performance, the SEC in 1993 began requiring mutual funds to select a
“broad-based securities market index” and report graphically the per-
formance of both the fund and its benchmark to enable investor com-
parisons.9 These performance comparisons are reported in each fund’s
annual prospectus and annual reports, and the choice of a performance
benchmark can change from report to report.

In order to capture the evolution of self-declared benchmarks over
time, our analysis uses snapshots of the Morningstar database collected
between December 2008 and December 2020. These snapshots contain
the listings of self-designated prospectus benchmarks of equity mutual
funds operating in the United States and the contemporaneous index
characteristics. We match these snapshots to the fiscal reporting dates of
our mutual fund sample to obtain the dates on which the benchmark

2.2.1. Definition of mismatched benchmarks

Following the method in Sensoy (2009), we consider an equity fund
to be mismatched if its self-designated prospectus benchmark has a
different equity style than the fund’s Morningstar Category. Specifically,
during our sample period, Morningstar Categories correspond to the
3-by-3 holdings-implied style-boxes that describe a fund or a bench-
mark’s size focus (Large Cap, Medium Cap, and Small Cap) and invest-
ment styles (Growth, Blend, and Value). If a fund had designated the
S&P 500 - a Large Cap Blend index- as its prospectus benchmark but was
categorized as a Large Cap Value fund by its holdings, we code this case
as a Benchmark Mismatch. This classification may be sensitive to the
boundaries of style boxes set by Morningstar; therefore, we also inves-
tigate the robustness of our findings using continuous measures of
benchmark mismatch (see Appendix B).

Panel B of Table 2 describes equity funds by the styles of both their
holdings and their benchmarks. Each column represents a different
holding-implied equity style, while each row represents a benchmark
style. The top of this panel divides up the sample using size target, while
the bottom does this using growth-value orientation. Funds with Large
Cap stock holdings use the most size-style appropriate benchmarks with
over 99 % of the fund-month observations having a benchmark that
indicated a Large/Broad size target. This consistency deteriorates in the
Small Cap and Mid Cap categories- with 4 % of the Small Cap and 25 %
of the Mid Cap funds using some type of a Large/Broad Cap benchmark.
In the growth-value dimension, there is a greater likelihood of mismatch
between funds and their benchmarks. The most severe disagreement
occurs in the Growth and Value fund categories: 41 % of Growth and 32
% of Value fund-time observations use a Blend benchmark.

2.2.2. Primary vs. secondary benchmarks

Mutual funds sometimes display their returns in comparison to more
than one benchmark index. Morningstar reports a “primary prospectus
benchmark” for all funds and a “secondary prospectus benchmark”
when applicable. This designation of “primary” and “secondary” is made
by the investment advisor and communicated to Morningstar.

Our analysis focuses on the primary benchmarks for four reasons.

First, while the fund may include a secondary benchmark in its pro-
spectus, the primary benchmark is more visible to investors. For the
majority of our sample period, Morningstar only included the primary
prospectus benchmark in its default fund performance information.11
Similarly, in examining media mentions, third-party reporting and the
marketing materials of several funds, we found that often the relative
performance display only included the primary benchmark index (see
Appendix C for several examples). Second, because the primary bench-
mark is self-designated by the fund to Morningstar, it is likely the more
important benchmark (usually the benchmark disclosed right after fund
performance and before other benchmarks), thus it can be more

important for the fund’s own marketing purpose and is more likely to
influence investor behavior. Third, there is likely to be a mechanical
negative relationship between the matched/mismatched designation of
primary and secondary benchmarks. Funds conceivably choose multiple
benchmarks to provide different metrics of their risk exposure. As a
result, there is little rationale for choosing two highly correlated
benchmarks (e.g., S&P 500 and Russell 1000). Instead, when the pri-
mary benchmark becomes more matched over time, the secondary
benchmark may mechanically become more mismatched over time.
Fourth, using only the primary benchmark creates a more robust mea-
sure of mismatching. For funds with multiple benchmarks, the incidence
of mismatching can be maximized (minimized) if a fund is considered to
be mismatched when only one benchmark (both benchmarks) is (are)
mismatched. Instead, we focus on the economically most relevant
benchmark.

3. Investment strategy breadth (IS Breadth)

In the principal-agent setting proposed by Holmstr¨om (1982), a
given agent is judged relative to a set of appropriate peers, such that as
the number of peers increases, firm-specific risk is diversified away and
the peer set then perfectly reflects and (via RPE) hedges the common risk
faced by each agent. In practice, finding a sufficiently large set of
appropriate peers may not be so simple. For mutual funds, this issue may
be magnified in that many benchmarks are designed to reflect the eq-
uities and risk within a given investment objective. For funds whose
strategy extends beyond a single category, identifying an appropriate
benchmark that reflects the common risk experienced by the manager is
more difficult. Because this key difference in investment strategy may
affect the economics underlying the choice of a mismatched benchmark,
we propose, validate, and then utilize a novel measure of the breadth of
an investment strategy: IS Breadth.

3.1. IS Breadth measure construction

In the simplest terms, investment strategy breadth is the degree to
which a fund manager invests beyond her core investment objective. We
rely on Morningstar’s split of the US equity mutual fund universe (and
equity universe as well) into nine categories based on the market capi-
talization (i.e., small-, mid-, and large-cap) and relative valuation (e.g.,
book-to-market) of their underlying holdings.12 We use the Morning-
star’s designation of each fund’s core investment objective category and
then sum the percentage of the fund’s holdings that fall in the other eight
style boxes to create a raw measure. To facilitate the interpretation of
the coefficients on the variable later in the analysis, we standardize IS
Breadth to have a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one across all
observations.13

in the degree to which certain funds cannot be easily categorized in the

best-fitting benchmark. A large Variance shows that the return variation
over time of the selected benchmark poorly matches that of the fund.
While managers could select benchmarks to minimize either bias or
variance (Geman, Bienenstock, and Doursat (1992)), a choice that
generates a positive bias without an accompanying reduction in vari-
ance is consistent with performance gaming.

In Table 6, we examine the sub-sample of funds with mismatched
benchmarks and regress Bias and Variance on IS Breadth to explore the
above hypotheses. All regressions include Morningstar portfolio style
box-by-time fixed effects, as well as benchmark fixed effects (to control
for any unconditional bias and variance from each benchmark) and
standard errors are clustered by fund. The first specification (Column
(1)) shows that high IS Breadth funds tend to exhibit lower Bias in their
selection of a primary benchmark index. One standard deviation higher
IS Breadth lowers the benchmark Bias of a mismatched fund by 0.0132
% cumulatively over a 36-month period. In the multivariate specifica-
tion (Column (2)), we observe a similar magnitude of IS Breadth coef-
ficient at 0.0127 %.

Furthermore, to characterize the role of benchmarks in hedging
common risks, we regress Variance against IS Breadth in columns (3) and
(4). We observe that within mismatched funds, broadly invested funds
tend to exhibit weakly lower Variance than specialized funds do. This is
consistent with broad funds selecting a “mismatched” benchmark to
hedge out potential common or systematic risk the manager is exposed
to by the broader investment strategy. In addition, the specialized fund
results are more consistent with performance gaming. These funds

inconsistent with the expected trade-off between Bias and Variance.

5.2. Specialized funds: evidence for gaming at fund inception

Complementing the analysis of possible best-fitting benchmarks, in
this subsection, we examine the subsample of funds that switched their
benchmarks over time. This subsample enables us to study how relative
benchmark returns (possibly reflecting gaming motives) influence a
fund’s initial benchmark choice relative to their future benchmark.18
For instance, if a mismatched fund began with the S&P 500 as its pri-
mary benchmark at its inception and eventually switched to, say, Russell
1000 Growth, its initial selection of the S&P 500 – if for gaming motives
– will capture a manager’s expectation that the S&P 500 would under-
perform the Russell 1000 Growth benchmark. Alternatively, mis-
matched funds without gaming motives may have chosen the S&P 500
for other reasons (for example, following the industry norm), which
would imply no future return differential between the two benchmarks.

6. Economic channels

The empirical finance literature finds that the effectiveness of RPE in
mitigating agency costs is limited by CEO/fund manager’s capture of the
peer group/benchmark selection process. In the mutual fund setting, our
evidence of shifts from mismatched to correct benchmarks suggests that
other economic forces may help reinforce the effectiveness of RPE by
reducing such capture. To explore the underlying economic forces, in
this section, we turn broadly to two sources. First, drawing from the
principal-agent framework, we examine possible improved monitoring

by more sophisticated principals. Our investigation includes investor
learning and institutional investor governance, which are discussed in
sections 6.1 and 6.2, respectively. Second, we examine the effects of
both product market competition and product characteristics in sections
6.3 and 6.4. Given our evidence that the use of “mismatched” bench-
marks by broad funds is consistent with hedging and not with gaming
motives, we control for (standardized) IS Breadth and its interactions
with the main economic drivers under investigation in these analyses.

6.1. Investor learning

The evidence from the prior literature suggests that mismatched
benchmarks arise out of agency settings with unsophisticated principals.
That
is, biasing relative performance by choosing a mismatched
benchmark is more likely when investors don’t know what constitutes a
“matched” benchmark. We therefore hypothesize that the incidence of
such cheating may be decreased if investors become more sophisticated
monitors through learning.

If investors become aware of the nature of the mismatched bench-
mark disclosures by fund managers, or if they learn about the correct
peer group from other sources, that may diminish the influence of a
fund’s self-designated benchmark. As discussed earlier, this process of
“debiasing” may be facilitated by investor education efforts from passive
competitors (e.g., Vanguard and BlackRock), third-party entities, or
incentive-aligned financial advisers. These sources can inform investors
of the true, holding-based benchmark of a fund, regardless of the fund’s
self-declared benchmark. We call the adjustment in investor behavior,
prompted by the recognition of inadequate benchmarking, and the
correction of misinformation the investor learning hypothesis.

6.3. Competition

While enhanced monitoring is an obvious potential explanation
stemming from the RPE literature, product market competition provides
a separate economic channel that may positively affect the selection of
matched benchmarks. In this section, we explore how direct measures of
market competition might affect the decline of mismatched benchmarks.
Regardless of whether investors are aware of the correct benchmark of a
fund, they see the self-declared benchmark prominently displayed on
the fund’s webpage, prospectus, annual reports, or advertising, and are
likely to interpret it as information on product positioning. For example,
a fund benchmarked to the S&P 500 may be viewed as holding the large-
cap segment, even if the fund’s actual portfolio has a differentiated
objective. Thus, funds with the same benchmark or benchmark category
may compete with one another regardless of their portfolios.

6.4. Product market characteristics – relative return and risk

While the last section considers direct measures of product market
competition, now we examine product characteristics upon which the
prior literature has shown investors base their investment decision:
performance and risk. In terms of performance, Sensoy (2009) has
shown, and we confirm in our sample, that fund investment decisions
are sensitive to the performance of the fund relative to its benchmark. In
terms of relative risk, James and Karceski (2006) and Del Guercio and
Tkac (2008), among others, have shown that more sophisticated in-
vestors are sensitive to tracking error. For tracking error specifically,
there is also a separate potential motivation that arises from the RPE
literature. Gibbons and Murphy (1990) suggests that selecting a more
closely matched benchmark (i.e., a lower tracking error benchmark) can
help to hedge risk for the agent (manager), inducing greater effort. The
anecdotal evidence that investment advisors use tracking error as an
overall risk control and link it to manager compensation further re-
inforces this second motivation for examining tracking error.

Why then consider relative return and risk in terms of benchmark
switches? Because the reference benchmark is used to estimate these two
relative quantities, switching benchmarks could potentially improve one
or both measures, increasing demand from investors. While the prior
literature highlights higher relative performance as a benefit of
declaring mismatched benchmarks, mismatched benchmarks may also
increase perceived risk (tracking error). Therefore, we hypothesize that
funds trade off higher relative performance with higher tracking errors
to decide whether to select mismatched or matched benchmarks.

why haven’t all specialized funds corrected their mismatched bench-

documented earlier, we also examine the role that managerial
entrenchment may play in benchmark corrections in the equity fund
industry.

potential benchmark mismatches and the investment breadth through
probit regressions. We show that manager tenure and AUM relative to
firm size are positively related to the likelihood of a fund reporting a
mismatched benchmark. In our most comprehensive specification in
column (3), one additional year worked at a fund family by the fund
manager increases the probability of Benchmark Mismatch by 1.52 %,
and a 1 % increase in the Manager’s share of family assets increases the
probability by 0.26 %. These coefficients represent economically
important magnitudes.26 These results are driven by specialized funds
and are suggestive of managerial entrenchment being a barrier toward
industry wide adoption of style consistent benchmarks for mutual funds
whose investment profile fits easily to a benchmark profile.

8. Conclusion

In 1993, the SEC began requiring mutual funds to select and disclose
a “broad-based securities market index” to mitigate concerns about the
potential moral hazard of excess risk-taking, thereby recognizing the
potential for relative performance evaluation (RPE) to mitigate moral
hazard in the mutual fund industry. In that disclosure, the SEC explains
that “The choice of an appropriate benchmark is important in evaluating
performance because it is important to compare apples to apples.” They
also indicate that investors should be wary of funds that select style
mismatched benchmarks, suggesting they “should question why that
benchmark was used.”

While this focus on correct risk-adjustment and the SEC’s recognition
of the potential for relative performance evaluation (RPE) to mitigate
moral hazard is to be commended, there were at least three aspects of
the SEC’s regulatory approach to this issue that fell short. First, the
regulatory guidance uses the S&P 500 as an example of an appropriate
benchmark which may have led to the overuse of this often style-
mismatched benchmark. Second, the SEC’s guidance on what consti-
tutes an “appropriate index” lacks clear direction about how the
benchmark’s investment style should relate to that of the fund. Third,
the SEC directs the fund itself, not an independent third party, to select
the appropriate benchmark. Given these three shortcomings, the evi-
dence documented by the prior literature that style-mismatched
benchmarks are frequently selected by funds is not surprising and
highlights a potential cost with RPE.

guided by strategic rent extraction concerns, and ii) forces outside the

Understanding the economics of benchmark choices in the RPE
framework is important for policy implications. For broad funds, the
choice of mismatched benchmarks does not appear to be driven by rent

extraction considerations. For specialized funds, however, if principals
remain naïve and agents choose benchmarks for the purpose of rent
extraction, regulations aimed at restricting benchmark choices could
potentially improve consumer welfare. However, we show in this paper
that market forces including investor learning and competition work
together to mitigate the bias in benchmark selection, and this implies
potential value in investor education, competition for sophisticated in-
