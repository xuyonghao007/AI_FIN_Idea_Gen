A B S T R A C T

We propose a fiscal policy expectations mechanism. When bad macro news arrives (in our study, when initial 
jobless claims (IJC) are higher than expected), investors may expect more generous government spending and 
drive up aggregate stock prices through the expected cash flow channel. Using a time-series sample from 
January 2013 to March 2021, we find that this phenomenon emerges when newspapers mention fiscal policy 
more. In the cross section, firms expected to receive more government spending – through stimulus supports 
during COVID-19 or procurement contracts before 2020 – exhibit higher individual stock returns when bad 
IJC shocks arrive.

announcement surprises on stock returns. In a low-interest-rate, crisis

Given the lack of futures markets or surveys to elicit high-frequency 
FP expectations, we provide evidence for two testable predictions of 
this mechanism and focus on the weekly initial jobless claims (IJC) 
announcements as the main macro events in the paper. The first pre-
diction is that return responses to IJC shocks should be higher during 
periods with more expansionary FP expectations, particularly when 
bad IJC news arrives. In a time-series sample from January 2013 to 
March 2021, we construct measures based on news articles on IJC 
announcement days to capture time-varying expectations of FP. The 
second prediction is that when FP is actively discussed nationwide amid 
heightened budgetary debates, firms and industries that are expected 
to receive more fiscal spending should exhibit higher individual stock 
returns when bad IJC shocks appear. We first use a specific Covid 
period (early 2020 to March 2021) with unprecedented fiscal activities 
as our identification strategy, and construct three cross sections based 
on  a  granular  Covid  stimulus  database,  actual  stimulus  bill  textual 
analysis, and firm fundamentals. In addition, we generalize the cross-
sectional test to our pre-2020 sample, where the dominant firm-level 
government spending is in the form of procurement contracts.

Initial Jobless Claims (IJC) are announced every Thursday at 8:30 
a.m. Eastern Time, and IJC surprises or shocks in our paper are defined 
as the percent difference between actual and expected IJC numbers. 
The higher the shocks are, the worse the news is. We first highlight 
two stylized facts from IJC announcement event days. For one, news 
articles that are written to describe IJC announcement news on the 
same day discuss different topics (such as fiscal policy (FP), monetary 
policy (MP), and uncertainty) at different points during our sample 
period.  There  are  fluctuations  in  FP-related  mentions  before  2019, 
typically at the height of government shutdown concerns and bud-
getary debates; moreover, FP mentions jump 2–3 standard deviations 
(SD) higher after 2020, particularly when solely arising from bad IJC 
announcements. These heightened FP mentions typically come with 
expansionary speculation, as the quote on Page 1 shows. Second, in a 
subsample from February 2020 to March 2021 with unprecedented FP 
mentions, stock returns significantly rise with IJC shocks, overturning 
the ‘‘bad is bad’’ pricing from paradigm models. A one SD increase 
in the IJC surprise leads to significant increases in daily open-to-close 
market  index  returns  of  around  30  basis  points.  The  phenomenon 
affects the market through the expected cash flow growth channel and 
only exists during bad IJC days. These stylized facts motivate our fiscal 
policy expectations hypothesis.

To test the time-series prediction, we interact IJC shocks with time-
varying topic mentions of FP, MP, and uncertainty. In the 2013–2021 
sample,  when  FP  mentions  are  one  SD  higher  than  average,  stock 
return responses caused by a 0.1 unit increase in bad IJC shocks are 
24–30  basis  points  higher.  This  finding  is  robust  to  controlling  for 
monetary policy and uncertainty mentions. We next find that return 
responses to good IJC shocks are not associated with fiscal policy expec-
tations but with monetary policy expectations (gauged by either text- 
or survey-based measures). This finding is consistent with predictions 
in Boyd et al. (2005) and empirical results in Elenev et al. (2024). In a 
low-interest-rate environment (which is what our sample focuses on), 
interest rates should have more potential to increase, and good IJC 
shocks should be able to trigger more expectations in such increases.

We next introduce our two cross section empirical strategies, in 
Covid and pre-Covid samples, respectively. First, we use the period with 
active fiscal stimulus debates from February 2020 to March 2021 to 
our identification advantage. During this period, fiscal stimulus bills 
received unprecedented public attention and should be economically 
relevant to almost all industries and firms. To capture industry- or firm-
level fiscal policy expectations, we construct three cross sections based 
on various textual or archival data sources. Investors may infer the 
likelihood of a particular industry receiving more fiscal support from 
reading industry mentions in actual stimulus bills. We search industry 
mentions in the major stimulus bills using industry keywords from 
the NAICS website. Industries mentioned more heavily in actual bills 
exhibit statistically higher return-IJC shock correlations, supporting our 
hypothesis. For instance, the healthcare industry receives a consider-
able amount of fiscal subsidy, given the nature of the pandemic crisis, 
and demonstrates a high industry return-IJC shock correlation at 0.228. 
Several non-crisis-related industries (e.g., transportation, manufactur-
ing) with more mentions in the actual bills also exhibit a stronger ‘‘Main 
Street pain, Wall Street gain’’ phenomenon.

We  next  construct  a  novel  database  based  on  raw  data  from  a 
Treasury Office website (USAspending.gov) that reports all promised 
and actual fiscal distributions from the Federal government to each firm 
under the legal bills. Fiscal distributions under the Covid stimulus bills 
(CARES of 3/27/2020, CAA of 12/27/2020, and ARP of 3/11/2021) 
are largely in the form of a Paycheck Protection Program (PPP) or other 
forgivable loans. We find that firms that are promised greater stimulus 
funding exhibit statistically higher return-IJC shock correlations and 
sensitivities. Importantly, we also use only promised PPP distributions 
(which should have a direct connection with labor market outcomes) 
and find both economically and statistically similar results. Consistent 
with our legal bill textual analysis study, the healthcare and air trans-
portation industries receive the greatest stimulus support during the 
early pandemic.

Last but not least, we obtain changes in firm fundamentals from 
2019 to early 2020 to quantify the initial effect of Covid on these 
firms; this should signal the fiscal support a firm is expected to receive 
from  the  Federal  government.  These  fundamental  measures  include 
job postings, employment, revenues, and earnings per share. Results 
remain robust at the 5% significance level.

In our second empirical strategy to test the cross-section predic-
tion, we generalize it to our pre-Covid sample from 2013 to 2019, 
where fiscal spending to firms is primarily in the form of government 
procurement contracts, as confirmed both in terms of absolute dollar 
amounts  and  public  attention.  Specifically,  we  construct  a  detailed 
Government Procurement dataset based on USAspending.gov, and use 
the recent past procurement obligation amounts (unscaled or scaled 
by recent revenue) as proxies for firm- and industry-level future fiscal 
dependence or sensitivity. Our cross-sectional results remain robust, 
both statistically and economically. During times with relatively height-
ened fiscal discussions, firms with more dependence on fiscal spending 
exhibit significantly higher stock returns in response to a unit of bad 
IJC shocks. Before 2020, such times align with heightened government 
shutdown concerns and budgetary debates (such as early 2013 and 
2019) according to our textual analysis. Our procurement-based fiscal 
dependence measure provides a novel and more granular alternative to 
the U.S. National Income and Product Accounts (NIPA)-based measure 
as used in Belo et al. (2013).

Related literature

Our research contributes to the economics and finance literature in 
several ways. First, we join the literature that shows that macro an-
nouncements matter to the stock market (e.g., Gilbert, 2011; Savor and 
Wilson, 2013; Ai and Bansal, 2018; Hirshleifer and Sheng, 2021; Fisher 
et al., 2022, among many others). In particular, our work joins existing 
papers that study the time series pattern of stock market reactions

to macro announcement surprises. The literature typically settles on 
two explanations. There is a business-cycle explanation (e.g., McQueen 
and Roley, 1993; Andersen et al., 2007) that predicts that business 
conditions reinforce the pricing of macro shocks during contractionary 
times. Another strand (e.g., Boyd et al., 2005; Elenev et al., 2024; Yang 
and Zhu, 2021; Caballero and Simsek, 2021) argues that time-varying 
return responses to macro news likely also depend on monetary policy 
expectations, which are often but not always correlated with business 
cycles.3 Our research contributes to this literature by providing empiri-
cal evidence of the existence and economic significance of a specific 
cash  flow  expectation  mechanism,  i.e.,  time-varying  fiscal  spending 
expectations.

Next, our empirical evidence also complements the existing fiscal 
policy literature. While there is an extensive economics literature on 
the macroeconomic effects of fiscal policy,4 there is scant research on 
its asset pricing effects. Most studies (see Croce et al., 2012a,b; Gomes 
et  al.,  2013;  Bretscher  et  al.,  2020;  Croce  et  al.,  2021,  etc.)  focus 
on examining the long-term price effects of actual policies (tax and 
spending) at quarterly or lower frequencies though the lens of an equi-
librium framework. Meanwhile, empirical work by Belo et al. (2013) 
and Belo and Yu (2013) examines the impact of actual government 
spending and tax policies on the cross section of equity risk premia, 
and  Diercks  and  Waller  (2017)  and  Elenev  et  al.  (2022)  examine 
the interplay between monetary policy, fiscal policy, and risk premia. 
More related to our work, Bianchi et al. (2021) document the role 
of congressional tweets in changing investor expectations about a bill 
passing. Xu et al. (2024) show that analysts form fiscal risk perceptions 
of procurement contracts. Theoretical work, including studies by Chris-
tiano et al. (2011) and Bianchi et al. (2023), has also predicted that 
fiscal policy would result in a much stronger effect to the real economy 
when monetary policy is less active. Our research contributes to this 
literature by hypothesizing that fiscal policy already affects the capital 
market by affecting investor expectations, which get capitalized at a 
high frequency. We use labor news announcements as an identification 
strategy to help ‘‘sign’’ investor fiscal policy expectations, and a low-
interest-rate sample period helps constrain MP behaviors on the lax 
policy side.

In  a  broader  view,  our  paper  documents  an  expected  ‘‘Govern-
ment Put’’ among investors, as fiscal policy and particularly spending 
policies have recently become more active and sizable. We plan to 
continue updating the new fiscal-related datasets used in our paper, 
such as the news-based FP mention measures and cross-sectional fiscal 
distributions, to make them available to other researchers.

2.1. Textual analysis on IJC announcement days

We examine topic mentions on IJC announcement days. We use 
CNBC’s IJC news articles, which are written each Thursday to describe 
and interpret that morning’s IJC announcement. Unlike other news 
sources such as WSJ or Bloomberg, CNBC has a designated website for 
Initial Jobless Claims announcements, https://www.cnbc.com/jobless-
claims/. A team of CNBC reporters writes and publishes one article 
about each Thursday’s IJC announcement by noon that day; a handful 
of times they collaborate with reporters from Reuters. We manually 
collect these CNBC IJC news articles on announcement days as far back 
as is available.7 We are able to identify 366 IJC articles from the CNBC 
website through March 18, 2021, when we began collecting data for 
this project. Fig.  1 shows the article distribution over time. We obtain 
only a few articles from before 2013 on their website, while the article 
collection becomes quite stable afterwards. This limits the start year of 
our textual analysis to 2013. The bottom plot shows a relatively stable 
split between bad and good IJC announcements per 60-announcement 
rolling window.

We  construct  time-varying  topic  mentions  metrics  of  5  general 
topics:  Fiscal  policy  (‘‘FP’’),  monetary  policy  (‘‘MP’’),  economic  un-
certainty (‘‘UNC’’), coronavirus-related (‘‘COVID’’), and normal words 
that appear in describing IJC (‘‘NORMAL’’). We relegate detailed lists 
of keywords and empirical methodology to Online Appendix section 
OB and discuss the key points below. First, general textbook terms 
that define fiscal policy, such as ‘‘fiscal policy’’, ‘‘tax’’, or ‘‘government 
debt’’, are not typically how fiscal policy as a topic gets mentioned 
in labor news announcement articles. To accommodate the needs of 
our research, we developed a group of words that reflect discussions of 
government spending, grants to the states, transfers (augmented unem-
ployment benefits), and lawmaking to capture fiscal policy mentions.

The second topic of interest is monetary policy. The word list includes 
‘‘central bank’’, ‘‘inflation’’, ‘‘Federal Reserve’’, Federal Reserve Chair-
persons’ last names, and so on. The third topic is economic uncertainty, 
and  we  follow  Baker  et  al.  (2016)’s  word  list.  The  last  two  topics 
– Covid-related and normal IJC terms – are included for validation 
purposes. Finally, we use state-of-the-art ‘‘Term Frequency-Inverse Doc-
ument Frequency’’ or ‘‘TF-IDF’’ scores in our textual analysis to obtain 
the topic mentions metrics (see a recent survey in Beel et al., 2016). 
In general, the score of a word (after stemming and lemmatization) 
increases proportionally to the number of times this word appears in 
the document (Luhn, 1957). In our research, the average of the TF-IDF 
scores of all words in the same topic then becomes the topic’s score. 
Given that each IJC article is relatively short (average = 327 words), 
we always construct topic mentions metrics using groups of weeks.

also  exhibiting  a  clear  hump  from  late  2016  to  late  2018  relative 
to  2015.  Therefore,  higher  MP  mentions  on  good  IJC  days  can  be 
interpreted  as  more  contractionary  MP  expectations,  which  can  be 
validated by their significant and positive correlation (0.46) with the 
quarterly revisions in future interest rate expectations (source: Survey 
of Professional Forecasters).10

Taken together, we find that IJC news is associated with different 
topics at different times. What is new and interesting is that specula-
tions about fiscal policy, particularly when bad IJC news arrives, appear 
sizable, exhibiting intuitive time variations.

Covid period. These observations suggest that the counteracting force 
likely works through the expected growth channel.

3. Mechanism hypothesis

The  stylized  facts  from  Section  2  motivate  a  specific  cash  flow 
mechanism for macro shocks to affect stock returns. In this section, 
we generalize these stylized facts conceptually and present testable 
hypotheses for the rest of the paper.

We  propose  a  fiscal  policy  expectations  mechanism.  In  a  low-
interest-rate crisis environment, when Main Street suffers more than 
expected,  investors  may  expect  more  generous  federal  government 
spending through fiscal policy, driving up expected future cash flow 
growth and stock prices. We expect this mechanism to be time-varying, 
as our textual analysis already shows that perceptions of policy tools do 
change over time (see Fig.  2). During periods when discussions about 
fiscal policy and related debates are not heightened, the ‘‘Main Street 
pain, Wall Street gain’’ pattern in stock returns should diminish.

It is however empirically challenging to test this mechanism, given 
that there are no readily observable futures markets or high-frequency 
surveys to elicit investor FP expectations. As a result, we examine two 
testable predictions:

1. Time series: Time-varying FP expectations, as a state variable, 
should  explain  time-varying  return  responses  to  IJC  shocks, 
particularly on bad IJC days.

2. Cross section:When FP is actively discussed nationwide, firms 
and industries that are expected to receive more fiscal support 
or spending should exhibit higher individual stock returns when 
bad IJC shocks occur.

To test the first prediction (see Section 4), we proxy time-varying FP 
expectations from 2013 to 2021 using news-based measures mentioned 
earlier. The framework also flexibly controls for other aforementioned 
mechanisms such as monetary policy. To test the second prediction 
(see Sections 5 and 6), we present two pieces of evidence. We first use 
the period with active Covid Stimulus Package debates from 02/2020 
to 03/2021 to our identification advantage and construct three de-
tailed firm- and industry-level cross sections based on various textual 
or  archival  data  sources  (the  stimulus  bill  texts,  the  Treasury  reg-
istry office, and firm fundamentals). Second, given the lack of widely-
distributed stimulus data before 2020, we generalize the cross-sectional 
test  to  our  pre-Covid  sample  from  2013  to  2019  and  use  detailed

4. Mechanism: Time-series evidence

We span the time variation in return sensitivities to IJC shocks on 
IJC announcement days with several state variables that are of interest. 
The specification is as follows: 

𝑦𝑡 = 𝛽0 + 𝛽1𝐼𝐽 𝐶𝑠ℎ𝑜𝑐𝑘𝑡 + 𝜷𝟐𝒁𝝉 + 𝜷𝟑𝐼𝐽 𝐶𝑠ℎ𝑜𝑐𝑘𝑡 ∗ 𝒁𝝉 + 𝜀𝑡,

(2)

where 𝑡 and 𝜏 denote day and quarter, respectively; 𝑦 is the daily 
market returns (in basis points) on IJC announcement days; 𝒁 is one or 
multiple quarterly state variable(s), which is standardized for interpre-
tation purpose. The unit of observation is the IJC announcement event 
day, and we exclude overlaps with macro and monetary announcement 
dates as before. As a result, the sample period is January 2013 to 
March  2021,  and  we  consider  a  bad  IJC  sample  (N  =  184)  and  a 
good IJC sample (N = 189). To obtain quarterly state variables for the 
bad IJC (good IJC) sample, we use all news articles that correspond 
to  bad  (good)  IJC  announcement  days  within  a  specific  quarter  to 
construct the bad (good) topic-mention scores (see construction details 
of the TF-IDF scores in Section 2.1). Three quarterly state variables 
are fiscal policy (FP), monetary policy (MP), and uncertainty (UNC). In 
addition, we follow Elenev et al. (2024) and also consider the difference 
between  the  one-quarter-ahead  forecast  and  the  nowcast  of  the  3-
month  Treasury  bill  rate  (‘‘𝛥𝑇 𝑏𝑖𝑙𝑙3𝑚’’,  𝑇 𝑏𝑖𝑙𝑙3𝑚𝜏+1|𝜏−1 − 𝑇 𝑏𝑖𝑙𝑙3𝑚𝜏|𝜏−1; 
source: Survey of Professional Forecasters (SPF)); this variable captures 
revisions in the future interest rate, conceptually close to the text-based 
MP measures.

As discussed in Section 2.1, higher FP (MP) mentions during this 
sample period (2013–2021) can be generally interpreted as more ex-
pansionary  (contractionary)  policy  expectations.  Good  IJC  day  MP 
mentions and 𝛥𝑇 𝑏𝑖𝑙𝑙3𝑚 are also significantly and positively correlated 
at 0.46 (p-value = 0.000). One disadvantage of the SPF-based quarterly 
interest rate forecast data is that we do not know when the survey was 
conducted, on good IJC, bad IJC, or non-macro-announcement days, 
whereas for our text-based measures we do know when the texts were 
written. Finally, the quarterly FP and MP mentions are statistically 
uncorrelated during the sample period, regardless of the bad or good 
IJC day sample.

Table  3 reports the regression results of Eq.  (2), where the inter-
action coefficients are of interest. First, columns (1) and (2) are our 

main result using open-to-close S&P500 log returns and DowJones65 
log returns, respectively. On bad IJC announcement days, when fiscal 
policy mentions are one SD higher than average, stock return responses 
to a 10% IJC shock are 28 basis points higher, given the significant 
and positive interaction estimates. Both columns control for the other 
competing channel mentioned earlier (MP expectations). In columns (3) 
and (4), we see that MP mentions and rate forecast revisions (𝛥𝑇 𝑏𝑖𝑙𝑙3𝑚) 
play an insignificant role in explaining return responses to bad IJC 
shocks.

We conduct several robustness tests and show them in Table  A.4. 
First, our main finding of the positive interaction coefficient with FP 
is  both  statistically  and  economically  robust  if  we  only  include  FP 
interaction terms (see columns (1) and (2)), which is not surprising 
because FP is not significantly correlated with other controls in our 
construction and sample period. We relegate other univariate results 
to Internet Appendix Table OA2. Second, columns (3)–(6) of Table  A.4 
complement columns (3) and (4) of Table  3 by incorporating 𝛥𝑇 𝑏𝑖𝑙𝑙3𝑚
and 𝑈 𝑁𝐶 into the model one at a time, and our results remain intact.
Given that FP mentions are categorically higher during 2020–2021, 
we also examine whether our results exist from 01/2013 and 12/2019 
(N  =  154).  As  shown  in  columns  (7)–(10)  of  Table   A.4,  we  find 
that while the statistical significance of the interaction term decreases 
from 1% significance to 10% significance, the main qualitative and 
quantitative results remain robust. This is an indication that the FP state 
variable variations already have the ability to explain time-varying 
return responses to IJC shocks before 2020. What is different is that 
the rise in FP speculations during the Covid period is strong enough 
(i.e., around 2–3 SDs higher than the full sample average) to completely 
overturn the sign of return responses to IJC shocks, and as a result, a 
simple subsample regression yields significant and positive coefficients 
(see Tables  1 and 2). On the other hand, the pre-Covid FP effect shows 
in smaller-than-usual decreases in returns when bad IJC shocks arrive. 
Before  2020,  increases  in  FP  mentions  when  bad  IJC  news  arrives 
can typically be attributed to periods around government shutdown 
threats. For instance, on January 10, 2013, actual IJC numbers were 
higher than expected, but the open-to-close stock returns ended up 16% 
higher; CNBC commented on this news with speculation that ‘‘Congress 
must vote to raise the government’s $16.4 trillion borrowing limit by 
around late February’’. One can find similar textual evidence during 
early  2018  and  early  2019  when  discussions  of  and  congressional 
debates about government shutdowns were heightened.

5. Mechanism: Cross-sectional evidence during covid

In this section, we test the cross-section prediction from Section 3 in 
the Covid-period sample from February 2020 to March 2021, excluding 
outlier IJC shocks and overlaps with macro and monetary policy an-
nouncements as before. During this period, fiscal stimulus bills received 
unprecedented public attention and should reach almost all industries 
and firms, allowing us to observe enough cross-firm variation.

When a bad IJC shock arrives, investors may expect an increase in 
the likelihood that an expansionary fiscal policy bill – in the case of 
the Covid period, a stimulus bill – will be passed. This change in belief 
could affect expectations about cash flow growth in the economy; firms 
and industries that are expected to receive more fiscal support thus 
exhibit higher individual stock returns. We construct three granular-
level datasets. Under the prediction, higher return responses to IJC 
shocks should occur in:

Sections 5.1, 5.2, and 5.3 introduce each of the three cross-sectional 
measures and present evidence. To be consistent with the time series 
section, we primarily consider the S&P 500 universe. All cross-sectional 
tests robustly support our hypothesis.

5.3. Firm crisis impact measures

More broadly speaking, investors might expect firms that are likely 
to be or have been more affected by Covid to receive more government 
support in the future. Both expected and realized impact are likely to 
enter active policy debates and are therefore meaningful in investors’ 
formations of their cash flow expectations. We use six measures to 
capture Covid impact to firm fundamentals in our third cross-section.

First,  we  use  a  novel  dataset  from  LinkUp  that  indexes  all  job 
listings directly from employer websites in real-time. Our first Covid 
impact measure is changes in the number of job postings from a firm’s 
2019 average to its 2020 April-May average. One advantage of this 
measure  is  its  foresighted  nature;  firms  cut  their  job  listings  when 
they  expect  weaker  business  prospects  in  the  near  future.  We  also 
consider realized impacts in early 2020: the quarter-on-quarter growth 
rates of total revenue between 2019Q2 and 2020Q2 (to control for 
seasonality), the change in quarter-on-quarter earnings per share (basic, 
excluding extraordinary items) from 2019Q2 to 2020Q2, the change in 
the number of employees from fiscal year (FY) 2019 to fiscal year 2020,

6. Mechanism: Pre-covid evidence using government procurement 
award data

In this section, we expand our cross-sectional test to a pre-Covid 
sample.  To  start,  we  search  for  the  main  source  of  fiscal  spending 
distributed to firms and industries before 2020 in order to have enough 
variations to test. Specifically, we evaluate both actual dollar amounts 
and public attention. Fiscal spending to individual firms in the form of 
direct cash, forgivable loans, or subsidies (or those categorized under 
these emergency fund codes) is minimal during our pre-2020 sample 
period. This stands in sharp contrast to another type of fiscal spending 
that also reaches firms: government procurement contracts.

To illustrate the magnitude, Fig.  7 plots rolling-12 month total fiscal 
spending to firms in the S&P500 universe. Fiscal spending through 
procurement contracts fluctuates around 300 billion dollars annually 
before 2020. After 2020, it only mildly increases, showing less domi-
nance as a source of fiscal spending compared to the stimulus packages. 
Fig.  8 shows the monthly mentions of words related to ‘‘stimulus’’ and

7. Discussion

A conceptual framework.  Empirical choices in our research are consis-
tent with the following conceptual framework. Developing a formal 
model that incorporates these scenarios merits future work. The base-
line scenario (A) describes traditional asset pricing models, where a 
bad macroeconomic shock leads to changes in state variables such as 
volatility, risk aversion, and expected growth, resulting in lower stock 
prices. Conversely, a good macro shock would lead to higher stock 
prices under the same framework. In a non-ZLB scenario (B) with active 
policy expectations, when a bad macro shock arrives, investors expect 
MP to be more lax in the future, resulting in a counteracting force that 
drives up the asset prices. This MP expectation mechanism operates 
through discount rate channels (i.e., interest rate and risk premium 
channels)  and  has  been  discussed  in  existing  literature.  To  keep  it 
simple yet realistic (with respect to recent decades), we assume that 
fiscal policy is mostly an expansionary policy tool, and the primary 
means of FP are the tax and spending policy tools, which should directly 
change expected cash flow growth. Scenario (C), a ZLB scenario with 
active policy expectations, is the most analogous to the sample we study 
in this paper (i.e., a low interest rate environment). The main difference 
with (B) is that MP is constrained at the ZLB. In this context, when bad 
shocks occur, public attention to and expectations about FP tools could 
increase.

In general, this conceptual framework underpins our empirical de-
cisions  to  separately  examine  CF  and  DR  channels  throughout  the 
paper and to distinguish between bad and good IJC day responses. 
Additionally, this mechanism should exist without the Covid period, 
which is tested in both the aggregate and cross section parts of the 
paper.

return difference between the lowest and the highest quintile bins;

8. Conclusion

Our paper starts with establishing a few stylized facts. Compared to 
earlier years, in 2020–2021 there are significantly heightened fiscal pol-
icy mentions when bad IJC news comes out, and stock returns increase 
with IJC shocks. In addition, the IJC effect mostly influences stock 
returns through the expected cash flow channel and is pronounced dur-
ing bad IJC days. Given these stylized facts, we hypothesize a specific 
cash flow mechanism that can be generalized, and provide empirical 
evidence  both  in  time  series  and  cross  section.  Time-varying  fiscal 
policy expectations, gauged by a news-based topic mention measure, 
significantly explain the time variation in return responses to IJC shocks 
on IJC announcement days. Next, we show two key pieces of evidence 
in the cross section. In general, firms that are expected to receive more 
fiscal spending – proxied by Covid stimulus after 2020 or procurement 
awards before 2020 – exhibit higher individual stock returns when 
bad IJC shocks appear. Our paper mainly contributes to the literature 
by empirically establishing a fiscal policy expectations state variable, 
and we use labor news announcements as an identification strategy 
to help ‘‘sign’’ the mechanism. Future research should examine the 
macroeconomic effects and welfare effects of fiscal policy expectations. 
The fact that people have fiscal policy expectations when bad news 
arrives could feed back to the macro economy through consumption 
behaviors, labor options, and borrowing decisions, as we observe in 
the inflation hikes and the Great Resignation of late 2021 and 2022. 
In addition, while the Covid crisis triggered an unprecedented adverse 
shock to the labor market, the capital market gain due to investor 
anticipation of fiscal stimuli is not trivial in dollar terms. From February 
2020 to March 2021, the average daily capital gain in the S&P 500 
market is 73 billion dollars on bad IJC days, 18 billion dollars on 

good  IJC  days,  and  44  billion  dollars  on  non-IJC  days  (see  details 
in Online Appendix Table OA4). Overall, our work implies that the 
distributional effect of fiscal policy could also transmit through the 
investor expectations channel, which gets capitalized into the stock 
market at a high frequency.
