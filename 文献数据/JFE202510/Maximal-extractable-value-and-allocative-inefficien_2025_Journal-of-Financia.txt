1. Introduction

The blockchain settlement layer facilitates systematic frontrunning, resulting in inefficient block-space alloca-
tion. Private transaction pools can reduce these inefficiencies and enhance welfare. However, full adoption 
is limited by misaligned incentives between users and validators. Validators are reluctant to forgo rents they 
earn from frontrunning – referred to as maximal extractable value – leading to a partial adoption equilibrium 
in which frontrunning persists. Our empirical analysis of Ethereums Flashbots private pool supports these 
findings: validators earn higher revenues, users facing greater frontrunning risk are more likely to use the 
private pool, and attackers cost-to-revenue ratios in private pools converge to one.

Historically, the settlement and record-keeping of financial transac-
tions have been managed by trusted, highly regulated central entities 
such as brokers and clearinghouses. The advent of blockchain technol-
ogy and smart contracts has paved the way for decentralized finance 
(DeFi), which seeks to address inefficiencies and opacity in traditional 

financial systems (Harvey et al., 2021; John et al., 2023). DeFi services, 
such  as  decentralized  exchanges,  lending  protocols,  and  real-world 
asset tokenization, operate without centralized intermediaries. Instead, 
they rely on a network of decentralized, pseudonymous validators for 
transaction execution and settlement. This reliance on self-interested 
validators introduces a potential misalignment of incentives between

In this paper, we investigate a critical inefficiency in the blockchain 
settlement layer: systematic frontrunning. This problem arises from two 
key characteristics of the blockchain infrastructure. First, the decen-
tralized  structure  of  blockchain  networks  prevents  the  effective  en-
forcement of time priority in transaction execution. Instead, transac-
tion  inclusion  is  solely  determined  by  validators,  typically  through 
a fee-based auction that prioritizes higher-fee transactions (Buterin, 
2015; Nakamoto, 2008). Second, the decentralized and pseudonymous 
nature  of  validators  necessitates  the  network-wide  broadcasting  of 
transactions. As a result, pending transactions become visible in public 
memory  pools  before  block  inclusion,  allowing  malicious  actors  to 
observe and exploit transaction information by submitting higher-fee 
transactions ahead of the original ones (Park, 2023; Daian et al., 2020).
These infrastructural features give rise to various forms of fron-
trunning that are uncommon in traditional finance and impact critical 
market functions such as trading, market making, and price discovery 
through arbitrage. For example, traders exchanging assets may experi-
ence slippage exceeding 90%—a severe loss due to frontrunning. When 
liquidity providers try to quote near the current mid-price, they may 
encounter frontrunners who manipulate the mid-quote, forcing them 
to offer liquidity at highly unfavorable prices.2 Arbitrageurs seeking 
to align prices across markets and contribute to price discovery might 
find their transactions preempted by frontrunners, effectively nullifying 
their arbitrage opportunities.

However,  these  direct  financial  losses  represent  only  a  fraction 
of  a  larger  problem.  Frontrunning  introduces  significant  allocative 
inefficiencies into the blockchain ecosystem, creating a high-risk en-
vironment for transaction submissions. This not only excludes poten-
tially  valuable  transactions  from  being  included  in  blocks  but  also 
distorts the efficient allocation of blockspace. The frontrunning activity 
benefits validators who extract infrastructure fees and increase their 
revenues from ordering transactions, a phenomenon known as Maximal 
Extractable Value (MEV).4 As controllers of the settlement infrastruc-
ture, validators prioritize high-fee frontrunning transactions, thereby 
profiting significantly from these opportunities.

The  problems  in  the  transaction  settlement  layer  of  blockchains 
have attracted significant attention, with experts arguing that it poses a 
challenge to the industry itself (see Auer et al., 2022) and threatens the 
integrity of smart-contract-enabled blockchains (as highlighted in Da-
ian et al., 2020 and Obadia, 2020). This concern is further underscored 
by IOSCO (2023), which published an extensive 128-page report con-
taining recommendations for regulating the DeFi ecosystem. The report 
emphasizes frontrunning as a key risk that must be identified, managed, 
and regulated to ensure the sustainability of the DeFi ecosystem.5

To address the issue of systematic frontrunning in the settlement 
layer, private transaction pools have been introduced and implemented 
as a potential solution across major blockchains. These private pools 
allow users to submit transactions directly to validators monitoring 
the pool, thereby reducing the risks and adverse effects of frontrun-
ning (Daian, 2022). While the technological infrastructure for private 
pools is in place, the question of their adoption by ecosystem partici-
pants remains underexplored. Do validators and users have sufficient 
incentives to participate in private pools? Will validators be willing to 
forgo rents from frontrunning activities? Can private pools effectively 
mitigate frontrunning risks and enhance overall welfare? Our paper 
aims to address these fundamental questions.

We  develop  a  sequential  game-theoretical  model  of  blockchain 
transaction processing featuring three types of agents: validators, users, 
and attackers. In the first stage, a discrete set of homogeneous val-
idators  allocate  their  validation  power  between  public  and  private 
pools.  The  second  stage  features  a  population  of  users,  comprising 
a  continuum  of  frontrunnable  users  –  vulnerable  to  attacks  when 
using the public pool – and a continuum of non-frontrunnable users. 
These users are heterogeneous in their valuation of transactions and 
choose between public and private pools for transaction submission. In 
the final stage, attackers compete to frontrun transactions submitted 
through the public pool.

We  analyze  the  prevailing  equilibrium  in  a  blockchain  that  re-
lies  solely  on  a  public  pool  to  identify  the  allocative  inefficiencies 
caused by frontrunning. We show that frontrunning leads to suboptimal 
blockspace allocation and a reduction in aggregate welfare through 
three interconnected mechanisms. First, high frontrunning costs dis-
courage marginal frontrunnable users from participating, resulting in 
the exclusion of potentially valuable transactions. Second, attackers

Transactions submitted to the private pool are subject to execution 
risk, with the likelihood of non-execution directly proportional to the 
amount of validation power not allocated to monitoring the private 
pool. In equilibrium, attackers strategically submit their transactions 
to both pools: they utilize the private pool to secure execution priority 
while using the public pool to reduce the risk of non-execution. In 
the private pool, attackers bid their true valuation of the frontrun-
ning opportunity, effectively transferring all potential revenues to the 
validators.

Frontrunnable users weigh the risk of being frontrun in the public 
pool against the risk of non-execution in the private pool. Their equilib-
rium decision is influenced by two key factors: the adoption rate of the 
private pool by validators, which determines the execution risk, and the 
expected costs of frontrunning in the public pool. As validator adoption 
of the private pool increases and frontrunning costs in the public pool 
rise, more users are incentivized to submit transactions through the 
private pool.

Validators must balance competing economic forces when decid-
ing  whether  to  adopt  private  pools.  On  one  hand,  allocating  vali-
dation power to the private pool enhances payoffs through two pri-
mary channels: increased demand from users seeking protection against 
frontrunning and the ability to extract rents from attackers through 
true-value bidding. On the other hand, greater private pool adoption 
reduces execution risk, drawing more users away from the public pool

and  thereby  diminishing  frontrunning  opportunities.  This  shift  may 
lower validators’ overall payoffs by reducing aggregate demand for 
blockspace, decreasing transaction fees, and lessening revenues from 
attackers’  competition.  Our  analysis  shows  that  integrating  private 
pools  into  public  blockchains  generally  enhances  welfare,  with  the 
extent of improvement depending on the severity of frontrunning risk.
We demonstrate that a unique subgame perfect equilibrium with 
full adoption of private pools emerges when frontrunning risk is high. 
Under these circumstances, frontrunnable users are driven to the pri-
vate pool, leaving validators with no incentive to allocate validation 
power to the public pool. This equilibrium leads to efficient blockspace 
allocation, effectively eliminating frontrunning and preventing the ex-
clusion of valuable transactions. However, when frontrunning risk is 
moderate, a unique partial adoption equilibrium arises. In this scenario, 
partial adoption of the private pool introduces execution risk, caus-
ing some frontrunnable users to continue submitting transactions to 
the public pool despite the risk of being frontrun. Validators, in this 
equilibrium, are indifferent to allocating additional validation power 
to the private pool, as the direct gains are counterbalanced by indirect 
losses due to an aggregate reduction in block rewards. This partial 
adoption equilibrium is inefficient because it fails to fully eliminate 
the frontrunning friction, leading to wasted blockspace and incomplete 
inclusion of valuable transactions.

The failure to achieve full adoption of private pools mirrors the clas-
sic hold-up problem: the benefits of monitoring private pools primarily 
accrue to frontrunnable users, leaving validators with reduced incen-
tives to allocate their validation power. To realign these incentives, we 
propose a contractual solution where frontrunnable users commit ex-
ante to pay validators an additional fee for executing their transactions 
in  the  private  pool.  This  mechanism  establishes  a  unique,  socially 
efficient equilibrium with full private pool adoption. By utilizing pre-
signed transactions sent to the private pool, this approach effectively 
addresses commitment problems that are typically more difficult to 
manage  in  traditional  bilateral  trade  settings,  where  maintaining  a 
commitment to non-renegotiation is often unfeasible.

Empirically, we test our main model predictions by analyzing data 
from Flashbots, a platform that supported the vast majority of privately 
submitted transactions on the Ethereum blockchain over the period we 
study.6

We construct a unique and comprehensive dataset that combines 
transaction-level frontrunning information, mempool data, and block-
level statistics. Our analysis reveals direct evidence of partial adoption 
of private pools, with a stable adoption rate of 50%–60% following an 
initial adjustment period after the introduction of Flashbots. We further 
validate the three key implications of our model concerning validators, 
attackers, and frontrunnable users.

Using a difference-in-differences strategy, we exploit the staggered 
adoption of Flashbots by the two largest Ethereum mining pools. We 
find that the first miner to adopt Flashbots – Spark Pool – earned 
an  additional  0.066  Ether  per  block  after  adoption,  relative  to  the 
control miner, Ethermine. Our triple difference regression confirms that 
this increase is attributed to private blocks, supporting our model’s 
implication that private blocks generate higher revenue than public 
blocks for miners.

Literature review.  Our work contributes to the growing literature on 
inefficiencies in public blockchains, focusing particularly on frontrun-
ning  and  MEV.  Existing  studies  in  this  stream  of  literature  include 
Park (2023) who examines ‘‘sandwich’’ attacks in automated market 
makers, and Lehar and Parlour (2023) who analyze competition be-
tween ‘‘good’’ and ‘‘bad’’ arbitrage bots, arguing that private settlement 
can mitigate under-investment in arbitrage aimed at price discovery. 
Capponi et al. (2023a) analyze private pools’ effects on price discovery, 
focusing on a single agent type: informed traders. Informed traders con-
ceal orders, thereby limiting the information revealed through pending 
orders.  In  contrast,  our  study  examines  how  private  pools  shift  in-
centives  as  settlement  layers  affect  overall  welfare,  while  explicitly 
accounting for the implications of frontrunning frictions.

Other  studies  have  explored  protocol-level  solutions  to  mitigate 
frontrunning risk. Gans and Holden (2022) propose a simultaneous 
report mechanism, while Canidio and Danos (2024) advocate for a 
commit-reveal protocol. These approaches aim to assess how changes 
in  the  blockchain  infrastructure  at  the  protocol  level  can  mitigate 
or prevent frontrunning. In contrast, our study focuses on the settle-
ment layer, analyzing the economic implications of private pools—an 
existing solution already implemented in major blockchains. This per-
spective enables us to assess the welfare implications of private pools 
and the allocative efficiency of blockspace.

A related strand of literature examines frictions in blockchain sys-
tems, focusing on different aspects of validator behavior. Chung and 
Shi  (2023)  and  Roughgarden  (2021)  analyze  manipulations  in  the 
transaction fee market, while Biais et al. (2019) investigates fork incen-
tives in proof-of-work blockchains. These studies, while also examining 
validator incentives, differ from our work in two key aspects: they 
abstract away from the frontrunning problem and consider only public 
transaction submission channels.

Our  paper  is  also  related  to  the  literature  on  the  economics  of 
transaction fees (Huberman et al., 2021; Easley et al., 2019), mining 
strategies (Capponi et al., 2023b; Cong et al., 2020; Prat and Walter, 
2021), and consensus protocols (Saleh, 2020; John et al., 2020; Hinzen 
et al., 2022; Bakos and Halaburda, 2021). While some studies have 
analyzed validators’ incentives, block formation mechanisms, and se-
curity implications, our work focuses on the economics of transaction 
submission mechanisms. Moreover, we show that private pools change 
transaction fee bidding and validator strategies as validators, including 
miners, gain the option to monitor private pools.

From  a  broader  perspective,  our  work  contributes  to  the  study 
of market design under frictions. Roth (2008) argues that to achieve 
efficiency, a market needs to provide thickness (Roth et al., 2004), 
overcome congestion (Roth and Xing, 1997), and be safe for partici-
pants (Niederle et al., 2006). These principles apply to the design of 
transaction submission pools for public blockchains, except that agents 
cannot be restrained from certain behavior such as frontrunning, and 
are not forced to participate in new mechanisms, such as private pools. 
We  discuss  how  to  improve  the  market  design  of  private  pools  to 
attain adoption, reduce waste in blockspace, eliminate frontrunning

2. Institutional details

We start this section by highlighting the vulnerability to frontrun-
ning  inherent  in  the  design  of  public  blockchain  settlement  layers. 
We  then  introduce  the  concept  of  private  transaction  pools,  using 
Flashbots  –  a  technological  infrastructure  that  establishes  a  private 
communication  channel  between  Ethereum  users  and  validators  for 
secure transaction submission – as a concrete example.

2.1. Blockchain structure and frontrunning vulnerability

A  blockchain  is  a  decentralized  ledger  technology  operating  on 
a peer-to-peer network. In this system, participants broadcast trans-
actions  that  are  executed  by  validators  and  subsequently  added  to 
blocks. These blocks are then appended to the existing chain, creat-
ing a chronological record of transactions maintained by all network 
participants. The probability of a validator appending the next block 
is proportional to their validation power, which depends on the type 
of  consensus  mechanism  employed.  In  Proof  of  Work  systems,  this 
power is determined by computational capacity, known as hash power, 
whereas in Proof of Stake systems, it is based on the amount of cryp-
tocurrency staked. Typically, the selected validator has sole discretion 
over the content of the next block.

Executing a transaction typically incurs a fee that is paid to the 
validator who incorporates the transaction into a block. The higher 
the fee, the stronger the incentive for the validator to prioritize that 
transaction over others in the queue. This pay-for-priority mechanism 
operates similarly to an auction, where participants bid to have their 
transactions executed first, with validators acting as auctioneers.

Each user must attach a unique sequence number, known as a nonce, 
to every transaction they send. Nonces are essential for maintaining the 
integrity of user accounts by preventing transaction duplication and 
ensuring proper sequential processing. Without this mechanism, the 
same transaction could potentially be executed multiple times, creating 
accounting inconsistencies and enabling unauthorized transfers. The 
execution order of a user’s transaction follows the sequential order of 
their nonces. Specifically, the first transaction a user submits has a 
nonce of 0, and transactions are executed in increasing nonce order. 
The system will not execute two transactions from the same user with 
the  same  nonce,  and  a  transaction  with  nonce  𝑘 + 1  will  only  be 
processed after the transaction with nonce 𝑘 has been executed.

The decentralized nature of blockchain necessitates a unique trans-
action  processing  mechanism.  Each  node  maintains  a  mempool—a 
repository  of  unconfirmed  transactions  awaiting  inclusion  in  the
blockchain.  Due  to  the  random  selection  of  pseudonymous  valida-
tors for transaction execution, users must broadcast their transactions 
broadly  to  ensure  they  are  considered.  However,  this  broadcasting 
mechanism creates an unintended consequence: it enables all network 
participants to see pending transactions before they are executed, thus 
introducing a systematic vulnerability to frontrunning attacks. These 
attacks typically manifest in three primary forms: displacement, sand-
wich, and suppression (Torres et al., 2021). Transactions susceptible to 
these types of attacks are referred to as frontrunnable.

Displacement attacks occur when an attacker observes a profitable 
transaction and broadcasts a similar one with a higher fee. By doing 
so, the attacker secures priority in execution, effectively ‘‘stealing’’ the 

Journal of Financial Economics 172 (2025) 104132 

arbitrage opportunity and diverting the profit away from the original 
user. For example, an arbitrageur aiming to align prices across ex-
changes might find their opportunity exploited by a frontrunner who 
copies the transaction from the mempool and submits it with a higher 
fee to ensure it is executed first.

In a sandwich attack, also known as an insertion attack, the attacker 
places transactions both before and after the victim’s transaction. For 
example, if the attacker spots a market buy order for a token on a 
decentralized exchange, they may place a buy order with a higher fee 
before the victim’s transaction and a sell order immediately after. This 
frontrunning transaction purchases the same token, thus impacting the 
price. Once the victim’s transaction executes, further increasing the 
token’s price, the attacker’s subsequent transaction sells the token at 
this elevated price. This sequence effectively transfers wealth from the 
victim to the attacker, causing slippage for the victim’s trade.7 In a 
similar scenario, if a market maker on a decentralized exchange tries to 
set quotes close to the current rate, a frontrunner can manipulate these 
quotes to a higher price. This manipulation forces liquidity providers 
to adjust their bids and offers upward, allowing the attacker to trade 
against these inflated prices and capitalize on the manipulated price. 
As empirically demonstrated by Torres et al. (2021), sandwich attacks 
constitute the most prevalent type of frontrunning attacks.

In a suppression attack, attackers flood the network with high-fee 
transactions to delay or prevent the inclusion of a specific transaction in 
a block. While this strategy is costly – consuming valuable block space 
– it can be highly profitable in scenarios such as preventing a borrower 
from repaying a loan, thereby triggering a forced and potentially costly 
liquidation.

2.2. Private pools in blockchain

Private pools have emerged as a market-driven solution to reduce 
frontrunning risk in public blockchains. These pools enhance the ex-
isting settlement layer by offering an alternative transaction channel, 
enabling users to bypass the public network and submit transactions 
directly to a selected group of authenticated validators.

Users  submit  their  transactions  to  designated  network  addresses 
provided  by  the  service  providers,  ensuring  that  these  transactions 
are visible only to the collaborating validators and service providers. 
This restricted visibility maintains transaction privacy away from the 
broader  public  blockchain  network,  and  effectively  protects  against 
potential frontrunners.

Collaborating  validators  place  transactions  submitted  to  private 
pools at the top of validated blocks, ensuring they are executed with 
priority. These validators are compensated through transaction fees 
and direct coinbase transfers. Additionally, an important service offered 
within private pools is the simulation of transactions before inclusion, 
allowing validators to screen out those likely to fail or be reverted. 
This pre-validation process helps prevent the inclusion of unprofitable 
transactions—a safeguard not typically available in public pools, where 
failed transactions can still be executed and incur unnecessary user 
costs.

The emergence of private pools is not confined to Ethereum; similar 
services  have  developed  in  other  major  public  blockchain  systems

Ethereum’s shift from Proof-of-Work (PoW) to Proof-of-Stake (PoS), 
known as the ‘‘Merge’’, altered the blockchain’s market structure. This 
transition led to a new division of roles in block production: builders 
and  validators.  Builders  assemble  blocks  by  selecting  transactions, 
while validators primarily focus on choosing the most profitable block 
proposed by builders to add to the chain, a departure from their previ-
ous role of constructing blocks themselves (see Section B of the online 
appendix for details). Consequently, the role of some validators has 
evolved to delegate block construction to these new agents, builders, 
although some continue to construct and validate their own blocks. As 
a result, private pools now mainly facilitate interactions between users 
and builders (Fig.  2), who then pass these transactions to associated 
validators. Furthermore, builders have largely established their own 
private pools, enabling direct user submissions of transactions. This 
restructuring has sparked increased competition among private pool 
service providers (Yang et al., 2022).11

From a user perspective, the network of private pools effectively 
functions as a single large pool, as users are typically incentivized to 
submit transactions to all pools to ensure execution. Similarly, valida-
tors are incentivized to monitor all private pools to maximize block 
rewards when allocating validation power to privately produced blocks. 
This allows us to conceptualize the system as a single private pool for 
analytical purposes, thereby focusing on the fundamental incentives 
for users, validators, and attackers within the blockchain settlement 
layer.12

4.2. Attackers’ pool choice and bidding strategies

To maximize their chances of execution, attackers use both private 
and  public  pools.  The  private  pool  offers  prioritized  execution,  as 
transactions submitted through it are placed at the top of the block 
by participating validators. However, anyone who submits through the 
private pool faces the risk of non-execution, as a public block will not 
contain their transactions. To mitigate this risk, attackers also submit 
transactions to the public pool to ensure execution.

The following proposition characterizes attackers’ equilibrium pool 

selection and fee-bidding strategies.

Proposition 2 (Attackers’ Equilibrium Strategies).  Upon detecting a fron-
trunnable transaction, for 0 < 𝛼 < 1, attackers submit transactions to both 
the private and public pools. In equilibrium:

1. In the private pool, attackers bid a fee equal to their true valuation:

0 and uniformly randomizes on [𝑓 , 𝑐].

Frontrunning opportunities create a common-value environment for 
attackers, as both stand to gain the same amount, 𝑐, by placing their 
transactions ahead of the victim’s. In the private pool, this setting takes 
the form of a sealed-bid, first-price auction with two bidders, each 
possessing a commonly known valuation of 𝑐. A well-known result for 
such auctions with identical and commonly known values is that the 
bidders’ unique best response is to bid exactly their valuation.

If a bidder bids below 𝑐, she risks losing the entire frontrunning 
profit (which is worth 𝑐 if included first). Conversely, if she bids above 
𝑐, any winning payoff would be zero or negative. Hence, in equilibrium, 
both attackers submit a fee of exactly 𝑐 in the private pool.

By  contrast,  in  the  public  pool,  attackers  compete  in  an  all-pay 
format. Here, an attacker who bids a higher transaction fee secures 
higher priority but must pay that fee regardless of whether they win 
or lose to an even higher bid. This structure forces each attacker to 
balance the higher probability of winning (and capturing 𝑐) against the 
risk of incurring a cost without earning any profit. As a result, bidders 
do not simply bid 𝑐 outright: bidding all the way up to 𝑐 at all times 
would involve excessive costs when losing. Instead, in the resulting 
mixed strategy equilibrium, each attacker randomizes over a range of 
possible fee levels, strategically placing probability mass below 𝑐 to 
balance these trade-offs and keep the other attacker indifferent. These 
distinct bidding behaviors are empirically observable and statistically 
validated in our analysis, as detailed in Section 6.

When the frontrunning problem is severe (𝑐𝑝 ≥ 𝑚), the unique 
equilibrium features full adoption of the private pool by validators. In 
this case, frontrunnable users can submit transactions privately without

the private pool, as doing so would mean forgoing the rent extracted

decrease block rewards. Even though there are no upfront investment 
costs for validators to monitor the private pool, this situation resembles 
a hold-up problem. Validators are deterred from fully monitoring the 
private pool because, though socially beneficial, this would primarily 
benefit frontrunnable users at the expense of validators’ revenues.

5.1. Achieving efficiency

Organizational  remedies  commonly  proposed  for  hold-up  prob-
lems    (Klein  et  al.,  1978;  Williamson,  1979)  are  poorly  suited  to 
the blockchain context. The anonymity and decentralization of users 
make integration and negotiations impractical and inconsistent with 
blockchain principles. In contrast, validators and attackers, being more 
centralized and less anonymous, often have stronger incentives for inte-
gration. This is evidenced by instances of validators renting validation 
power to frontrun users on major blockchains, such as Solana.21

A more practical approach to resolving the misalignment of incen-
tives between validators and frontrunnable users is through contractual 
agreements (Edlin and Reichelstein, 1996), where users and validators 
establish  ex-ante  commitments  on  fees.  This  strategy  leverages  the

Proposition 5 (Attaining Full Adoption). There exists 𝑚 ≥ 0 and a function 
𝜃(𝑖) such that if 𝑚 > 𝑚, and frontrunnable users 𝑖 commit ex-ante to pay 
validators a protection fee of 𝜃(𝑖) for executing their transactions within the 
private pool, a full adoption equilibrium is attained, maximizing aggregate 
welfare. Under the full adoption equilibrium, the expected payoff for all 
validators is strictly higher, while the expected payoff for the frontrunnable 
user does not decrease compared to the partial adoption equilibrium.

The pre-commitment mechanism described in the above proposition 
incentivizes full adoption of the private pool by validators, thereby 
maximizing  aggregate  welfare.  With  this  mechanism,  frontrunnable 
users’ expected payoffs either increase or remain unchanged, as they 
now pay a protection fee that is lower than their potential frontrun-
ning  losses,  while  avoiding  being  frontrun  altogether.  Additionally, 
the elimination of attacks reallocates previously wasted blockspace to 
legitimate users.

One possible implementation involves users pre-signing transactions 
with higher fees and sending them to private pools. This approach 
ensures commitment, as validators in the private pool can observe and 
include the original signed transaction, preventing ex-post renegotia-
tion to lower transaction fee. Even if users attempt to submit other 
transactions with the same nonce to revise their choices, validators can 
still include the original transaction, given that it is already signed. 
This  strategy  is  similar  to  the  ex-ante  manipulation  of  bargaining 
power used to address hold-up problems in Chung (1991). Admittedly, 
implementing this pay-for-protection mechanism requires users to be 
sophisticated enough to determine appropriate fee levels 𝜃(𝑖), which 
depend on the composition of order flow and user preferences.

Sample period selection.  We restrict our analysis to the pre-Merge era 
(i.e., before Ethereum’s switch from Proof-of-Work to Proof-of-Stake in 
mid-September 2022). During this period, Flashbots was the dominant 
private pool provider, accounting for over 80% of privately submitted 
transactions according to Yang et al. (2022).24 Flashbots not only dom-
inated market share but also maintained comprehensive transaction 
records, allowing us to directly observe private transaction flows. This 
data availability is crucial because most other private pools do not pub-
licly disclose their transaction data, making activity inference rather 
than direct observation necessary. After the Merge, the proliferation 
of  new  private  pools,  combined  with  their  limited  data  disclosure 
and frequent market entry/exit, makes systematic tracking of private 
transactions  substantially  more  challenging.  These  data  limitations, 
coupled with concurrent changes in market structure, make the pre-
Merge period particularly suitable for analyzing the economic impact 
of private transaction pools.

Transaction data construction.  We construct two complementary trans-
action  datasets  to  analyze  frontrunning  in  decentralized  exchanges 
(DEXs).

Our  primary  dataset  comprises  all  transactions  triggering  swap 
events in Uniswap V2 and Sushiswap smart contracts between May 
2020 and September 2022, totaling 73,372,192 transactions involving 
88,156,404 individual swaps. We focus on swaps involving Ether, the 
native  token  of  Ethereum,  as  transaction  fees  are  denominated  in 
Ether, allowing precise calculation of attackers’ fee-to-revenue ratios 
without cryptocurrency exchange rate complications. Table  2 summa-
rizes key characteristics of these transactions, including their resource

conditions (liquidity pool size).

Classifying transaction submissions.  To analyze how users and attack-
ers interact with private pools, we develop two distinct transaction 
classifications  based  on  our  merged  dataset  of  blockchain  transac-
tions, Flashbots records, and Amberdata mempool observations. The 
first classification distinguishes between private and public submissions 
from the user’s perspective, while the second tracks whether transac-
tions are ultimately executed through Flashbots or traditional mempool 
mechanisms (i.e., Non-Flashbots).

A transaction’s submission type – private or public – is determined 
by how users initially submit it to the network. We classify a transaction 
as private if the user submits it exclusively through private channels 
like Flashbots, never exposing it to the public mempool. Conversely, 
transactions first appearing in the public mempool are classified as pub-
lic, as their details become visible to all network participants, including 
potential attackers. To implement this classification, we cross-reference 
Flashbots transaction records with Amberdata’s comprehensive mem-
pool  data  (available  from  block  11,278,560  onward).  Of  the  fron-
trunnable transactions we identify, 122,415 were submitted privately, 
while 4,436,242 appeared first in the public mempool.

Separately, we classify transactions based on their execution chan-
nel (Flashbots or non-Flashbots). A transaction is labeled as Flashbots 
if it is ultimately included in a Flashbots bundle,27 regardless of its 
submission type. Notably, many public transactions end up in Flashbots 
bundles when attackers spot them in the mempool and incorporate 
them into their frontrunning strategies.

These  classifications  serve  distinct  purposes.  The  private/public 
submission  distinction  is  essential  for  understanding  user  behavior, 
particularly  how  users  strategically  protect  vulnerable  transactions. 
In contrast, the Flashbots/non-Flashbots execution distinction is more 
relevant for analyzing attacker behavior, as Flashbots bundles operate 
through  a  sealed-bid  auction  format  that  influences  how  attackers 
compete  and  bid.  When  attackers  execute  through  Flashbots,  they 
must adhere to these auction rules, regardless of whether the target 
transaction was submitted publicly or privately.

Identifying  frontrunning  attacks.  We  analyze  our  primary  dataset  to 
identify realized frontrunning attacks, focusing on sandwich attacks—
the most prevalent form of frontrunning in DEXs. Following Wang et al. 
(2022), we identify attack patterns by examining transaction sequences 
within blocks, requiring: (i) consecutive transactions in the same liq-
uidity pool, (ii) matching transaction sizes between the opening and 
closing legs of the attack, and (iii) directional alignment between the 
victim’s trade and the attacker’s opening position. This methodology 
reveals 703,990 attacked transactions, with 239,075 executed through 
Flashbots bundles.

Panel A of Table  4 presents statistics on realized attacks, distinguish-
ing between those executed via Flashbots (Panel A1) and the public 
mempool (Panel A2). The distribution of attack revenues shows signif-
icant right-skew: while the median revenue through Flashbots is 0.045 
ETH, the 99th percentile exceeds 1.45 ETH. Notably, attacks executed 
through Flashbots typically yield lower revenues but incur higher costs, 
consistent with attackers using private channels for smaller or riskier 
exploits (avoiding fees for failed attempts).

6.2. Evidence of partial adoption equilibrium

Our  theoretical  model  predicts  two  possible  equilibria  following 
the introduction of private transaction pools: full adoption or partial 
adoption. We empirically investigate these predictions by analyzing 
the adoption of Flashbots, which was introduced on January 1, 2021. 
Fig.   4  presents  the  daily  adoption  rate  of  Flashbots,  measured  as 
the proportion of total daily blocks containing transactions submitted 
through Flashbots private pools.

The data reveal two distinct phases in Flashbots adoption:

1. Initial adjustment (January 1, 2021–May 28, 2021): This period 

is characterized by rapid growth in adoption.

2. Stabilization (May 29, 2021–September 14, 2022): The adoption 
rate stabilizes at approximately 50%, providing strong evidence 
for a partial adoption equilibrium. This stable period persists for 
nearly 16 months.

The stabilization at approximately 50% adoption during the second 
phase is more consistent with the partial adoption equilibrium of our 
model rather than the full adoption equilibrium.

The largest miner, Spark Pool, which mined 24.3% of blocks during

of blocks, followed suit on April 23, 2021, at block 12,297,642. The

the impact of private pool adoption on validator payoffs.

Our primary identifying assumption is that the nineteen-day differ-

random, considering their comparable market positions and the emerg-

us to consider the adoption event as a quasi-experiment.

2.  Triple  Difference  (DDD):  We  take  advantage  of  the  fact  that 
Spark Pool, shortly after adopting Flashbots, continued to produce some

6.4. User adoption of private pools

Our theoretical model predicts that users facing higher expected 
losses from frontrunning or a higher probability of being attacked are 
more likely to use private pools. We empirically validate this hypothesis 
using transaction-level data from the Ethereum blockchain, covering 
block 11,550,019 (January 2021, when Flashbots was introduced) to 
block 13,804,681 (December 2021). The start date marks the earliest 
point at which users could choose between public and private pools. We 
end our sample in December 2021 due to a major infrastructural change 
in Uniswap: the introduction of Auto Router V2,29 which replaced the 
Uniswap V2 router contracts we track. While a few transactions through 
the original Uniswap V2 router still continued after this date, they were 
predominantly automated trades by arbitrage bots and legacy protocol 
integrations, rather than typical user transactions. This shift makes the 
later period less suitable for analyzing users’ pool choice decisions.

6.5. Impact of private pool on attackers’ fee-to-revenue ratios

We examine the impact of private pools on attackers’ fee-to-revenue 
ratios. Our analysis begins with a visual examination of the distribution 
of these ratios over time, followed by a transaction-level regression 
analysis.

6.5.1. Visual evidence

Fig.  7 presents the quarterly distribution of the fee-to-revenue ratio 
for sandwich attacks submitted through public mempools (Panel (a)) 
and  private  pools  (Panel  (b)).  For  sandwich  transactions  submitted 
through the public mempool, we observe substantial variation in the 
fee-to-revenue ratio. The median ratio fluctuates and is slightly higher 
in the post-Flashbots period, while the interquartile range remains wide 
throughout the sample period. This pattern aligns with our model’s 
prediction for the public mempool, where the fee-to-revenue ratio is 
expected to be below 1 on average.

In contrast, for sandwich transactions submitted through the private 
pool, the fee-to-revenue ratio quickly concentrates around 1, particu-
larly after the initial adoption period of Flashbots. The interquartile 
range narrows significantly, and the median consistently approaches 
1. This closely matches our model’s prediction for the private pool, 
where the sealed-bid, first-price auction format is expected to drive the 
fee-to-revenue ratio close to 1.

mechanisms in the blockchain settlement layer expose users to system-
atic and costly frontrunning attacks. These attacks not only cause direct 
losses for affected users but also waste block space and computational 
resources, leading to significant allocative inefficiencies. This vulnera-

an efficient and fair DeFi ecosystem, potentially undermining the entire

critical flaw, the vision of a robust and equitable DeFi ecosystem on

Our  paper  tackles  this  critical  challenge  by  developing  a  game-
theoretical model to assess the benefits and limitations of augmenting 
public blockchains with private transaction pools—a leading solution 
proposed  to  mitigate  frontrunning  risk.  We  demonstrate  that  while 
private pools can enhance allocative efficiency by shielding some users 
from frontrunning and increasing the inclusion of valuable transactions, 
they do not fully resolve the underlying issues. Our analysis uncovers 
a persistent misalignment of incentives between validators and users, 
resembling a hold-up problem, where validators are reluctant to fully 
adopt private pools due to their desire to capture MEV. This reluctance 
leads to a partial adoption equilibrium that deviates from the welfare-
maximizing outcome. Moreover, our empirical analysis confirms that 
frontrunning attacks persist even after the introduction of private pools.
We  emphasize  that  effective  solutions  must  leverage  the  unique 
strengths of blockchain technology, particularly its ability to ensure 
credible commitment. Our proposed approach, which involves ex-ante 
contractual agreements between users and validators, exemplifies this 
principle.

More  complex  market-based  solutions,  such  as  those  involving 
additional agents like executors, may fall short of their intended goals. 
For  instance,  the  Flashbots  proposal  (see  Flashbots,  2022),  which 
introduces  executors  to  participate  in  auctions  for  order  flows  and 
blockspace, heavily relies on the assumption of perfect competition 
among these sophisticated agents. However, in practice, such designs 
may inadvertently lead to new forms of centralization and rent extrac-
tion, as seen in recent implementations like Proposer Builder Separation 
(Capponi et al., 2024). Economically, these sophisticated, centralized 
entities often tend towards vertical integration, which enhances their 
competitive edge but further undermines the assumption of perfect 
competition  (Gupta et al., 2023).

Despite  these  challenges,  there  is  promise  in  technological  ap-
proaches that focus on improving communication mechanisms among 
nodes. These include using zero-knowledge proof technology to en-
crypt the mempool, implementing fair sequencing mechanisms to mod-
ify transaction ordering (Yang et al., 2022), or developing verifiable 
sequencing  rules  that  provide  execution  price  guarantees  for  users 
(Ferreira and Parkes, 2023).
