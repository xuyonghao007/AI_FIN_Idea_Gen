1. Introduction

Several influential studies show that transformations of implied volatilities calculated from options prices predict 
stock returns. This predictability is puzzling because market participants readily observe options prices. We find 
that  this  predictability  is  consistent  with  implied  volatilities  reflecting  stock  borrow  fees  that  are  known  to 
predict  stock  returns.  We  derive  a  formula  relating  the  option-implied  volatility  spread  to  the  borrow  fee. 
Motivated by this relation, we show that the return predictability from implied volatility spread and skew de-
creases by at least two-thirds if high-fee stocks are excluded. The patterns for other predictors computed from 
option implied volatilities are similar.

The options market’s informational role has drawn attention since 
the inception of listed options trading (Black, 1975; Back, 1993; Biais 
and Hillion, 1994; Easly et al., 1998; Chakravarty et al., 2004; Hu, 2014, 
2018). An important finding in the empirical options literature is that 
transformations of options prices, including option implied volatilities, 
predict stock returns over one to several weeks. Bali and Hovakimian 
(2009) and Cremers and Weinbaum (2010) (CW) show that the volatility 
spread, the difference between the implied volatilities of calls and puts 

with the same strike price and expiration date, predicts the cross-section 
of stock returns. Xing et al. (2010) (XZZ) show that the volatility skew, 
defined  as  the  difference  between  the  implied  volatility  of  an 
out-of-the-money (OTM) put and an at-the-money (ATM) call with the 
same expiration date, also predicts of stock returns. These predictors are 
closely related because the volatility skew can be decomposed into the 
volatility  spread  and  the  difference  between  the  OTM  and  ATM  put 
implied  volatilities.  Research  has  also  identified  other  options-based 
predictors, many derived from implied volatilities.1

that  stock  prices  are  not  efficient  with  respect  to  the  information  in 
options prices, as they only slowly reflect that information.2 While there 
are  compelling  reasons  to  expect  informed  trading  in  options  (Black, 
1975;  Easly  et  al.,  1998),  the  finding  that  it  takes  several  weeks  for 
options  information  to  be  fully  reflected  in  stock  prices  is  puzzling 
because it suggests that stock market investors can earn large abnormal 
returns by following simple trading strategies based on readily available 
options data. What prevents investors, who actively trade in both mar-
kets and can easily access this information, from arbitraging away the 
predictive power of option prices for stock returns?

We propose an alternative hypothesis that the volatility spread and 
skew predict stock returns because they proxy for the stock borrow fee 
paid by short sellers. This fee is one of the strongest predictors of returns 
(Drechsler and Drechsler, 2021; see also Jones and Lamont, 2002; Cohen 
et al., 2007; Muravyev et al., 2022; Engelberg et al., 2025). The stock 
borrow fee is also a limit to arbitrage that prevents the information in 
the option signal from being promptly reflected in stock prices. That is, 
the  cost  of  borrowing  stock  to  sell  short  precludes  sophisticated  in-
vestors,  such  as  hedge  funds,  from  profiting  from  the  apparent  mis-
pricing.  First,  we  derive  a  simple  relation  showing  that  the  implied 
volatility  spread  based  on  implied  volatilities  that  ignore  the  stock 
borrow fee is proportional to the borrow fee. Then, we present various 
results that support our hypothesis.

Stock borrow fees are paid by short sellers and received by long-side 
investors  who  lend their shares.  The fees are  analogous to dividends, 
which decrease the returns to short sellers and increase the returns to 
long-side investors. Like dividends, they are reflected in options prices. 
In fact, Ofek et al. (2004) show a strong correlation between put-call 
parity  violations  and  the  cost  of  short  selling.  Extending  their  work, 
Muravyev et al. (2022) show that option-implied borrow fees match the 
actual fees on average. Options market makers incorporate these fees 
directly  into  their  pricing  models  through  the  cost-of-carry,  using 
borrow  fee  data  from  their  prime  brokers.  However,  academic  re-
searchers have typically used option implied volatilities from vendors 
such  as  OptionMetrics  computed  assuming  a  borrow  fee  of  zero. 
Ignoring non-zero borrow fees creates the same pricing error as omitting 
dividends. This error distorts call and put implied volatilities in opposite 
directions,  affecting  the  entire  implied  volatility  surface.  Thus,  the 
implied volatility spread and skew, calculated from these flawed implied 
volatilities, correlate strongly with borrow fees.

Using  a  simple  Taylor  expansion,  we  show  that  put-call  implied 
volatility spreads at equal strikes are proportional to the omitted borrow 
fee  when  volatilities  are  computed  assuming  zero  borrow  fees.  This 
result, together with the finding that the fee is a strong predictor of stock 
returns,  suggests  that  the  volatility  spread  and  skew  predict  stock 
returns  because  they  proxy  for  the  borrow  fee.  This  relationship  also 
enables  extracting option-implied  borrow fees  from volatility calcula-
tions that assume zero fees.

Our  empirical  analyses  begin  by  examining  whether  the  volatility 
spread and skew predict stock returns in our 2006–2020 sample using 
decile portfolio sorts. The volatility spread is a strong predictor, with the 
long-short  portfolio  (decile  ten  minus  decile  one)  earning  significant 
returns  of  64–66  basis  points  per  month,  using  either  raw  returns  or 
abnormal returns based on Daniel et al. (1997) (DGTW) benchmarks. 
Sorting based on the volatility skew results in statistically significant but 
modestly smaller returns, consistent with the skew being a noisy esti-
mate of the spread. For both option-implied variables, the predictability 
is  concentrated  in  the  decile  one  portfolios,  which  exhibit  negative 
abnormal returns and would be shorted to exploit them. In contrast, the

Excluding high-fee stocks reveals that predictability stems primarily 
from  borrowing  costs.  Abnormal  returns  in  the  first  decile  of  both 
spread-  and  skew-sorted  portfolios  fall  to  less  than  one-tenth  of  their 
original levels and become insignificant. Long-short portfolios exhibit 
similar,  though  less  severe,  declines.  This  dramatic  performance 
reduction occurs despite removing only 11% of our sample—stocks with 
annual  borrow  fees  greater  than  1%.  If  spread  and  skew  strategies 
captured any predictive signal beyond borrowing costs, eliminating this 
small subset of high-fee stocks should have little impact on performance.
Prior  research  has  identified  additional  option-based  predictors 
derived from either implied volatilities or moments of the risk-neutral 
return distribution (Rehman and Vilkov, 2012; An et al., 2014; Huang 
and  Li,  2019; Tang, 2019;  Stilger et  al., 2017; Baltussen  et al.,  2018; 
Ilhan  et  al.,  2021).  Similar  to  CW  and  XZZ,  these  papers  generally 
interpret their results as evidence of stock market inefficiency.4

These predictors help us further test whether options implied vola-
tility predicts returns by reflecting stock borrow fee information. First, 
because these predictors are less directly connected to the borrow fee, 
we expect them to be weaker predictors of returns than the volatility 
spread and skew. Second, these predictors provide additional portfolios 
to test if the abnormal returns survive adjusting for borrow fees, and we 
expect their fee-adjusted returns to be small.

This is what we find. Before adjusting for fees, these additional sig-
nals are weaker predictors than the spread and skew. Adjusting for fees, 
the average fee-adjusted returns on both the decile one portfolios and 
the long-short portfolios are small, and not statistically significant. Even 
the strongest among these predictors produce statistically insignificant 
fee-adjusted abnormal returns: (cid:0) 12 basis points (t-statistic (cid:0) 1.24) for 
decile one portfolios and 27 basis points (t-statistic 1.65) for long-short 
portfolios.

Martin  and  Wagner  (2019) and  Chabi-Yo  et  al.  (2023) propose 
options-based bounds on expected stock returns, and Goyal and Saretto 
(2009) introduce  the  volatility  risk  premium  (the  difference  between 
implied  and  realized  volatilities).  Unlike  volatility  spread  and  skew, 
these predictors could be considered risk-based and their relationship 
with borrow fees is theoretically ambiguous. Our sample provides mixed 
evidence about the performance of these predictors, before adjusting for 
fees.  Regardless,  most  of  the  abnormal  returns  associated  with  these 
predictors survive adjusting for borrow fees.

Our results indicate that the stock return predictability using implied 
volatility information is not exploitable by investors after accounting for 
borrow  fees.  Volatility  spread  and  skew  sorting  strategies  generate 
minimal profits net of fees and become unprofitable after institutional 
trading costs. The strategies also are not profitable in the subsample of 
low-fee stocks after taking account of trading costs. These results pro-
vide little support for the market segmentation hypothesis, which posits 
that stock prices slowly incorporate information from a more efficient 
options market.

Other  factors  may  explain  the  presence  of  the  volatility  skew,  for 
example,  stochastic  volatility  and  stock  price  jumps.  Also,  limits  to 
arbitrage,  such  as  wide  options  bid-ask  spreads,  limited  capital,  and 
inventory risk, could contribute to slow information diffusion between 
options  and  stocks.  The  volatility  spread  and  skew  reflect  both  the 
borrow fee and these alternative factors. If these other considerations 
are  strongly  associated  with  stock  return  predictability,  then  simply 
accounting for the borrow fee should not so dramatically attenuate the 
long-short portfolio performance. Thus, the substantial abnormal per-
formance  of  these  strategies  using  conventional  implied  volatilities 
largely reflects the borrow fee.

Our  finding  that  abnormal  returns  concentrate  in  short  positions 
differs  from  some  findings  by  CW  and  XZZ,  who  document  profits  in 
both long and short legs. While limited borrow fee data prevents direct 
analysis  before  July  2006,  we  develop  a  proxy  using  option-implied 
borrowing costs and find results during the 1996–2006 period that are 
largely similar to the main results during the 2006–2020 period. The 
abnormal performance is concentrated in portfolio one for both spread- 
and skew-sorted portfolios. These returns attenuate considerably after 
adjusting for implied borrow fees. Any remaining differences between 
the  1996–2006  period  and  2006–2020  period  may  reflect  either 
increased  cross-market  efficiency  or  measurement  noise  in  option 
implied  fees.  Regardless,  we  find  that  the  differences  in  abnormal 
returns  are  concentrated  on  the  short  side  in  this  earlier  period,  and 
cannot readily be exploited after paying the implied borrow fee.

Our evidence is consistent with an equilibrium in which the options 
and stock markets are connected via the stock lending market. In such an 
equilibrium,  the  finding  that  implied  volatility  information  predicts 
stock  returns  could  be  consistent  with  the  hypothesis  that  informed 
traders exploit the options market, as discussed in CW and XZZ. How-
ever, it is also consistent with the hypothesis that short sellers’  infor-
mation  about  the  stock  return  is  reflected  in  the  borrow  fee,  which 
mechanically feeds into options prices. That abnormal returns largely 
vanish after fee adjustment suggests that options signals mainly reflect 
borrowing costs.

Several  observations  support  this  equilibrium.  First,  informed  in-
vestors can trade in the options market, the stock market, or both, and 
information  flows  rapidly  between  the  two  markets.  Second,  option 
order imbalances are rapidly transmitted to the stock market via market 
makers’ delta hedge trades (Hu, 2014) and options exchange data feeds 
(Muravyev, 2016). Third, option market makers rely on autoquotation 
algorithms that quickly respond to stock price changes, creating tight 
cross-market linkages (Muravyev et al., 2013).

2. Stock borrow fees and options implied volatilities

In this section, we analyze how neglecting the stock borrow fee af-
fects implied volatility calculations. We begin by describing the market 
for borrowing stock and the borrow fee, and then explore their impact 
on implied volatility estimation.

2.1. The market for borrowing stock and the stock borrow fee

D’Avolio (2002) and Kolasinski et al. (2013) describe the market for 
borrowing stock. It includes three groups of participants: (i) lenders such 
as  mutual  funds,  pension  funds,  and  insurance  companies,  some  of 
which lend through agent lenders (custodians), (ii) ultimate borrowers, 
for example, hedge funds, proprietary trading desks, and option market 
makers,  and  (iii)  prime  brokers.  Typically,  hedge  funds  and  option 
market makers borrow the securities from their prime brokers, who in 
turn  borrow  from  mutual  funds,  pension  funds,  and  other  ultimate 
lenders (Kolasinski et al., 2013; Fig. 1).

The  prime  brokers  “mark  up”  the  fee,  i.e.  they  borrow  from  the 
original lender and then relend to the short seller at a higher fee. The 
borrow fee is not usually quoted directly but is derived from the quoted 
rebate rate. The security borrower usually provides cash collateral to the 
security lender, and the security lender pays interest, the rebate rate, on 
the collateral. The rebate rate is typically less than the short-term in-
terest rate, which makes borrowing shares costly. The borrow fee is the 
difference between the market short-term interest rate and the rebate 
rate paid on the cash collateral.5 The borrow fee can, and sometimes 
does, exceed the short-term interest rate because the rebate rate can be 
negative.6 It accrues daily whenever a stock loan is open, like the way 
interest  accrues.  Stock  loans  almost  always  cover  only  one  overnight 
period, with the borrow fee potentially changing from day to day.7

Because the borrow fee is analogous to a dividend, it is reflected in 
options prices, in the same way that dividends are reflected in options 
prices. This occurs through the valuation models used by options market 
makers, as the borrow fee is included as a model input through the cost 
of carry.8 The borrow fee is also reflected in implied volatilities that are 
computed  ignoring  it,  and  can  be  inferred  or  “implied”  from  such 
volatilities.9

Specifically, we use a Taylor expansion to show that if one computes 
call and put implied volatilities while treating the borrow fee as zero, the 
resulting implied volatility spread is proportional to the omitted borrow 
fee. The expansion also shows that the borrow fee implicit in options 
prices, that is the option-implied borrow fee, can be readily computed 
from the implied volatilities computed by setting the fee to zero.

For simplicity, we assume that the options are of the European type, 
the stock price follows geometric Brownian motion, the interest rate is 
constant, the borrow fee is a constant rate continuously paid to share-
holders,  and  no  dividends  are  paid  before  option  expiration.  Like  a 
continuous dividend, the borrow fee lowers the expected return on the 
stock under the risk-neutral measure below the risk-free rate.

We illustrate the proposed approach for computing implied fees with 
two examples. The solid red line in Fig. 1, Panel A shows the five-day 
moving  average  of  the  daily  estimates  of  Tesla’s  option-implied

the daily borrow fee from Markit (dashed black line). The estimate of the 
implied  borrow  fee  himplied  tracks  the  Markit  borrow  fee  very  closely.

the  30-day  volatility  spread  taken  from  the  OptionMetrics  volatility 
surfaces  clearly  embeds  the  borrow  fee.  In  this  case,  the  very  close 
relation between the five-day moving average of himplied  and the vola-
tility spread suggests that the 30-day volatility spread reflects little other 
than the borrow fee. Indeed, the correlation between the two variables 
in Panel A is 0.97.

tems. The estimate of Factset’s implied borrow fee displays considerable 
“noise” stemming from wide options bid-ask spreads, especially during 
the second half of 2011 and the first half of 2012. For this reason, the 
implied fee tracks the borrow fee less closely for Factset than for Tesla. 
Nevertheless, there is still a strong relation between the two series. The 
patterns  in  Fig.  1 for  Tesla  and  Factset  suggest  that  option-implied 
borrow  fees  computed  from  put  and  call  implied  volatility  surfaces 
using Eq. (6) provide excellent proxies for borrow fees.

A researcher who wants to construct a proxy for the borrow fee can 
apply  Eq.  (6) to  the  at-the-money  implied  volatilities  from  implied

maturity  determines  the  horizon  of  the  estimated  borrow  fee.  For 
example,  using  30-day  ATM  implied  volatilities  will  yield  a  market- 
based estimate of the average borrow fee over the next 30 days.

3.1. Stock borrow fees

We obtain the borrow fees and other data about stock borrowing and 
lending from the Markit Securities Finance Buy Side Analytics Data Feed 
available from Markit, Ltd. (now S&P Global). This database includes 
daily  data  on  securities  borrowing  and  lending  activity,  including  re-
bates and borrow (loan) fees, the quantity on loan, the number of loans, 
the number of active brokers and lending agents, and other data. Markit 
obtains  the  information  from  more  than 100  equity  loan  market  par-
ticipants, including beneficial owners, hedge funds, investment banks, 
lending agents, and prime brokers, who together account for approxi-
mately  85%  of  US  securities  loans  (Markit,  2012).  While  the  Markit 
dataset includes a broader range of securities, we focus on the subset of 
U.S. equities with listed options. Our sample begins in July 2006 because 
the data coverage expanded significantly around that time and the data 
are available at daily frequency beginning June 28, 2006. The sample 
ends in December 2020.

The  market  structure  in  which  prime  brokers  are  typically  the 
financial intermediaries implies that there are two fees, a buy-side fee 
paid by  the ultimate borrower (for example, a  hedge fund or options 
market maker) and a lender-side fee received by the ultimate lender (for 
example, a mutual fund), which is lower than the buy-side fee. We use 
the “IndicativeFee,” which is a buy-side fee. Specifically, it is Markit’s 
estimate of the “The expected borrow cost, in fee terms, for a hedge fund 
on a given day,”  based on “both borrow costs between Agent Lenders 
and  Prime  Brokers  as  well  as  rates  from  hedge  funds  to  produce  an 
indication  of  the  current  market  rate”  (Markit,  2012).11 To  evaluate 
trading strategies, it is important to use the borrow fee paid by institu-
tional investors rather than the fee received by lenders, as this reflects

The Markit data also include the quantity on loan (QuantityOnLoan) 
and the utilization rate (Utilization), defined as the ratio of the quantity 
on  loan  to  the  lendable  quantity  (LendableQuantity).  Utilization  is  a 
component of the portfolio performance adjustment related to the stock 
borrow fee described in Section 4.2.

3.2. Options and stock market data

Most  of  the  signals  we  use  are  computed  from  end-of-day  option 
implied volatilities obtained from the OptionMetrics Option Prices files 
or the OptionMetrics Volatility Surface files. This is consistent with the 
empirical options literature, which relies primarily on the OptionMetrics 
data. We either compute the signals ourselves, or obtain them from other 
researchers who shared their signals with us. A few signals are computed 
from  other  options  market  information,  for  example,  options  trading 
volume  and  end-of-day  bid-ask  spreads,  also  from  the  OptionMetrics 
database.

When  we  compute  the  signals,  we  use  the  version  of  the  Option-
Metrics  database  that  was  available  during  May  2023.  OptionMetrics 
changed how they compute implied volatilities to adjust for the stock 
borrow fee in a version of their database released during July 2024, due 
to  an  earlier  version  of  this  paper.12 OptionMetrics  now  computes 
option-implied  borrow  fees  and  incorporates  them  into  implied  vola-
tility calculations. We use an earlier version of the database because the 
existing literature that uses options market data to predict stock returns 
relies on OptionMetrics data predating July 2024. Thus, our use of the 
earlier data allows our results to directly relate to prior research.

Stock prices, returns, and some other data are from the Center for 
Research in Securities Prices (CRSP) files. We address NASDAQ delisting 
returns as in Shumway and Warther (1999).

3.3. Options-based predictors

We consider 17 predictors (Table 1), grouped by their connection to 

the stock borrow fee.

The  first  group  consists  of  the  predictors  of  main  interest—the 
volatility spread and skew. These are differences between call and put 
implied volatilities and thus are directly connected to the stock borrow 
fee. We use two versions of each of these predictors. Our main results for 
the implied volatility spread use the difference between the 30-day ATM 
(50-delta) call and put volatilities taken from the OptionMetrics Vola-
tility Surface files. For the second version of the spread, we follow CW 
and compute it as the open-interest weighted average of the difference 
between the OptionMetrics call and put implied volatilities across put- 
call  pairs,  where  the  implied  volatilities  are  from  the  Option  Price 
files.13 The  results  for  the  two  versions  of  the  volatility  spread  are 
similar.

Our main results for the implied volatility skew use the difference 
between the 30-day implied volatilities of an ATM (50-delta) call and an 
OTM (25-delta) put, with the volatilities taken from the OptionMetrics 
Volatility Surface files. We compute the second version of the volatility 
skew as the difference between the average implied volatility of ATM 
calls with moneyness K/S satisfying 0.95 < K/S < 1.05 and the average 
implied volatility of OTM puts with moneyness satisfying 0.7 < K/S <
0.95, where the implied volatilities are from the OptionMetrics Option 
Price files. This is the negative of the measure used by XZZ. We use the 
negated measure so that stocks with greater values of the measure are 
expected  to  have  higher  performance,  consistent  with  the  other  pre-
dictors in this paper.

We focus on the versions of the spread and skew computed from the 
implied volatility surfaces because the XZZ version of the skew is not 
available  for  many  stock-dates.  This  occurs  whenever  options  in  the 
required moneyness ranges are not available. The skew computed from 
the implied volatility surfaces is also a stronger predictor of stock returns 
before adjusting for the borrow fee than the XZZ version, and we think it 
better to focus on the stronger predictor.

The  second  group  consists  of  other  predictors  that  either  are 
computed from implied volatilities or are moments of the risk-neutral 
distribution of stock returns that capture aspects of how implied vola-
tilities differ across strike prices. These predictors, while not based on 
call-put implied volatility differences, likely correlate with the borrow 
fee since it affects both option prices and implied volatility calculations 
in pre-July 2024 OptionMetrics data.

The first three predictors in the second group are due to An et al. 
(2014). They are the monthly change in the 30-day ATM call implied 
volatility, the monthly change in the 30-day ATM put implied volatility, 
and the difference between these two monthly changes. The predictor 
from Baltussen et al. (2018), volatility-of-volatility, is the standard de-
viation of the 30-day ATM call implied volatility over the month ending 
on the portfolio formation date, divided by the sum of the 30-day ATM 
call implied volatility on the portfolio formation date and 0.02 (to avoid 
division by near zero). Variance asymmetry is the difference between 
upside  and  downside  risk-neutral  semivariances  computed  from  the 
prices of OTM calls and puts, respectively (Huang and Li, 2019; Tang, 
2019). We use the difference between the implied volatilities of 30-day 
10-delta  calls  and  puts  to  proxy  for  this  measure.  We  compute  these 
measures  using  volatilities  taken  from  the  OptionMetrics  Volatility 
Surface  files.  Ex  ante  skewness  is  the  third  central  moment  of  the 
risk-neutral distribution normalized by the risk-neutral variance raised

measured in underlying shares, divided by stock trading volume (from 
CRSP), where both volumes are computed over the week through the 
portfolio formation date. We also impose options maturity and options 
trading activity filters.15 The second is a measure of options illiquidity 
(Goyenko and Zhang, 2022), computed as the open interest-weighted 
average of end-of-day options bid-ask spreads, where the bid and ask 
prices are from the OptionMetrics Option Price file. To avoid outliers for 
the spread calculation, we exclude option contracts with a bid price of 
less than ten cents and a spread exceeding 200% on a given day.

predictors show much weaker correlations with the fee (largest magni-
tude –0.083), reflecting their indirect connection to the borrow fee.

The  Martin  and  Wagner  (2019) bound  on  the  expected  return  is 
positively  correlated  with  the  borrow  fee,  while  the  Chabi-Yo  et  al. 
(2023) bound on the risk premium is not. The O/S ratio is positively 
correlated with the borrow fee, which is to be expected given the ar-
guments in Johnson and So (2012), and is likely related to the result that 
the  O/S  ratio  predicts  returns.  The  option  bid-ask  spread  correlates 
strongly  with  the  XZZ  volatility  skew  (0.232),  but  less  so  with  the 
volatility surface skew.

Figs.  2  and  3 illustrate  the  close  relations  between  the  volatility 
spread and skew and the borrow fee. Panel A (B) of Fig. 2 displays a five- 
day moving average of Tesla’s daily volatility spread (skew) together 
with its stock borrow fee during our sample period. The spread and skew

with Tesla showing closer alignment than Factset due to its more liquid 
options market and resulting less noise in the option-implied measures. 
Nevertheless,  the  correspondence  is  still  quite  strong  for  both  com-
panies, consistent with the theoretical analysis in Section 2.2.

4.1. Unadjusted returns of portfolios sorted by volatility spread and skew

In these analyses, we begin by sorting stocks into decile portfolios 
based on the variable of interest as of the close of trading day t. Stocks 
are held from the close of trading day t + 1 until the close of trading day t 
+ 22, to mimic the length of a typical month with 21 trading days. The 
approach of starting the return interval at the close of trading on date t +

the portfolios and the evaluation period for the portfolio return. This

and  non-tradable  price  difference  generates  substantial  stock  return 
predictability the next day according to Goncalves-Pinto et al. (2020)
and Bogousslavsky and Muravyev (2023).

4.2. Abnormal returns of portfolios sorted by the volatility spread

We  compute  the  abnormal  returns  by  following  DGTW  and  sub-
tracting  from  each  stock  return  the  return  on  a  portfolio  of  stocks 
matched  on  book-to-market,  past  six-month  return,  and  market  capi-
talization, after first excluding the high-fee stocks from those used to 
construct the benchmarks. We exclude the high-fee stocks because the 
borrow fees affect not only the options-based trading strategies but also 
the  benchmark  returns.  Thus,  we  cannot  use  off-the-shelf  benchmark 
returns but must rebuild them using only stocks with low borrow fees. 
This crucial step is much easier to implement for the DGTW benchmarks 

than for alternative approaches.17

4.5. Extending the sample backward in time

Our sample begins in July 2006 because the Markit data are available 
at daily frequency beginning near the end of June 2006. We extend the 
sample backward in time by computing option-implied borrow fees from 
the volatility surface, and using the option-implied fees in place of the 
Markit indicative fees to fee-adjust portfolio performance. Specifically, 
we use Eq. (6), together with 30-day ATM call and put implied volatil-
ities from the OptionMetrics Volatility Surface files, to compute daily 
option-implied stock borrow fees starting from January 1996, the first 
month  for  which  the  OptionMetrics  data  are  available.  We  use  these 
option-implied borrow fees in place of the indicative fees to compute 
fee-adjusted returns, recognizing that this implied fee is a noisy estimate 
of the daily borrow fee that we do not observe. We perform this analysis 
for the period from January 1996 through June 2006.

To  implement  this  approach,  we  alter  the  way  we  construct  our 
DGTW-based benchmark portfolios. Without the Markit data, we cannot 
easily  exclude  stocks  with  a  high  borrow  fee  from  the  conventional 
DGTW benchmarks that we use to compute abnormal returns elsewhere 
in  this  paper.  From  a  practical  perspective,  there  are  relatively  few 

optionable stocks at the start of the sample period in 1996, and so the 
number of stocks that would be classified as having a high borrow fee in 
the  DGTW  benchmark  portfolios  is  initially  quite  low.  In  addition,  it 
would be a mistake to exclude individual stocks from the benchmark 
portfolios  using  a  noisy  estimate  of  the  borrow  fee  on  a  daily  basis 
throughout the period.

As shown in Fig. 1 Panel B, the implied borrow fee contains mean- 
zero noise relative to actual borrow fees. While Factset’s indicative fee 
remains  below  1%  (but  is  positive)  for  most  of  the  latter  part  of  the 
sample period, the implied fee fluctuates substantially around it, often 
exceeding 1% or falling below zero. Using this noisy fee to determine 
benchmark  portfolio  membership  would  create  unstable  constituent 
sets. Therefore, we use conventional DGTW benchmarks for all stocks, 
including  those  with  high  borrow  fees,  avoiding  daily  benchmark 
changes  due  to  estimation  noise.  The  implied  fee’s  noise  poses  less 
concern for computing fee-adjusted returns, as mean-zero noise does not 
bias portfolio return estimates, and the noise in the fees for individual 
stocks gets averaged out across portfolio stocks each month and over 
time.

6. Discussion

Our results show that the implied volatility spread and skew predicts 
stock returns, but this predictability is not readily exploitable. The re-
turn predictability is concentrated in the first decile of the spread-sorted 
and skew-sorted portfolios, and so are the high-fee stocks. Due to this, 
the results reported in Tables 4 through 6 indicate that trading strategies 
based on sorting stocks using the volatility spread and skew offer at most 
limited profitability after accounting for the borrow fees paid by short 
sellers. After adjusting for fees, the decile one spread-sorted  portfolio 
shows an insignificant (cid:0) 0.06% abnormal return, while the long-short 
portfolio yields an insignificant 0.10%. Similarly, the fee-adjusted first 
decile  skew-sorted  portfolio  returns  an  insignificant (cid:0) 0.08%,  with  its 
long-short  portfolio  returning  an  insignificant  0.17%.  The  average 
abnormal return on the low-fee stocks in the first decile spread-sorted 
(skew-sorted)  portfolio is  only  (cid:0) 0.03%  (0.00%), and  the  fee-adjusted 
long-short return is only 0.12% (0.14%).

We obtain consistent results when we examine additional options- 
based predictors that are either computed from implied volatilities or 
are moments of the risk-neutral distribution of stock returns (Rehman 
and  Vilkov,  2012;  An  et  al.,  2014;  Huang  and  Li,  2019;  Tang,  2019; 
Baltussen et al., 2018). These variables show some predictive power for 
abnormal returns, but trading strategies based on them fail to generate 
significant positive returns net of fees.

The fee-adjusted abnormal returns on spread and skew-sorted port-
folios  (Tables  4,  5)  are  diminished  even  further  when  considering 
institutional transaction costs. Both the fee-adjusted abnormal returns 
and  the  before-fee-adjustment  returns  on  low-fee  stocks  would  be

consumed  by  these  costs.  According  to  Frazzini  et  al.  (2018),  during 
2010–2016,  a  sophisticated  institutional  investor  trading  1%  of  daily 
volume faced price impact  costs of about 0.10%, implying round-trip 
costs  near  0.20%.  These  costs  would  eliminate  the  net-of-fee  returns 
from shorting the decile one portfolios when implemented with monthly 
turnover.

Other studies indicate that transactions costs are about the same or 
greater.  Almgren  (2010), using  institutional trade  data from Almgren 
et al. (2005), indicates that trades representing “a few percent of daily 
volume”  face  price  impacts  of  “tens  of  basis  points.”  Novy-Marx  and 
Velikov (2016) estimate round-trip costs exceeding 50 basis points for 
typical value-weighted strategies. Additionally, a 2021 report from Virtu 
Financial (2021), a leading high-frequency trader and execution broker, 
indicates  combined  investment  shortfall  and  commission  costs  of 
approximately  0.3%  for  large-cap  stocks  during  the  sample  period. 
These findings suggest that estimates reported by Frazzini et al. (2018)
represent a lower bound for institutional transaction costs.

Overall, these results provide little support for the hypothesis that 
the stock and options markets are segmented and the stock market is less 
efficient than the options market. Instead, they align with an equilib-
rium  in  which  these  markets  are  linked  through  the  stock  lending 
market. The performance of portfolios sorted by borrow fees reported in 
Table  7 shows  that  the  fee  is  the  primary  driver  of  abnormal  return 
predictability. The high correlations between volatility spread, volatility 
skew, and borrow fee (Table 3 and Figs. 2, 3) suggest these measures 
largely capture the same underlying phenomenon.

However, the evidence also suggests that the borrow fee might not 
fully  explain  the  abnormal  returns  of  the  spread-  and  skew-sorted 
portfolios. Though statistically insignificant, the point estimates of fee- 
adjusted  abnormal  returns  for  spread-sorted  and  skew-sorted  long- 
short portfolios in Tables 5 and 6 are 0.10% and 0.17%, respectively. 
Similarly, unadjusted abnormal returns on these portfolios for low-fee 
stocks  are  0.12%  (marginally  significant)  and  0.14%.  These  magni-
tudes are economically substantial, annualizing to between 1.2% (= 12 
× 0.10%) and 2.04% (= 12 × 0.17%). While statistically weak, these 
estimates suggest that volatility spread and skew retain modest predic-
tive  power  beyond  stock  borrow  costs,  though  this  residual  predict-
ability  likely  cannot  be  exploited  after  accounting  for  institutional 
transaction costs.

7. Conclusion

A common interpretation of stock return predictability using infor-
mation from the options market is that demand pressure in the options 
market due to informed trading alters option prices and implied vola-
tilities, but this information embedded in option prices is only slowly 
reflected  in  stock  prices.  As  a  result,  the  implied  volatility  measures 
predict stock returns. In the absence of an offsetting friction, this pre-
dictability  would  allow  market  participants  to  earn  substantial  risk- 
adjusted returns.

It is difficult to reconcile this common interpretation with other re-
sults and features of the options markets. First, option trades and option 
prices  are  readily  available  in  real  time.  Second,  the  stock  market  is 
more liquid than the options market, so this interpretation requires that 
one believe that the less liquid options market contains information that 
is  only  slowly  reflected  in  the  more  liquid  stock  market.  In  fact,  Hu 
(2014) shows that options order flow is quickly passed through to the 
stock market via the delta-hedge trades of options market makers, and 
Muravyev et al. (2013) present evidence that at high frequency options 
prices follow stock prices. This latter result should be expected because 
NBBO  options  price  quotes  are  typically  from  options  market  makers 
who use autoquotation algorithms in which the stock price is an input. 
Finally,  while  demand  pressure  in  the  options  market  can  certainly 
impact prices, in the well-known model of Gˆarleneau et al. (2009), op-
tion  demand  pressure  does  not  create  put-call  parity  deviations  or 
non-zero implied volatility spreads.

We propose an alternative interpretation of the evidence that option- 
based measures related to implied volatility predict stock returns. We 
show that if options prices are equal to their no-arbitrage values that 
reflect the borrow fee but implied volatilities are computed omitting the 
borrow fee, the resulting implied volatility spread is proportional to the 
omitted borrow fee. Because the volatility skew can be decomposed into 
a volatility spread and the difference in the volatilities of OTM and ATM 
puts, the analogously computed volatility skew also reflects the borrow 
fee.

These  analytical  results  suggest  the  hypothesis  that  the  implied 
volatility  spread  and  implied  volatility  skew  predict  stock  returns 
because they proxy for the borrow fee. Moreover, the borrow fee is a 
substantial  friction  that  is  reflected  in  options  prices  and  limits  stock 
investors’ ability to exploit options market information—investors must 
pay  a  high  borrow  fee  to  short  stocks  with  a  high  implied  volatility 
spread or skew. We provide evidence consistent with this hypothesis. 
Our  findings  indicate  that  the  volatility  spread  and  skew  predict  un-
derlying stock returns largely because they proxy for the borrow fee.

The abnormal returns for the long-short portfolio strategies using the 
volatility  spread  (skew)  are  reduced  by  more  than  four-fifths  (two- 
thirds)  after  taking  stock  borrow  costs  into  account.  The  estimate  of 
abnormal  performance  for  the  decile  one  portfolio  for  each  of  these 
strategies is (cid:0) 0.06% ((cid:0) 0.08%) after adjusting for the borrow fee. Thus, 
short selling the stocks in portfolio one based on these sorting strategies 
is not nearly as profitable once the cost of borrowing stocks is consid-
ered.  Reasonable  estimates  of  institutional  trading  costs  reduce  the 
benefits of the related strategies to zero or close to zero.

We examine seven additional options-based predictors derived from 
implied volatilities and their variation across strike prices. While we find 
statistically significant negative abnormal returns on decile one across 
most of the seven predictors, and positive abnormal returns for some of 
the  long-short  portfolios,  these  profits  disappear  after  accounting  for 
fees. In fact, the average long-short portfolio abnormal return across all 
seven predictors is almost identically zero after fees. Thus, options-based 
predictors  that  are  directly  related  to  the  implied  volatility  surface 
appear to predict underlying stock returns largely because they proxy for 
the borrow fee.

Our findings do not eliminate the potential for information in options 
prices  related  to  demand  pressure,  but  not  due  to  the  borrow  fee,  to 
predict stock returns. However, the returns attainable from exploiting 
this residual informational advantage trading the underlying stock are 
small, and the information is likely not exploitable after accounting for 
institutional transactions costs.

Given  that  fees  robustly  predict  returns,  it  remains  a  puzzle  why 
investors holding long positions do not sell their high-fee stocks until 
this predictability disappears. Explaining this puzzle is beyond the scope 
of this paper.

of the stock, and no dividends are paid prior to option expiration. This borrow fee is like a continuous dividend and it lowers the expected return of the 
stock under the risk-neutral measure below the risk-free rate. The stock price process is governed by geometric Brownian motion.
