A B S T R A C T

We examine the adoption rates of responsible investment (RI) policies among university endowments. Adoption
rates are higher among universities that face stakeholder pressure and are donation-dependent. Policy adoption
predicts greater abnormal donations totaling 12 % of endowment assets, especially from “socially conscious”
donors and during periods of higher media attention to climate change. Universities also experience greater
student applications following adoptions. RI endowments have greater management costs, greater return vola-
tility, and similar overall asset growth (donations plus net-of-cost investment income) compared to non-RI en-
dowments. We conclude that RI policies are an important part of the optimal contract between universities and
their stakeholders.

1. Introduction

Although much has been written about the responsible investment
(RI) policies of mutual funds and other investment companies, the ac-
ademic literature has devoted little attention to the RI policies of en-
dowments used to fund charitable and nonprofit institutions. This is
surprising because endowments, especially those used to fund the

operations of universities, provide a unique laboratory to measure the
costs and benefits of RI policies to its stakeholders. Universities are at the
forefront of political, environmental, and social change where stake-
holders (e.g., faculty, students) often engage in “campus activism” to
impact the endowment’s investment policy. By responding favorably to
stakeholder pressure, universities may realize benefits in the form of
greater charitable giving and/or greater student applications. On the

In this paper, we fill this gap in the literature by providing a detailed
examination of the motivation for and economic tradeoffs of RI policies
among university endowments. Our empirical analysis uses a sample of
1004 endowments from the National Association of College and Uni-
versity Business Officers (NACUBO) surveys over 2009–2017. The
NACUBO data provide information about endowment performance and
investment policies, including whether the endowment adopts RI pol-
icies. In our sample, the percentage of endowments adopting RI policies
grows over time, from 30 % in 2009 to 46 % in 2017 (Panel A of Fig. 1),
with RI endowment assets in our sample totaling $224 billion in 2017.
This is broadly consistent with the rapid growth in RI documented in the
mutual fund sector (Bialkowski and Starks, 2016).3

The first part of our investigation establishes an economic rationale
for RI policies by relating the presence of RI policies to endowment
characteristics. A key consideration is that a university’s stakeholders
provide non-investment revenues to the university, including charitable
giving from current and future donors. If stakeholders’ preferences are
more socially conscious, then universities may adopt RI policies in
response to stakeholder pressure, to secure current and future streams of
donations and other revenues. In support of the view that RI policies are
used to cater to a university’s stakeholders, we find that RI policies are
more likely among: (i) endowments that face explicit calls from uni-
versity stakeholders, especially students and donors, to pursue such
policies (i.e., stakeholder pressure), (ii) universities with a religious
affiliation, and (iii) universities located in states that rank highly on
corporate social responsibility. In addition, RI policies are more com-
mon when charitable giving contributes a larger amount to the univer-
sity budget and, therefore, when universities are more dependent on
donations to fund operations. Overall, our evidence indicates that RI
policies are more common among universities that depend more on
donations and have stakeholders that highly value RI policies.

Having examined the issue of why RI policies are adopted, we
continue the study by examining the potential tradeoffs universities face
across their endowment and the broader institution when adopting such
decisions. To do so, we incorporate data from The Chronicle of Philan-
thropy (TCOP) on donations to universities. The TCOP data cover large
donations of at least $1 million and identify the name and background of
the donor. We merge the donations data with our NACUBO data on
endowments’ investment policies and find that universities with RI
policies realize significantly higher donations over the following year.
The results are strongest when we look at donations from more socially
conscious donors as measured by whether donors live in a state with
greater corporate social responsibility (i.e., where potential donors are
perceived as having a strong social mission) or by whether donors derive
their wealth from industries other than energy, gambling, and defense
contracting.

During our sample period, many university stakeholders grew
increasingly concerned about climate change and called for universities
to divest their endowments from fossil fuel companies. Therefore, we
exploit time-variation in climate change sentiment as measured by
media attention to climate change issues as in Ardia et al. (2023). We
include interaction variables between climate change sentiment and RI
policies in our analysis and find that the positive relation between RI
policies and future donations is greatest during periods of heightened
climate change sentiment and when policies pertain to screening ac-
tivities such as fossil fuel divestment. This suggests that endowments
with RI policies tend to realize higher donations during periods of
heightened climate change sentiment, even if those RI policies were
adopted long ago.

Next, we focus on patterns of charitable giving to universities around
the time they adopt RI policies. We implement a difference-in-
differences (DiD) analysis to study the abnormal donations of 145
“treated’’ endowments that adopt RI policies over our sample period.
We find that universities tend to enter a period of significantly higher
donations after they adopt RI policies. Fig. 3 shows that universities
experience cumulative abnormal large donations of 12 % of endowment
assets over the eight years following adoption (Panel A). The results are
again strongest when we look at donations from more socially conscious
donors (Panel C); post-adoption abnormal donations from so-called “oil
and vice” donors are not significant (Panel B). The persistence of
abnormal donations for several years following the adoption event
suggests that the negotiation and finalization of large donations can take
a considerable amount of time, and that the donations are more per-
manent than temporary in nature. We also test for placebo effects and
find no evidence that abnormal donations predate the year of RI policy
adoption. Taken together, our findings highlight donations as an
important benefit to universities that adopt RI policies in response to
stakeholder pressure to do so.

In addition to donations, we also examine student applications. A
university’s student body is an important source of university financing
through tuition revenues and future donors as members of the alumni
network. Many students are concerned with an endowment’s RI policy
given their involvement in fossil fuel divestment campaigns and that
their application decisions are influenced by colleges’ commitment to
environmental issues.4 Therefore, following our analysis of donations,
we run a parallel set of tests for student applications. We find that RI
policies predict greater student applications, but only among smaller
endowments. Moreover, as in our donations analysis, we find that ap-
plications are especially high during periods of heightened climate
change sentiment and when funds adopt screening policies that involve
selling or removing certain investments from the endowment portfolio.
This provides some evidence that RI policies also benefit the university
in terms of greater student demand.

Finally, we examine the potential costs to universities that choose to
adopt RI policies. For endowments, the most obvious costs are drags on
investment performance due to RI-based restrictions on the investment
opportunity set. We first verify that the asset allocation decisions of
endowments indeed align with their stated RI policies. Detailed holdings
data on university endowments are not publicly available. Therefore, we
infer endowments’ asset allocations to oil and vice industries from their
portfolio returns and find that portfolio exposures to oil-related in-
dustries are significantly lower among RI endowments; the “oil betas” of
RI endowments are 50 % lower than those of non-RI endowments. In
addition, we return to our DiD framework and compare endowments’
stated allocations to broad asset categories (i.e., “policy portfolio
weights”) as reported in the NACUBO surveys, before and after changing
RI status. We find that an endowment’s policy portfolios, before and

Overall, our results provide insights into the benefits and costs for
universities that adopt RI policies. Since these universities tend to be
those with a larger share of RI-sensitive stakeholders, we do not claim
that our findings imply a causal effect between RI policies and dona-
tions, or that other universities could increase donations by spontane-
ously adopting RI policies. Instead, our evidence shows that investment
policy constraints are an important part of the optimal contract between
university stakeholders and endowment managers. Managers choose to
adopt RI policies when they expect greater benefits (e.g., charitable
donations) from stakeholders who value those constraints. Such non-
investment revenues act as a compensating differential for RI-related
drags on investment performance. In contrast, managers and stake-
holders choose a low constraint arrangement when RI policies are not

valued as highly and managers depend more on investment income to
finance university operations. Indeed, when we examine endowments’
total asset growth – i.e., donations plus investment income, we find no
significant difference between RI and non-RI endowments
in
benchmark-adjusted asset growth.

Our paper contributes to existing evidence on the RI practices of
asset managers. Mutual fund investors direct more of their capital to
funds with RI objectives and/or that rank highly on sustainability issues
(Bialkowski and Starks, 2016; Hartzmark and Sussman, 2019; Aragon
and Chen, 2024); venture capital
investors willingly accept lower
financial returns on so-called “impact investments” (Barber, Morse, and
Yasuda, 2021); and hedge funds that endorse the United Nations Prin-

Sun, and Teo, 2022).6 We show that university stakeholders also value
social objectives as indicated by their greater donations to endowments
that adopt RI policies; to our knowledge, we are the first to show a
relation between RI policies and donations to non-profit organizations.

longer-horizon investors since university trustees must safeguard equity
among many generations of stakeholders (Tobin, 1974). As we show, RI
policies are associated with higher donations and other benefits, such as

Prior work considers endowments as integrated parts of universities’
overall objectives. Merton (1993) argues that non-endowment income,
like donations, are implicit investments in traded assets that could
impact the endowment’s investment policy; for example, if donations
are positively related to stock market performance, then the endowment
should allocate less of its portfolio to (and possibly short) equity secu-
rities. Interestingly, we find that endowments allocate less (not more) to
the oil and vice sectors when universities are likely to have more socially
conscious donors and, hence, already have a lower implicit investment
in such sectors. Finally, Gilbert and Hrdlicka (2015) show that, when
donations are risky, a university’s endowment provides a precautionary
savings buffer to support and expand its capital stock which produces
“social dividends.” We highlight a feedback from an endowment’s in-
vestment policy to a university’s donations and production of social
dividends: endowments adopt RI policies to align the university with its
stakeholders and secure future revenue streams in the form of charitable
donations.9

necessary to produce an optimal contract with university stakeholders.

2. Background

3.1. NACUBO database

The investment policies of an endowment typically follow the rec-
ommendations of an investment committee serving in an advisory ca-
pacity to the university’s board of trustees. The committee’s decision to
recommend RI policies – loosely used in the context to include corporate
social responsibility (CSR), environmental, social and governance
(ESG), or other forms of RI – may depend on the university’s mission and
tastes of the committee’s members, but also on stakeholder pressure to
adopt such policies. Such pressure can arise from students, faculty,
alumni, corporate recruiters, and other potential donors.10 By promising
to adhere to RI practices in its endowment, a university can align itself
more closely with potential donors and secure an important non-
endowment source of revenue.11 Aligning with donors’ preferences is
especially important for universities that depend more on donations to
fund operations, since a failure to do so could endanger funding.

RI policies often involve screening activities that impose restrictions
on an endowment’s portfolio holdings as certain investments with poor
CSR/ESG records are excluded. This can potentially result in higher
portfolio volatility due to diversification losses and an inability to

We use several databases in our analysis. First, the National Associ-
ation of College and University Business Officers’s (NACUBO) database
of North American endowments and foundations contains information
on the investment management activities of endowments and the uni-
versities these endowments serve. The data are self-reported and
collected from annual surveys. To address survivorship bias, we include
institutions that ceased participating in the survey (Brown et al., 1992).
Our sample period runs from 2009 to 2017. It begins in 2009 when
NACUBO began querying survey respondents about their responsible
investment practices, in the “Social Investing Criteria” section of its
questionnaire. We construct an indicator variable, denoted by RI, that
equals one if the endowment undertakes any activities related to RI as
indicated in the Social Investing Criteria section, and zero otherwise. In
Table A.2 of Appendix A, we classify these activities as relating to either
screening, engaging, or other RI activities. While our main analysis fo-
cuses on RI, in Section 5, we also examine alternative RI measures that
capture the scope/intensity and type of RI activities (see Table 9).

We also use the NACUBO survey to construct measures of whether an
endowment faces pressure from university stakeholders to adopt RI
policies. Since 2014, the survey asks each university to indicate which
stakeholder groups have requested that the university include RI con-
siderations in its endowment, and which specific RI considerations the
stakeholder would like the university to consider. We show this survey
question in Fig. 2. There are six possible groups: Students, Alumni,
Employees, Donors, Grantmakers, and Other. The NACUBO survey asks
its respondents to specify the stakeholders in the Other category; in our
sample, the university’s trustees are the most frequently specified
stakeholders. In addition, with respect to each group, there are five
possible RI considerations: ESG, SRI, Divestment, Impact, and Other.
Our main measure of stakeholder pressure, denoted by Stakeholder
pressure, is the sum of the 30 possible (= 6 × 5) group-type indicators.
The variable has a theoretical minimum value of zero, in which case no
group has exerted pressure for any type of consideration. Its theoretical
maximum value 30 occurs when all six groups request the university to
consider all five types of RI considerations. Our main analysis focuses on
Stakeholder pressure but we also examine alternative measures, including
the number of stakeholder groups that have ever requested the univer-
sity to include RI considerations (Pressure groups), an indicator variable

3.2. Other databases

In addition to the NACUBO database, we also use data from several
other sources. First, we use the MSCI ESG Stats Database to construct a
state-level measure of the strength of corporate social responsibility
(CSR index), in the spirit of Lins, Servaes, and Tamayo (2017). To
construct this index, we use information on firm-level CSR ratings along
five CSR categories: community, diversity, employee relations, envi-
ronmental, and human rights. For each firm-year-category, we divide
the rating score by the maximum rating score across firms and sum the
scaled rating scores over the five categories. The overall scaled score of a
firm thus ranges from zero to five. We then compute the CSR index value
of a state as the market capitalization-weighted overall scaled scores of
firms within the state.

Second, we use data from The Chronicle of Philanthropy’s database
of large charitable gifts of at least $1 million (TCOP). The unit of
observation is an individual gift and provides information on the gift’s
date, dollar amount, and recipient, and the donor’s name, location, and
source of wealth. We use donor information to identify donors that are
plausibly more “socially-conscious,” e.g., whether the donor lives in a
high CSR state or derives their wealth from industries other than oil or
vice.13 Each gift is also accompanied by a brief narrative about the gift,
including any specific purposes related to the gift.

Third, we use the National Science Foundation (NSF) awards data-
base to calculate the number of sustainability science research grants
awarded to faculty at each university in our sample (Sustainability science
research). We construct this variable because faculty are an important
group of university stakeholders, at the forefront of climate research (e.

“endowment” and “socially responsible”; 4) “university” and “endow-
ment” and “divest”. This search yields 5548 articles, of which 445 ar-
ticles relate to fossil fuel divestment.

14 %, 4 %, and 3 % indicate pressure to consider ESG, SRI, Impact, and
Other, respectively. This evidence (not tabulated) is consistent with
divestment from fossil fuels being a primary RI concern over our sample
period. The sample mean of Donation contribution is 3.56 % and higher
among RI funds. Specifically, donations represent a 0.56 % higher per-
centage of university budgets compared to non-RI funds (t-statistic =
2.49). These univariate relations provide initial evidence that RI policies
are more common among universities that are donation-dependent and
face greater stakeholder pressure.

4. Analysis and results

We now present our main analysis on the determinants of RI policies
and how the adoption of such policies are related to a university’s do-
nations, student applications, and endowment performance.

4.2. RI policies and university donations

The empirical findings in the previous section indicate that endow-
ment managers respond to the demands of university stakeholders: if
stakeholders prefer RI policies, then managers respond accordingly.
Since donors are an important set of stakeholders, a natural question is
whether universities realize higher donations when they include RI
considerations when investing their funds.

We address this question using TCOP data on large donations (over
$1 million) since these data includes granular data on the donor and
nature of the gift. We estimate pooled Poisson regressions where the
dependent variable is the dollar amount of large donations received by
university i in year y (Large donationsiy). The coefficient on RI is a semi-
elasticity measuring the percentage change in donations associated with
the presence of an RI policy (see, e.g., Cohn et al., 2022). A positive
coefficient on RI would indicate that funds with RI policies are associ-
ated with greater subsequent donations received by the university. The
unit of observation is fund-year and we cluster standard errors at the
fund level.

tion survives in Column (2) after including the control variables (coef. =
0.339; t-statistic = 1.74).19 This means that dollar donations are about
40 % larger among universities that implement RI policies in their en-
dowments as compared to non-RI universities (i.e., 0.40 = 100 ×

in contrast, no significant relation is found between RI and charitable
giving from oil and vice donors or Low CSR donors. Overall, RI policies
attract higher donations to the university, especially from donors that
care more about responsible investing.

We also implement a difference-in-differences (DiD) analysis of do-
nations around changes in endowments’ RI policies. The event year is
defined as the first year that an endowment fund’s RI variable changes
from zero to one (event year T = 0). There are 145 such instances in our
sample. For each treatment fund, we identify five control funds by
matching on Total assets and donations at T-2. We calculate the sample
mean of annual donations during the pre-event window (T-4 to T-2) and
the event window (T-1 to T + 8). Our event window includes the one
year prior to the adoption event to capture a lag between policy adop-
tion and when the policies were reflected in the annual NACUBO survey.
We include the eight years after the adoption event to capture the slow

arrival of donations in response to changes in an endowment’s RI policy.
In addition, looking out eight years post-adoption addresses concerns
that universities harvest short-term donations at the expense of those in
the more distant future, in anticipation of poor endowment performance
and budget shortfalls caused by RI policies (similar to the effect docu-
mented by Davies and Van Wesep (2018).20 In our DiD tests, we expand
our sample of large donations data from TCOP through 2022, five years
after the end of our NACUBO sample period.21 We do not require
treatment funds to have a full set of event window observations to be
included in the test; therefore, the treated sample includes endowments
with RI initiations in both the earlier and latter part of the sample
period.

Table 4 shows the DiD test results where the outcome variable is
donations as a percentage of endowment assets. The number of treated
funds is reported at the top of each column. Funds adopting RI policies
(treatment) attract 0.66 % per year higher donations (t-statistic = 2.60)
over the period from one year before to eight years after the change.
During the pre-event window, no significant difference in donations is
found (coef. = –0.09 %;
t-statistic = –0.36). The difference-in-
differences, 0.75 % per year, is significant at the 5 % level (t-statistic
= 1.97). By comparison, Column (2) in Table 3 estimates that RI funds
have 40 % higher dollar donations compared to non-RI funds. Based on
the sample mean donations of $3.69 million, this represents a $1.49
million per year increase in large donations, or, a 43 basis point increase
in donations relative to sample mean assets under management.
Therefore, by focusing on RI policy adoption events, the effects of RI on
donations are larger than those from the full sample regressions (75 vs.
43 basis points). The larger magnitudes may result from RI policies
having a greater impact in the years soon after adoption, which is what
the DiD test focuses on. In contrast, Table 3 regressions include en-
dowments with “stale” RI policies adopted well before the start of our
sample period.

The next set of columns of Table 4 show the results by donor type.
Once again, the effects are driven by donations from “socially conscious”
donors – i.e., non-oil-and-vice donors and donors that live in High CSR
states.22 A potential concern is that, due to the drop in oil prices
following 2013, oil donors were financially constrained when many of
the RI adoptions take place, and that is why donations from oil-and-vice
donors do not increase following policy adoptions. However, total do-
nations by oil donors increase, not decrease, from 2013 to 2014 and 2015
(not tabulated); therefore, it is unlikely that oil donors are cutting back
on charitable giving due to financial distress. Another potential concern
is that the “non-result” for oil-and-vice donors is due to a lack of sta-
tistical power as this group represents a small fraction of total donations
(Table 1). Therefore, we ran a power analysis using simulated data
drawn from a Pearson distribution that matches sample moments of
donations from oil-and-vice donors, for different differences-in-means in
donations between RI and non-RI groups. Reassuringly, the rejection
rates for difference-in-means tests converge to 100 % when differences
are only 15 % of one standard deviation.

4.5.3. Portfolio returns

We examine three measures of portfolio returns: Return, Return minus
policy return, and Adjusted returns. We define Return minus policy return as
in Eq. (3) using annual returns, not quarterly returns. We define Adjusted
return in Appendix C as fund returns adjusted for its policy portfolio
return and exposures to oil and vice factors. The oil and vice adjustment
is motivated by our Table 6 evidence that RI funds have a lower expo-
sure to oil, and that our sample period includes a two-year period
covering 2014–15 during which oil dependent firms declined in value by
30 %. Hence, RI funds may have earned higher returns due to plausibly

unexpected, realized returns paths of oil dependent firms over a short
sample period.27 Furthermore, a fund’s policy portfolio adjusts for a
fund’s exposures to broad asset classes, not a fund’s portfolio tilt within
an asset class.28 Therefore, subtrating a fund’s policy portfolio return
from its raw return, as in Return minus policy return, may not sufficiently
control for differences in oil and vice exposures.

Columns (6)-(8) of Table 7 show that the coefficient on RI is positive
and not significant for Return and Return minus policy return, but is
negative and significant for Adjusted return (coef. = (cid:0) 77.548 basis points
per year; t-statistic = (cid:0) 2.01). The negative sign on RI for adjusted
returns may be due to RI funds having a lower exposure to oil (Table 6)
during a period of falling oil prices; hence, adjusting for a lower expo-
sure to the oil sector makes RI funds look worse compared to peers.29
Overall, while we cannot draw strong conclusions given our short
sample period, the significantly higher management costs, higher return
volatility, and lower adjusted returns among RI funds is more consistent
with RI policies imposing a drag on investment performance.

5. Further analysis and robustness

We now perform additional tests to enrich and strengthen our main

results.

5.1. Alternative DiD analysis using stacked regressions

Baker, Larcker, and Wang (2022) show that staggered DiD treatment
effect estimates can be biased when treatment effects change over time.
This can result from a “bad comparisons” problem in which the control
group for a specific treatment group may include firms that have already
been treated. However, our baseline DiD analyses in Section 4 are un-
likely to be affected by this issue because we compare each treatment
fund with a set of control funds that do not adopt an RI policy over the
full sample period. Nevertheless, we implement an alternative DiD
analysis based on the stacked regression estimator suggested by Baker,
Larcker, and Wang (2022) and implemented by Gormley and Matsa
(2011). This approach creates event-specific datasets with “clean” con-
trols and allows for different comparison groups and control variables in
estimating the treatment effects from adopting an RI policy, thereby
providing an important robustness check.

Specifically, for each year in our sample, we construct a cohort of
adopters and non-adopter universities using fund-year observations for
the five years before and the five years after the adoption. We obtain
similar findings if we instead compare the four years before with the four
years after each adoption. Funds are not required to be in the sample for
the full ten years around the event but must have at least three non-
missing observations to be included. We also drop funds that are al-
ways RI funds during the full sample period and, as in our baseline DiD

analysis, we drop 28 RI funds that switch back to non-RI status (i.e., RI
switches from one to zero) during the sample. We then pool the data
across cohorts (i.e., across all new adoption years) and estimate the
average treatment effect. Specifically, we estimate the following firm-
panel regression:

5.2. Alternative measures of RI

RI only captures whether the endowment adopts policies related to
RI or not, rather than the scope/intensity or type of such policies. In this
section, we repeat our main analyses using an alternative measure (RI*)
that counts the number of RI practices adopted by the endowment.
These practices are either 1) whether to exclude or screen out in-
vestments to better align the endowment’s portfolio with the in-
stitution’s mission (screening); 2) whether to vote proxies consistent
with responsible investing criteria (engaging), and/or 3) whether to
include investments that rank higher on ESG criteria and further the

institution’s mission (other).30 We also consider separate indicator
variables for each of the three components of RI*: RI_Screen, RI_Engage,
and RI_Other.

Panel A of Table 9 shows that the pairwise correlation between RI*
and RI is 0.87; however, whereas RI is a dummy variable that equals one
if the endowment adopts any of the three responsible investing prac-
tices, RI* ranges from zero to three and is a measure of RI intensity and
scope. Across policy types, screening has the largest correlation with RI
(0.86) and is the most frequent type (sample mean of 31 %); in fact,
conditional on an endowment having any RI policy, the mean of
RI_Screen is 82 % (not tabulated), as compared to 39 % and 51 % for
engaging and other, respectively.

5.2.1. Determinants of RI intensity

Panel B of Table 9 shows the results from a linear regression of RI* on
university characteristics. We find that stakeholder pressure to adopt RI
policies and financial dependence on donations are positively related to
RI*. For example, an increase in Stakeholder pressure of one standard
deviation is associated with a 0.272 increase in RI*, or, about a quarter
of one standard deviation. Alternative stakeholder pressure variables –
Pressure groups and Pressure dummy – and variables related to pressure
from students, donors, and university trustees, also positively predict
RI*. This evidence confirms our Table 2 findings from linear regressions
of RI.

5.2.3. Exposures to oil and vice and fund performance

Panels F and G of Table 9 show the results from regressions in Ta-
bles 6 and 7 with RI* instead of RI. The evidence, again, shows that RI
policies are negatively related to the endowment’s portfolio exposure to
oil and vice and positively related to the endowment’s management
costs and portfolio volatility. The main difference from our earlier re-
sults in Table 7 is that the negative coefficient on the RI intensity vari-
able, RI*, in the Adjusted asset growth regression is now significant. For
example, Column (11) shows that adjusted asset growth is 60.9 basis
points per year lower for each additional RI consideration included by
the fund (t-statistic = (cid:0) 2.10). Overall, the results from using RI* support
our main findings using RI.

5.3. Using NACUBO data on donations

Our main donations variable is based on TCOP database of large gifts
(greater than or equal to $1 million) received by the university. These
data allow us to identify donor characteristics (e.g., source of wealth,
where donor lives) that we utilize in our analyses. However, endow-
ments report aggregate donations (both small and large gifts) that they
receive each year to the NACUBO database, but without identifying
information about the donors. As shown in Panel A of Table 9, the
pairwise correlation between the two donations variables is 0.27 and,
similar to our findings for TCOP donations in Table 1, NACUBO dona-
tions are significantly higher among RI funds (t-statistic = 4.06). For
robustness, we repeat our All donations regression (Column (2) of
Table 3) using the NACUBO donations variable. The results are reported
in Columns (8)-(10) in Panel C of Table 9 and show that RI, RI*, and
RI_Screen are significant predictors of higher donations in the subsequent
year. The coefficient on RI is smaller than that in Table 3 (0.150 vs.
0.339), and the coefficient on RI* is more significant compared to that in
Column (1) using TCOP data (t-statistic equals 2.18 vs. 1.20). Overall,
our finding that RI policies predict greater donations is robust to an
alternative source of donations data.

6. Concluding remarks

While endowments have no explicit residual claimants, they never-
theless respond to the demands of their stakeholders to maximize the
production of social goods. We find a growing and persistent trend of RI
policy adoption among university endowments over 2009–2017. RI
policies are more common among universities that face greater pressure
from stakeholders to incorporate RI practices, rely more on donations to
fund operations, and are in geographical areas with greater corporate
social responsibility. In turn, RI policies are associated with significantly
higher charitable donations to the university, especially from donors
that plausibly derive higher utility from RI, and during periods of greater

Overall, our results provide a consistent description of outcomes
around the trade-offs universities face across their endowment and the
broader institution when adopting responsible investment decisions.
Though we do not claim any causal effect of responsible investing, the
results nevertheless point to interesting heterogeneity in the costs and
benefits across universities. In particular, our evidence supports the
view that universities face diverse groups of stakeholders, and therefore
different universities serve society best by adopting different objectives.
Universities facing pressure to adopt RI policies can realize substantial
benefits (in the form of donations) from doing so, and such non-
endowment revenues act as a compensating differential for RI-related
drags on endowment performance. In contrast, universities that do not
face strong pressure from stakeholders to adopt RI policies and/or rely
less on donations face a smaller reward and greater opportunity cost
from imposing investment constraints on endowment managers.

Appendix B. Construction of variables from the NSF awards database

Starting from the National Science Foundation (NSF) Awards Advanced Search database (https://www.nsf.gov/awardsearch/advancedSearch.
jsp), we download all Active Awards and Expired Awards as .csv files over the period 2000–2020, as of January 25, 2020. This is achieved by
entering the period “2000–2020″ into the “Original Award Date” field of the search query.

Next, from the raw NSF data files, we create a list of unique names of all NSF organizations that receive at least one award over the sample period.
We then manually match this list of organization names with the list of university names corresponding to the endowment funds in our NACUBO
sample. That way, we can identify all NSF awards, if any, with each endowment in our sample.

We then return to the raw NSF data files and create a dummy variable that equals one if the NSF award is related to sustainability science. We
identify sustainability science awards based on a textual analysis of the “Title” and “Abstract” of each NSF award proposal. Specifically, an award is a
sustainability award if the title or abstract contains a bigram (consecutive two-word combinations, obtained after frequent stop words and punctuation
and extra white spaces are removed) that is included in a bigram word cloud from the sustainability science literature (specifically, we use Fig S3 of
Bettencourt and Kaur, 2011).

Finally, we aggregate the number of sustainability science awards by endowment-quarter, where quarter is the calendar quarter of the original

award date. We label this variable as Sustainability science research.

Before aggregating the raw NSF award data, we drop any observation with a missing value for either the title, abstract, organization, principal

investigator, or Awarded amount to date fields. We also drop a few observations for which the Awarded amount to date variable is equal to zero.
