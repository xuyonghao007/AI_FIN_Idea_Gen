1. Introduction

This paper presents a framework to study how investors’ social concerns affect technology choices. Con-
sequentialist preferences (disutility from aggregate harm) influence outcomes only if investors coordinate, 
unless internalized harm is independent of an investor’s mass. Non-consequentialist preferences (disutility from 
stockholdings) affect outcomes regardless of coordination. Both preferences have stronger impact when risk-
sharing consequences of technology supply are small (e.g., highly correlated returns), and their effects cannot 
be inferred from cost-of-capital differences. When harm is stochastic, polluting firms may appear less risky to 
social investors. Depending on type and strength of social preferences, this can support or hinder the green 
transition.

Under the traditional finance paradigm, agents’ willingness to pay 
for financial assets, such as stocks, is solely determined by the net 
present value of future cash flows that the owners of these assets expect 
to receive. In recent years, this paradigm has increasingly come under 
scrutiny. In particular, there is an intensifying debate on whether this 
valuation paradigm is too narrow, since people do not just care about 
cash flows, but also have ethical and social concerns.

One might argue that investment decisions can be separated from 
ethical  considerations,  that  investors  can  optimize  portfolios  purely 
based on financial returns and then allocate their wealth according 
to their ethical and social preferences. This view was famously artic-
ulated by Friedman (1970) in his New York Times article. However, 
as highlighted by Hart and Zingales (2017), there are compelling rea-
sons why agents’ non-financial objectives and their portfolio decisions 
cannot be fully separated. In practice, investor preferences beyond a 
narrowly defined risk-return tradeoff, particularly environmental and 
social objectives, seem to have contributed substantially to the recent 

growth in investment vehicles such as ESG funds, which take environ-
mental, social and governance characteristics explicitly into account 
when constructing their investment portfolios.

Theoretical research on the effects of social preferences on portfolio 
decisions, financial asset valuations, and firms’ production choices has 
grown significantly in recent years. The way in which social preferences 
are modeled in this emerging literature varies substantially across stud-
ies. Some studies assume that investors with social preferences adhere 
to ethical imperatives that directly constrain their feasible portfolio 
choices. For instance, Heinkel et al. (2001) and Hong and Kacperczyk 
(2009) examine investors who avoid holding certain securities, such 
as stocks of firms with high carbon emissions, or those involved in 
controversial industries like weapons manufacturing see also Davies 
and Van Wesep (2018), Berk and van Binsbergen (2025), Hong et al. 
(2023),  Halling  et  al.  (2022).  These  preferences  are  referred  to  as 
deontological preferences.

Other studies assume that investors with social preferences derive 
non-pecuniary benefits or costs from holding specific securities, such 
as  stocks  of  firms  investing  in  renewable  energy  or  oil  companies,

Alongside the rapidly expanding theoretical work on social prefer-
ences, an emerging body of empirical, survey-based, and experimental 
research  examines  their  prevalence  (e.g.,)Riedl  and  Smeets  (2017), 
Bauer et al. (2021), Krueger et al. (2020), Dyck et al. (2019), Bolton 
et al. (2020), Giglio et al. (2025).1 Overall, these studies provide strong 
evidence for the existence of social preferences and show that investors 
are frequently willing to accept lower returns to hold portfolios aligned 
with their ethical considerations.2 There is also a growing experimental 
and  survey-based  literature  that  evaluates  different  types  of  social 
preferences.  Most  of  these  studies  suggest  that  non-consequentialist 
preferences are predominant.3 Cole et al. (2023), for example, estimate 
that only about 12% of investors in the venture capital space exhibit 
consequentialist preferences, identified as those who are willing to fund 
high-impact firms that would otherwise not have attracted capital.

While  this  work  has  generated  important  insights,  it  also  raises 
important new questions. How do different social preferences influence 
asset prices and corporate decisions in a general equilibrium setting, 
and what are the key determinants of these effects? To what extent do 
the implications of alternative social preferences depend on investor 
coordination?  How  do  these  effects  extend  to  environments  where 
social harm is stochastic? Moreover, how are these effects reflected 
in observable financial metrics, such as differences in firms’ cost of 
capital?

Addressing these questions requires a unified theoretical framework 
capable of incorporating and comparing alternative specifications of 
social preferences. To this end, we categorize preferences into three 
categories. Deontological preferences: investors avoid holding stocks of

At the core of our framework is a fundamental tradeoff: the non-
pecuniary payoffs that a portfolio generates for investors with social 
preferences versus the costs of deviating from the portfolio that pro-
vides the optimal financial risk-return tradeoff. To isolate the effects of 
this tradeoff, we do not consider other channels through which social 
preferences may influence outcomes, such as shareholder activism via 
direct negotiations with management or via voting on shareholder pro-
posals (see, for example, Levit et al. (2024)), or through consumption 
decisions (see, for example, Kaufmann et al. (2024)).

Our analysis yields several key insights. First, we demonstrate that 
the effects of different social preferences on real outcomes, i.e., the 
equilibrium fraction of firms that use the green technology, depend 
on fundamentally different economic quantities. When social investors 
are consequentialists, corporate technology supply depends on an in-
dividual investor’s internalized harm. Thus, consequentialist investors 
only have an impact on the supply of technologies if they coordinate 
through a strategic fund, but they have no effect on real outcomes 
in  a  competitive  equilibrium,  in  which  the  mass  of  an  individual 
investor converges to zero. This result holds as long as the fraction 
of total harm internalized by an individual investor decreases with 
their mass. In the absence of coordination, consequentialist investors 
affect  technology  supply  only  in  the  extreme  case  in  which  every 
small investor internalizes the same fraction of aggregate harm as a 
single representative investor would. Therefore, investor coordination 
is essential for consequentialist preferences to have real effects.

In stark contrast, we show that the real effects of social preferences 
are determined by an aggregate measure of internalized social harm for 
non-consequentialist preferences. As a result, the supply of technologies 
is entirely unaffected by whether non-consequentialist social investors 
behave as competitive price takers or as a single coordinated strategic 
investor. This insight provides a novel perspective on trends observed 
in the asset management industry, such as the rise of coordinated en-
gagements.5 While such platforms offer coordination for impact funds, 
which plausibly cater to consequentialist investors, no similar platforms 
exist for funds that target non-consequentialist investors, such as funds 
that follow passive exclusionary strategies.

Second, we show that regardless of the specific type of social prefer-
ences considered, their real effects on equilibrium outcomes are larger 
when  the  risk-sharing  consequences  of  shifts  in  relative  technology 
supplies are small. For instance, when the cash flows of green and 
brown stocks are highly correlated, social investors are more effective, 
as firms’ technology choices respond more elastically to changes in the 
share of social investors. This challenges the view that social investors 
should have limited impact on corporate decisions when cash flows 
are highly correlated, since in this case financial investors can easily 
‘‘offset’’ social investors’ portfolio tilts by increasing their own holdings 
in brown firms. We show that this intuition misses an important coun-
teracting mechanism, namely that higher correlations make technology 
supply more elastic, thereby increasing the real economic effects of so-
cial investors. Similarly, we find that all types of social preferences have 
stronger real effects when firms’ cash flow volatility or investors’ risk

Third, we find that the impact of social investors’ investment deci-
sions on firms’ cost of capital provides limited insight into their real 
effects. This holds true for all types of social preferences, as corporate 
supply adjustments largely neutralize the effects of social preferences 
on cost-of-capital differences.

Finally, we show that our findings remain qualitatively robust when 
social harm is stochastic. The quantitative effects of stochastic social 
harm, however, depend critically on its correlation with brown firms’ 
cash  flows.  When  this  correlation  is  sufficiently  high,  brown  firms 
effectively become less risky for social investors, as highlighted first 
by Baker et al. (2022) in the context of consequentialist investors.

This mechanism prompts non-consequentialist investors to choose 
more extreme portfolio holdings in brown shares. When model param-
eters are such that non-consequentialist investors take long positions 
in brown firms in the deterministic case, they optimally increase these 
positions when harm is stochastic, thereby slowing down the green 
transition. Conversely, when non-consequentialist preferences are suf-
ficiently strong such that social investors take short positions in brown 
firms when harm is deterministic, the correlation between harm and 
brown  firms’  cash-flows  supports  the  green  transition.  In  this  case, 
the hedging effect incentivizes social investors to increase their short 
positions.6

For  consequentialist  investors,  the  same  mechanism  consistently 
leads to increased long positions in brown shares, thereby perversely 
impeding the green transition. Importantly, these results imply that 
competitive consequentialist preferences are not merely ineffective – 
they can actually lead to a brown transition when harm is stochastic, 
increasing the supply of brown firms relative to a world without social 
investors.

Our  paper  primarily  adopts  a  positive  rather  than  a  normative 
approach. However, some of our results have implications for policy-
makers. For example, they may consider policy tools that influence 
the correlation between brown firms’ cash flows to shareholders and 
social harm. In a plausible scenario where reducing this correlation 
facilitates a green transition, policymakers could, for example, require 
firms to purchase costly carbon certificates once their gross revenues 
exceed certain thresholds, thereby reducing the correlation between 
social harm and firms’ cash flows to shareholders.

The structure of the paper is as follows. Section 2 introduces the 
different types of social preferences. Section 3 evaluates their effects 
on asset prices, corporate technology supply, and social harm in market 
equilibrium. It also examines how the equilibrium effects of different 
social preferences depend on key model parameters and highlights the 
varying role of coordination across preferences. Section 4 investigates 
how results change when social harm becomes stochastic and Section 5 
concludes.

3.2. Consequentialist investors

The effects of consequentialist preferences depend crucially on how 
internalized social losses adjust as the mass of a social investor changes. 
As discussed above, in the case of non-consequentialist preferences, 
internalized social losses adjust mechanically with investor mass, since 
they are proportional to the investor’s equilibrium holdings of brown 
shares, which naturally vary with their mass. By contrast, for conse-
quentialist preferences, it becomes necessary to explicitly specify how 
𝜆𝑆,𝑖 – the fraction of total harm internalized by an individual investor 
– adjusts as their mass, 𝛼∕𝑁𝑆 , changes.

While it appears natural to assume that the share of total harm 
internalized by social investors with a given total mass should be inde-
pendent of the mass of each individual social investor, some studies (see, 
e.g.,  Broccardo  et  al.  (2022),  Kaufmann  et  al.  (2024))  assume  that
each individual investor internalizes a constant fraction of total harm. 
In the latter case, the total harm internalized by social investors with 
aggregate mass of 𝛼 increases with 𝑁𝑆 .

The available experimental evidence on this issue is mixed. A study 
by Schumacher et al. (2017) suggests that, in the case when the costs 
of negative externalities are spread among multiple individuals rather 
than concentrated within a few, pro-social concerns respond primarily 
to the magnitude of the harm incurred per capita. Thus, this finding 
does not accord with the view that each investor internalizes a constant 
fraction of total harm, independent of their mass within the set of social 
investors. Using a different experimental design, Bartling et al. (2019) 
exogenously vary whether a fixed external harm from market activity 
is concentrated on a single individual or diffused over many. They find 
only a modest reduction in participants’ concerns when its impact is 
diffused.

per individual social investor remains constant as 𝑁𝑆 changes (𝑘 = 0 in 
the following assumption).16 Specifically, we assume:

3.3.2. Risk sharing, cost of capital and the effect of social preferences

In addition to the degree of competition among investors, our uni-
fied framework implies that the effect of social investors on technology 
supply is also affected by other model parameters. In particular, the 
elasticity, with which technology supply responds to a change in the 
fraction of investors with social preferences, crucially depends on the 
risk-sharing consequences associated with a changing technology sup-
ply. The model primitives that capture these effects are the correlation 
between the two technologies’ cash flows, 𝜌, the standard deviations 
of the two technologies’ cash flows, 𝜎, and investors’ risk aversion, 𝛾. 
This subsection therefore presents numerical results illustrating how 
the risk consequences of a change in technology supply interact with 
the equilibrium effects of different types of social preferences, using the 
same set of common parameter values as in Section 3.3.1.

A change in the fraction of social investors, 𝛼, generally changes not 
only the equilibrium technology supplies and thus the risk character-
istics of the economy, but also the cost-of-capital differences between 
green and brown firms. Since the importance of social preferences for 
corporate decisions is frequently evaluated empirically by their effects 
on firms’ cost of capital, the numerical examples in this subsection also 
show how the cost-of-capital difference between green and brown firms 
is affected by the fraction of social investors.24

There are several empirical strategies that can be used to evaluate

demand shocks or ownership shocks and relate these to firms’ subse-

what our analysis implies.29

refining the suggested approaches appears to be a promising direction 
for future work.

vanishes, as we consider an economy where each investor’s mass con-

4.4. Discussion

The analysis in this section shows that important takeaways about 
the  differential  impact  of  consequentialist  and  non-consequentialist 
investors derived in earlier sections for deterministic harm are robust 
or become even more pronounced when harm is stochastic. In the plau-
sible case of 𝜌𝐵ℎ > 0, we find that social investors with consequentialist 
preferences even trigger a brown transition, i.e. they increase the supply 
of brown stocks, while in the case of deterministic harm, they had no 
impact on supply. In contrast, non-consequentialist investors usually 
continue to decrease the supply of brown firms when harm is stochastic, 
although that decrease is generally smaller than in the deterministic 
harm case.

While the findings presented in this section are broadly consistent 
with those of Baker et al. (2022), our unified model highlights the 
importance of the correlation between social harm and brown firms’ 
cash flows.33 If 𝜌𝐵ℎ > 0, social investors’ hedging demands typically im-
pede the green transition or may even trigger a brown transition. This 
raises the question whether policy makers could lower this correlation 
to mitigate the negative consequences of the hedging channel.

One way to do so would be a green capital income tax (see Pedersen 
(2024) for a related discussion) that is progressive in the harm that 
an investor’s portfolio is related to. Such a tax would break the link 
between investors’ financial after-tax dividends and harm, and thus 
reduce the hedging motive for social investors. Similarly, one could 
envision a progressive carbon tax or a cap-and-trade framework, assum-
ing that the harm considered is caused by carbon dioxide emissions. In 
the latter case, one could make the initial free allocation of allowances 
inversely related to the revenues generated per ton emitted. Again, the 
idea would be to progressively reduce the net-profitability of brown 
firms in those states in which their economic activities are likely to 
produce more emissions, or harm more generally speaking.

Finally, our analysis also reveals an interesting relation between 
stochastic harm and the strength of social preferences that could lead to 
economic ‘‘tipping points’’ when preferences evolve dynamically. When 
social preferences are weak, so that even social investors hold brown 
shares, the hedging benefits of non-pecuniary payoffs hurt the transi-
tion. Once social preferences become sufficiently strong, so that social 
investors go short brown shares, then the hedging effects of stochastic 
social harm will incentivize social investors to take even larger short 
positions, thereby helping a green transition. Thus, in the presence of 
stochastic social harm, even small shifts in green preferences may lead 
to substantial supply effects.

In summary, the analysis in this section provides potentially useful 
advice for policy makers and opens up several directions for research. 
To correctly assess the impact of stochastic social harm, it is crucial 
to find out how it interacts with the pure financial returns of firms. If 
stocks with lower ESG performance – but still held by ESG investors 
– exhibit a higher correlation than those with good ESG performance, 
then the contributions of social preferences towards a green transition 
will be muted or could be even negative. To find out if the latter is true, 
it is important to investigate how prevalent the different types of social 
preferences are. If most social investors are small and are found to care 
about the impact of their investments, then stochastic social harm most 
likely leads to higher demand for brown firms than in a world without 
any social preferences. Only when they coordinate could they mitigate 
or reverse this perverse effect on the supply of green technologies. 
These questions currently appear almost entirely unexplored.

5. Conclusions and outlook

This paper provides a unified framework to analyze the effect of 
different types of social preferences on portfolio decisions, stock prices 
and corporate investment. Social preferences are classified into non-
consequentialist  and  consequentialist  preferences,  where  the  former 
also nest deontological social preferences as a special case. At the core 
of the analysis is the tradeoff between risk sharing and non-pecuniary 
payoffs associated with the different types of social preferences and 
the resulting effects on the supply of green and brown technologies 
(Appendix  C provides a compact summary of our main results).

The analysis highlights the difference between the effects of the 
two main types of social preferences: if social investors are consequen-
tialists, the supply of firms with green technologies depends on an
individual social investor’s internalized harm. If this converges to zero as 
the mass of each individual investor converges to zero in a competitive 
equilibrium, social preferences have zero effect in the limit.

In fact, the only scenario in which competitive consequentialists 
influence equilibrium outcomes is when the level of internalization 
remains constant as the mass of an investor decreases, i.e., if 𝑘 = 0 in 
our model notation. Once the internalized harm decreases as their mass 
shrinks (i.e., 𝑘 > 0), the impact of such social preferences disappears 
completely in the competitive limit.

This is in stark contrast to non-consequentialist preferences, where 
technology supply is shown to depend on the aggregate measure of 
internalized harm, i.e., the product of the aggregate mass of all so-
cial investors and the perceived non-pecuniary payoff per share. This 
quantity does not scale with the mass of the individual social investor. 
As a result, we find that the supply of green technologies remains 
unaffected by whether non-consequentialist social investors act as small 
and competitive agents or coordinate strategically.

Building on these results, we show that consequentialist investors 
have stronger incentives to coordinate than non-consequentialist in-
vestors. This insight provides a novel perspective on trends observed in 
the asset management industry, such as the rise of coordinated engage-
ments. While such platforms offer coordination for impact funds, which 
plausibly cater to consequentialist investors, no similar platforms exist 
for ESG funds that follow passive exclusionary strategies. Consistent 
with the framework developed in this paper, these ESG funds are likely 
to attract non-consequentialist investors, who have weaker incentives 
to coordinate.

We also provide insights into the key determinants of the real effects 
of social preferences. An increase in the fraction of social investors 
leads to stronger equilibrium supply effects when the costs of deviating 
from optimal risk sharing are low, regardless of the specific type of 
preferences considered. Importantly, when the cash flows of brown 
and green firms are highly correlated, social investors become more
effective.  In  other  words,  firms’  technology  supply  is  highly  elastic 
with respect to the fraction of social investors when cash flows be-
tween brown and green firms are highly correlated. This contrasts the 
common view that social investors should have limited influence on 
corporate decisions in such cases, as financial investors can supposedly 
‘‘offset’’ social investors’ portfolio tilts by increasing their own holdings 
in brown firms. Contrary to this perspective, we demonstrate that high 
cash flow correlation facilitates greater influence of social preferences 
on corporate technology decisions, even after accounting for financial 
investors’ optimal portfolio adjustments.

A similar intuition underlies the finding that the real effects of social 
preferences on technology supply become weaker when firms’ cash flow 
volatilities increase and/or investors’ risk aversion increases. In both 
cases, this makes risk-sharing considerations more important, weaken-
ing corporations’ equilibrium response to investors’ social preferences. 
This result is robust in the sense that it does not depend on the type of 
social preferences.

The unified general equilibrium framework also provides insights 
into the relationship between the real effects of social investors and the 

Journal of Financial Economics 172 (2025) 104139 

differences in firms’ cost of capital. The key takeaway is that the impact 
of social investors on firms’ cost of capital offers little information 
about their real economic impact. The effect of an increase in social 
investors on technology supply is either not at all or only weakly related 
to differences in the observed cost of capital. In other words, supply 
effects largely neutralize observed effects on cost-of-capital differences, 
making them a poor measure of the significance of social preferences.
Finally, the paper examines how the results change when social 
harm becomes stochastic. We find that the main findings under deter-
ministic harm remain robust and, in some cases, are even amplified 
under stochastic social harm. When social harm is highly correlated 
with brown firms’ cash flows, brown firms become less risky to so-
cial investors. As a result, if social investors take long positions in 
brown firms in the deterministic case, they optimally increase these 
positions under stochastic harm, thereby hindering a green transition. 
Conversely, if preferences are strong enough for social investors to take 
short positions in brown firms, then a positive correlation between 
harm and brown cash-flows supports the green transition. In this case, 
the hedging effect induces social investors to take even larger short 
positions.

The latter results also have implications for policy setters: if rev-
enues of firms with poor ESG ratings are more highly correlated with 
social harm, then this may make them more attractive as a hedge for 
social investors. Linking Pigou taxes, such as a carbon charge, to these 
firms’ revenues, or requiring them to acquire carbon certificates when 
their production – and thus their emissions – exceed certain thresholds 
could eliminate this hedging property, and thus help a green transition.
Our framework can be extended in various ways, including incor-
porating  the  dynamic  nature  of  portfolio  and  corporate  investment 
decisions, addressing potential disagreement among investors regard-
ing what qualifies as an ethical investment, or accounting for imperfect 
information about firms’ adherence to ethical standards. However, even 
the parsimonious unified model presented in this paper provides valu-
able theoretical insights into how different types of social preferences 
affect equilibrium outcomes and offers a foundation for their empirical 
measurement and analysis.

Equilibrium with many social investors: In contrast to the case 
with only one large social investor, now 𝑁𝑆 identical social investors 
participate in the asset market. For any finite value of 𝑁𝑆 , each social 
investor acts strategically, anticipating the own impact on price-taking 
financial investors, the technology supply of price-taking firms, and 
𝑁𝑆 − 1 other social investors who themselves consider the price impact 
of their demand. In a symmetric Nash equilibrium, the demand function 
of social investors is a fixed point, with all 𝑁𝑆 investors adopting the 
same demand function that is each investor’s optimal response to the 
identical demand function of the other 𝑁𝑆 − 1 investors.

To solve for the equilibrium, we proceed in several steps. First, we 
separate social investors into a single investor, indexed by (𝑆, 𝑖), and 
the remaining investors, indexed by (𝑆, 𝑗) with 𝑗 = 1, … , 𝑁𝑆 − 1. Using 
the market-clearing conditions, we show the price impact of investor 
(𝑆, 𝑖) depends on financial and 𝑁𝑆 − 1 other social investors’ demand-
price sensitivities (i.e., slope of investors’ demand curves). Thus, we 
extend Equation (B.1) (derived in Appendix  B.1) to allow for more than 
one social investor. Second, we derive the demand-price sensitivities 
for financial and 𝑁𝑆 − 1 other social investors, respectively. Third, 
combining the results from the first two steps, we solve the price impact 
of social investors as a fixed point, which then allows us to evaluate the 
supply impact of social investors. Finally, using the solved price and 
supply impact, we solve for a symmetric Nash equilibrium in which all 
social investors use the same demand function.
