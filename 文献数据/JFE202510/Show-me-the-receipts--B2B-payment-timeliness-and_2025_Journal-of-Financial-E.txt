A B S T R A C T

Trade credit is an important source of firm financing, yet its rich informational content pertaining to payment 
timeliness is under-explored in asset pricing. Using an extensive data set from a leading private information 
exchange on business payment performance, we study the effects of trade credit payment timeliness on stock 
returns. We document two distinct channels through which trade credit payment behavior impacts future 
stock returns — slow diffusion of information and risk stemming from a customer firm’s vertical bargaining 
power position in the supply chain. Consistent with our first channel, a sudden delay in a firm’s payment 
to its suppliers predicts significantly lower future returns for its stock. Consistent with our second channel, 
firms that pay their bills moderately late on a consistent basis relative to terms earn significantly higher stock 
returns.

1. Introduction

Trade credit – the short-term financing (e.g., 30, 60, 90 days) that 
a supplier provides to a buyer for the purchase of goods and services – 
is an important source of external finance for firms. About a quarter of 
corporate debt is trade credit, and the size of this trade credit is roughly 
three times the size of bank loans and 15 times the size of commercial 
paper (Rajan and Zingales, 1995; Barrot, 2016; Ivashina et al., 2016; 
Freeman, 2020). Non-financial firms are the main providers of short-
term corporate financing to their customers. Despite the importance 
of trade credit for business-to-business contracting, research of its rich 
information  content  in  asset  pricing  is  limited.1  In  this  paper,  we 
provide novel evidence of the impact of trade credit on asset prices 
by focusing on the timeliness of customer payments.

Although a supplier sets the amount and terms (typically 30 days) of 
trade credit before supply chain transactions, the equilibrium outcome 
includes the actual post-sales payment determined by the downstream 
customer’s  ability  and  willingness  to  pay.  We  explore  two  distinct 
channels relating customer payment behavior to equity prices. First, 
if a firm’s speed of trade credit payment reflects its privately known 
fundamentals, but this information is not widely available or immedi-
ately priced by investors, timely trade credit payment information  can 
lead to predictable returns via a slow diffusion of information channel.

and sharp increases in late payments to suppliers can indicate that a

Second, a firm’s trade credit payment behavior often reflects its 
vertical bargaining power in the supply chain, enabling some firms 
to  consistently  delay  payments  to  suppliers.3  Delaying  payments  to 
suppliers can serve as a tactical working capital strategy that improves 
the focal (customer) firm’s short-term cash flows while reducing the 
need for external short-term financing.4 While this practice may sta-
bilize cash flows from current operations, it also creates rigidity in 
supplier relationships. This rigidity impedes firms’ ability to capitalize 
on new opportunities arising from supply chain pressures, such as pent-
up demand. Consequently, late-paying firms’ growth options become 
less valuable during periods of high aggregate supply chain pressure — 
a state associated with high marginal utility for investors. This reduced 
flexibility makes late payers poorer hedges against systematic supply 
chain pressure risk, leading to higher expected returns.

The prior asset pricing literature has paid little attention to the 
supply chain payment behavior of firms and their asset pricing effects 
due, in part, to the difficulty of obtaining reliable data on trade credit 
payment  transactions.  In  this  paper,  we  provide  direct  tests  of  the 
informational  content  of  trade  credit  payment  timeliness  on  equity 
returns using a proprietary data set of trade credit offerings between 
suppliers and buyers from Dun & Bradstreet (D&B)’s corporate trade 
credit  payment  program.  The  available  data  covers  publicly  listed 
firms headquartered in the U.S. with monthly payment behavior data 
relative to trade credit terms, which are substantially richer than typical 
accounts payable disclosures.5

Our analysis focuses on D&B’s flagship PAYDEX score, which has 
been recognized as the standard in commercial credit reporting for 
more than thirty-five years (e.g., Kallberg and Udell, 2003). The score 
is calculated based on the payment experiences reported to D&B by a 
company’s suppliers and measures the promptness of a company’s bill 
paying relative to agreed terms.6 The score ranges from 1 to 100, with

measure of ‘‘late payment shocks’’ that captures changes in a firm’s

Using our novel measures, we provide two noteworthy findings. 
First, we find that significant increases in the delay of a firm’s payment 
to suppliers predict substantially lower stock returns for the firm. To 
demonstrate this relation, we develop long/short trading strategies that 
take a long position in a value-weighted portfolio of stocks with high

with low 𝑍𝑝𝑎𝑦𝑑𝑒𝑥. Our first headline result is that the zero-investment 
portfolio earns an average excess return of 18 basis points per month 
with a significant t-statistic of 1.81. The strategy alphas range between 
20 and 23 basis points depending on the factor model, with t-statistics 
equal to or exceeding 1.98 in all cases.

While these results are directionally consistent with our hypothesis, 
the payment delay effect is asymmetric. Firms that suddenly start to 
pay significantly late should underperform going forward. However, 
there is no obvious reason to expect that firms which suddenly start 
to pay early will outperform. Early payment usually reflects early pay-
ment discounts, while it is a sub-optimal working capital management 
decision in most other instances. To get around this asymmetric effect, 
we use a decomposition of the PAYDEX score into payment types that 
capture slow and current payment behavior separately. We proceed to 
calculate two additional scores, 𝑍𝑠𝑙𝑜𝑤 and 𝑍𝑐𝑢𝑟𝑟𝑒𝑛𝑡, that are similarly

dollars payable to suppliers. 𝑍𝑐𝑢𝑟𝑟𝑒𝑛𝑡 captures shocks in a firm’s balance 
of ‘‘current’’ dollars payable to suppliers. The long/short strategy based 
on 𝑍𝑠𝑙𝑜𝑤 yields an average monthly return of 37 basis points, with a 
statistically significant t-statistic of 3.43. We also obtain similar alphas, 
which range between 41 and 46 basis points per month, with t-statistics

signal does not produce economically nor statistically robust average 
returns and alphas.

firms’ ability to adapt to supply chain pressures and limits their growth

Our  paper  contributes  to  several  important  bodies  of  research, 
starting with the extensive literature on trade credit. One key strand 
of this literature explains the existence of trade credit as financing 
of last resort, with suppliers offering trade credit when financing via 
bank credit or securities issuance is costly or problematic (e.g., Meltzer, 
1960; Petersen and Rajan, 1997; Nilsen, 2002; Fisman and Love, 2003; 
Love et al., 2007; Atanasova, 2007; Molina and Preve, 2012). A second 
strand of this trade credit literature focuses on suppliers’ informational 
advantage over other credit providers about their customers’ businesses 
(e.g., Goto et al., 2015; Ivashina and Iverson, 2018; Hirshleifer et al., 
2020). A third important strand of this trade credit literature examines 
the heterogeneity of trade credit terms in customer–supplier interac-
tions (e.g., Fabbri and Klapper, 2008; Klapper et al., 2012; Murfin and 
Njoroge, 2015; Barrot, 2016; Wu et al., 2023; Billett et al., 2021).

Our  research  bridges  these  three  strands  of  the  trade  credit  lit-
erature.  In  one  set  of  results,  we  show  that  a  sudden,  significant 
delay in a customer firm’s payments to its suppliers predicts an under-
performance  of  its  future  stock  returns.  Our  results  provide  novel 
evidence contributing to the first two strands of the trade credit lit-
erature characterizing trade credit as a financing of last resort and 
also suggest suppliers’ significant informational advantage regarding 
their customers. Additionally, we find that customers who consistently 
pay moderately late earn excess returns, contributing to the literature 
on trade credit heterogeneity by demonstrating how payment timing 
reflects bargaining power and impacts systematic supply-chain risk. In 
doing so, we also fill an existing gap in quantifying trade credit effects 
on customers as trade credit payment behavior from the customer point 
of view has been rarely studied because data containing details of trade 
credit payment transactions are difficult to obtain.

Second, our paper also contributes to a large asset pricing literature 
on the slow diffusion of information (Hong and Stein, 1999). One part 
of this literature suggests that equity investors have limited attention

and do not immediately incorporate all publicly available information 
into stock prices (e.g., Ball and Brown, 1968; Hirshleifer and Teoh, 
2003; Picconi, 2006; Johnston et al., 2012). Further, investors lag in 
incorporating public information especially when it is harder to process 
and imposes a greater cognitive burden (e.g., Cohen et al., 2013a,b; Ko-
rniotis and Kumar, 2013; Fitzgerald et al., 2021). We contribute to this 
literature with unique evidence that the speed of trade credit payments 
reflects  company  fundamentals,  but  such  information  is  not  widely 
available nor immediately priced by investors. Instead, changes in pay-
ment timeliness are impounded in stock prices consistent with a slow 
diffusion of information channel so that increases in payment delays 
predict firm’s stock returns over subsequent months. Our late payment 
shock signals are positively correlated with the firm’s likelihood of 
distress, negative momentum in the firm’s recent stock performance, 
and predict negative abnormal returns around the firm’s future earnings 
announcements. Our research implicitly sheds light on whether formal 
business credit information exchanges could be informative to equity 
investors.

Lastly, we contribute to the recent literature on vertical relations 
and asset prices. Grigoris et al. (2023) argue that trade credit reduces 
the departure probability of high-quality customers, whereby reducing 
trade credit extends firms’ exposures to systematic costs incurred in 
finding new customers. Ahern (2012)  suggests  that  product market 
relations determine firms’ bargaining power and affect the division of 
gains in mergers. Gofman et al. (2020) argue that a firm’s relative 
position in the production network (i.e., distance from final consumers) 
has key implications for its risk and returns. In a real options model of 
strategic bargaining between a customer and a supplier, Bustamante 
(2021)  shows  that  the  firm  with  higher  vertical  bargaining  power 
extracts a larger fraction of the joint future value of the supply chain 
and enhances firms’ values. We add to this literature by showing how 
firms’ bargaining power in customer–supplier relations summarized by 
trade credit late payment behavior predicts asset prices, irrespective 
of firms’ locations in the supply chain. We also document the micro-
level manifestations and outcomes of firms’ vertical bargaining power 
through their trade credit behavior.7

2. Hypothesis development

Our  study  examines  whether  the  timeliness  of  customers’  trade 
credit payments can be informative for the cross-section of stock re-
turns. We hypothesize two distinct channels through which trade credit 
payment  behavior  relates  to  future  stock  returns:  slow  diffusion  of 
information and vertical bargaining power of customer firms. While 
both of these channels reflect payment behavior, they have widely 
disparate predictions about how the promptness of payments impacts 
future stock returns.

2.1. Slow diffusion of information channel

One stream of research argues that firms extend financing to their 
corporate customers because they have an information advantage over 
financiers when dealing with adverse selection (Petersen and Rajan, 
1997) or with moral hazard (Burkart and Ellingsen, 2004). Consistent 
with  suppliers  having  an  informational  advantage  about  their  cus-
tomers’ businesses, the amount of trade credit that a supplier offers 
to a buyer is positively associated with future buyer financial perfor-
mance and solvency (Goto et al., 2015; Hirshleifer et al., 2020). Once 
trade credit is extended, however, the ability to pay the trade credit 
and the timeliness thereof should contain critical information about a 
customer’s future prospects.

Sudden delays in payment are likely to indicate negative cash flow 
shocks and increased likelihood of distress. To this point, Hill et al. 
(2010) and Lee (2011) show that firms nearing financial distress tend 
to be more aggressive in their working capital management, collecting 
faster from clients and delaying payments to suppliers. Further, Molina 
and Preve (2012) provide evidence that financially distressed compa-
nies use more trade credit and take longer to repay their trade payables 
than when they were not in financial distress, consistent with firms 
in financial distress accumulating debt with suppliers by delaying or 
stopping payment.

We thus hypothesize that sharp increases in trade credit payment 
delay  predict  negative  future  stock  returns.  We  conjecture  that  in-
vestors are unable to immediately incorporate information about the 
timeliness of a firm’s payment practices into its stock price. Information 
about trade credit payment delays is initially only available to the 
firm’s direct suppliers, while not available to the marginal investor. 
Imperfect trade credit information is available in financial statements 
only at the quarterly frequency subject to filing delays. If investors 
are slow to incorporate this information, we expect that the significant 
deterioration of how promptly a customer pays its suppliers should lead 
to a pronounced decrease in its stock price. An investor with access to 
information about a firm’s payment practices should be able to exploit 
this information and design profitable trading strategies in the cross-
section of stock returns, a result we show in Section 4.1. Importantly, a 
negative shock to a firm’s payment behavior caused by financial distress 
would only be incorporated into its stock price once investors realize 
that the firm is in distress. If our conjecture is correct, we expect the 
information contained in trade credit payment delays to be impounded 
into stock prices around future earnings announcements, which we 
show in turn in Section 3.3.3.

Theoretically,  our  hypothesis  regarding  sharp  increases  in  trade 
credit payment delays predicting negative stock returns can be mo-
tivated based on Hirshleifer et al. (2011), who develop a model in 
which investors with limited attention, or alternatively limited infor-
mation acquisition and information processing ability, neglect relevant 
earnings-related information. This subsequently results in slow diffu-
sion of information and (temporary) misvaluation. We conjecture that 
this mechanism will lead to cross-sectional return predictability, which 
will not be explained by standard factor models.

To further illustrate our hypothesis, consider Liquid Holdings Group, 
Inc. (ticker: LIQD). Liquid was a software service and risk management 
technology  provider  that  catered  to  the  securities  and  hedge  fund 
industry.  Fig.   1  plots  the  stock  price  (left  axis)  of  Liquid  Holdings 
along with its PAYDEX score (right axis) between September 2013 and 
March 2014. As we detail in Section 3, PAYDEX is a score that assesses 
the timeliness of payments made to a firm’s suppliers (a score of 80 
indicates prompt, or on-time, payments; a score greater than 80 – early 
payments; a score less than 80 – late payments).

In late 2013, Liquid experienced a significant negative cash-flow 
shock due to trouble with its major customer QuantX, which accounted 
for a substantial portion of Liquid’s revenues. The issue spilled over to 
Liquid’s ability to pay its suppliers. As a result, Liquid’s PAYDEX score, 
while being basically constant at 80 until then, suddenly collapsed to

2.2. Vertical bargaining power channel

While  late  trade  credit  payment  behavior  can  indicate  financial 
distress, it could also reflect a customer’s ability to pay its suppliers 
late, especially if that behavior is observed consistently. This conjecture 
is consistent with prior research showing that trade credit interactions 
reflect the borrower’s market power in its downstream product market 
(e.g., Brennan et al., 1988; Murfin and Njoroge, 2015; Barrot, 2016; 
Giannetti et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2023).

Firms that consistently pay late can leverage several operational 
benefits. Late payments serve as a working capital strategy, freeing 
up cash for various uses such as operational investments or financing 
of  receivables.  This  practice  helps  customers  lower  financing  costs 
by  reducing  their  need  for  expensive  short-term  credit  (Fabbri  and 
Klapper, 2008; Klapper et al., 2012). Payment delays also enhance cus-
tomers’ negotiating position with suppliers regarding price, quality, and 
quantity. These benefits primarily affect assets in place by stabilizing 
current operations and improving short-term cash flows.

The ability to sustain late payments without severe consequences 
stems from suppliers’ desire to maintain important supply chain re-
lationships  and  their  reliance  on  customers  for  future  sales.  While 
suppliers can technically impose late payment penalties, many waive 
these fees, particularly for important trade partners (Klapper et al., 
2012; Fabbri and Klapper, 2016; Wu et al., 2023).8 As a result, market-
dominant customers can often compel suppliers to bear the cost of 
extended payment terms (Hofmann and Kotzab, 2010; Barrot, 2016; 
Murfin and Njoroge, 2015).

The  rent  extraction  through  late  payments,  however,  cannot  be 
infinite and is ultimately balanced against potential deadweight costs 
from supplier distress, particularly in specialized relationships. Conse-
quently, more moderate forms of late payment are more likely to persist 
than severe payment delays. These arguments lead us to hypothesize 
that firms with high vertical bargaining power are able to optimize a 
consistent late payment pattern, allowing them to tactically extract cash 
flows from their suppliers without distressing them.

However, even moderate late payment practices come with signif-
icant costs, particularly regarding firms’ growth options and exposure 
to systematic risk. When firms regularly delay payments, they become 
deeply embedded in existing supplier relationships that are costly to 
diversify or replace (e.g., Acemoglu and Tahbaz-Salehi, 2025). This en-
trenchment creates rigidity in their supply chain structure. While such 
rigidity may stabilize current operations, it fundamentally impairs these 
firms’ ability to adapt when aggregate supply chain pressures increase. 
During periods of high supply chain pressure – which we hypothesize 
represent states of high marginal utility for investors – firms can often 
capitalize on emerging opportunities like pent-up demand by flexibly 
adjusting their supplier relationships. However, consistent late-paying 
firms find themselves constrained by their existing ‘‘sticky’’ supplier 
relationships.

3.2. PAYDEX score

3.1. Dun & Bradstreet’s Credit Score Archive Database

Dun & Bradstreet (D&B) collects many different types of information 
about a company’s business activities including its public filings (liens, 
lawsuits, judgments, bankruptcy) and financial statements. Arguably, 
however, the most valuable data that D&B collects is payment history, 
the vast majority of which is trade payment history (Kallberg and Udell, 
2003). At the core of D&B’s database is a proprietary trade program 
composed of over 15,000 suppliers across 60 countries that collect de-
tailed payment (invoice) information on millions of public and private 
companies worldwide.10 The quality and the reliability of D&B’s data

In broad terms, a lower PAYDEX score means that a firm should be 
considered less credit-worthy because it pays its bills later. This could 
imply that a firm does not have the cash on hand to pay its suppliers 
and therefore has to rely on trade credit to make payments — which is a 
significant risk for prospective business partners or commercial lenders. 
However, this relation could be complicated due to several factors. On 
the one hand, small firms can pay later because they do not have the 
cash on hand to pay their suppliers. On the other hand, the largest 
publicly traded firms could afford to pay later, not because they do not 
have cash, but because they have the market power over their suppliers 
to do so. We proceed with introducing our two primary trading signals 
that we use to test our hypotheses.

3.4.1. Definition of  LPC

In our second hypothesis discussed in Section 2.2, we argue that 
vertical bargaining power should play a special role in the context of 
trade credit payment timeliness. On the one hand, bargaining power 
enables buyers to stretch terms and pay their suppliers consistently late. 
This tendency, however, should be reflected ex-ante in the payment 
terms of trade credit rather than late payment relative to agreed upon 
terms (e.g., Klapper et al., 2012). Additionally, late payment is to be 
balanced with expected deadweight costs associated with the negative 
effects of late payment on suppliers (e.g., supplier distress as in Barrot, 
2016).

We thus a priori expect that powerful customers will have a tendency 
to  pay  moderately  late  on  a  consistent  basis,  but  they  should  not 
deviate  much  from  payment  terms.  While  defining  a  ‘‘theoretically 
correct’’ measure to capture bargaining power in the speed of trade 
credit  payment  is  admittedly  challenging,  we  proceed  with  a  two-

4. Trading on late payment signals

This section reports our main results from trading strategies based 
on our previously introduced measures of trade credit payment timeli-
ness, 𝑍𝑝𝑎𝑦𝑑𝑒𝑥 and 𝐿𝑃 𝐶.

4.1. Late payment shocks and slow diffusion of information

We start with testing our hypothesis in Section 2.1 that late payment 
shocks (i.e., sudden delays in trade credit payment) capture a firm’s 
cash-flow position deterioration and increased propensity of distress. 
If these shocks are not immediately incorporated into a firm’s stock 
price, one could expect these stocks’ prices to under-perform when 
information is subsequently revealed to the market (e.g., during subse-
quent earnings announcements or other significant information events 
for companies).

Table   7  reports  average  returns,  alphas,  and  Fama  and  French 
(2018) six-factor-loadings for our baseline strategy seeking to exploit 
the  ‘‘late  payment  shock’’  hypothesis.  Panel  A  reports  results  for  a 
long/short trading strategy that takes a long position in the portfolio 
of stocks with high 𝑍𝑝𝑎𝑦𝑑𝑒𝑥 and a short position in the portfolio of 
stocks with low 𝑍𝑝𝑎𝑦𝑑𝑒𝑥. The strategy yields an average monthly return 
of 18 basis points, with a significant t-statistic of 1.81.19 We observe 
similar patterns in the alphas, which range between 20 (CAPM) and 
23 (FF6) basis points per month, with t-statistics exceeding 1.98 in all 
cases. Panel B shows that the long/short 𝑍𝑝𝑎𝑦𝑑𝑒𝑥 strategy does not load 
significantly on any of the six Fama and French (2018) factors other 
than the market factor, which has a loading of −0.05 with a t-statistic 
of −1.82.

While the results from our baseline strategy confirm our hypothesis, 
an important observation about these results is that the ‘‘late payment 
shock’’ effect is not symmetric. Specifically, there is no obvious reason
a priori to expect that firms with significantly positive PAYDEX shocks

We thus next refine our ‘‘late payment shock’’ baseline trading strat-
egy to address this asymmetry issue. Using information on the amount 
of (payable) dollars ‘‘slow’’ and dollars ‘‘current’’ that each company 
has in a month, we decompose the PAYDEX score into payment classes 
that capture slow and current payment behavior separately (see Table 
1). We expect firms that experience sudden increases in slow dollars to 
underperform (i.e., implying negative cash flow shocks which increase 
likelihood of distress), while firms that experience sudden decreases in 
their slow dollars should outperform (i.e., implying positive cash flow 
shocks which decrease likelihood of distress). By using information in a 
company’s ‘‘slow’’ dollars only, we eliminate the uninformativeness of 
firms’ early or prompt payment behavior. Changes in a firm’s current 
dollar position should not be informative about a company’s return 
performance.  Specifically, a positive shock in current dollars might 
be indicative of an expansion in business operations with a sudden 
near-term increase in expenses, but also, a potential future increase in 
revenues. Not only is this a different signal, it also lacks the insight that 
shocks in slow dollars have regarding the changes in cash flow.

(increases/decreases)  in  a  firm’s  balance  of  ‘‘slow’’  dollars  payable 
to suppliers. 𝑍𝑐𝑢𝑟𝑟𝑒𝑛𝑡 captures shocks (increases/decreases) in a firm’s 
balance of ‘‘current’’ dollars payable to suppliers. We then execute two

positions in a portfolio of stocks with low 𝑍𝑠𝑙𝑜𝑤 and 𝑍𝑐𝑢𝑟𝑟𝑒𝑛𝑡 scores,

4.2.2. Alternative explanations

Our vertical bargaining power hypothesis posits that vertical bar-
gaining  power  commands  a  risk  premium,  which  results  in  LPC’s 
significant predictive power over stock returns. However, there can be 
alternative explanations, which we explore and account for in this sec-
tion. Our methodology is Fama-MacBeth regressions — parametric tests 
of whether cross-sectional variation in LPC predicts future stock returns 
after accounting for the alternative predictors. All of our specifications 
control  for  well-known  cross-sectional  predictability  of  other  stock-
specific variables including variables from the Fama and French (2015) 
factor model such as the log of market capitalization (log(ME)), the log 
of the book-to-market ratio (log(B/M)), gross profitability (GP/A), and 
asset growth (AG). The GP/A variable follows Novy-Marx (2013) and 
the AG variable follows Cooper et al. (2008). The Fama-MacBeth speci-
fications also include controls for one-year price momentum (𝑟12,1), and 
monthly reversals (𝑟1,0) since these variables have been shown to have 
strong monthly predictability.

Table  11, columns (1) and (2) show the results from our baseline 
specifications, excluding or including LPC. Consistent with recent stud-
ies documenting attenuated performance of known anomalies over our 
sample period (e.g., Chordia et al., 2014; Chen and Velikov, 2023), 
most of the control variables have coefficients that are indistinguishable 
from zero. The two exceptions are AG and 𝑟1,0 – both variables signif-
icantly negatively predict stock returns. Column (2) also specifically 
shows that, over the same period, LPC significantly positively predicts 
returns with a coefficient of 0.39 and t-statistic of 3.14.

The first alternative explanation we examine relates to Novy-Marx 
(2011),  who  shows  that  firms  with  higher  operating  leverage  earn 
significantly higher average returns than firms with lower operating 
leverage. If financial or non-financial penalties associated with con-
sistent late payment result in higher operating leverage, then LPC’s 
return predictability can in fact be driven by operating leverage. To 
address this possibility, we calculate Novy-Marx (2011)’s measure of 
operating leverage (Operating Leverage), which we then include in our 
Fama-MacBeth specifications. Table  11, column (3) shows that oper-
ating leverage does not significantly predict returns in our sample.
LPC preserves its predictive ability (coefficient of 0.40 and t-statistic 
of 3.28) even after controlling for operating leverage.22 We conclude 
that operating leverage is an unlikely explanation of our results in the 
previous section.

The second alternative explanation we examine relates to Gofman 
et al. (2020), who show that firms that are farther away from con-
sumers in their production network (i.e., upstream firms) have higher 
risk premiums. Given that companies’ trade credit policies (e.g., extend-
ing or receiving trade credit) are related to firms’ positions in the pro-
duction network (Gofman and Wu, 2022; Grigoris et al., 2023), it can 
be that firms’ ‘‘upstreamness’’ is driving the return predictability of LPC. 
To address this possibility, we include in our Fama-MacBeth regressions 
the measure of firms’ industry-level vertical positions (Upstreamness) 
that captures each industry’s weighted distance from the production 
of  final  consumption  goods  (Antrás  et  al.,  2012;  Antrás  and  Chor, 
2018).23 Table  11, column (4) shows that a firm’s upstreamness does

The  third  alternative  explanation  we  examine  relates  to  Wang 
(2019), who shows that a firm’s cash conversion cycle (CCC) predicts 
returns — firms with shorter CCCs earn higher returns. CCC is defined 
as the number of days that a firm’s cash is tied up within the operation

management and intrinsic need for external financing. To the extent 
that the consistently late payment of trade credit is associated with

conversion cycle measure (CCC), which we subsequently include in 
our Fama-MacBeth specifications. Table  11, column (5) shows that in

conversion cycle is also an unlikely explanation of our results in the 
previous section.

While we have documented the predictive power of LPC for stock 
returns and its robustness, it is also important to provide evidence 
that this relationship is not merely a statistical artifact. To this end, 
we  conclude  this  section  with  a  falsification  test,  which  serves  to 
examine  whether  the  risk  premium  we  have  identified  is  uniquely 
associated with moderately late payments, as our vertical bargaining 
power hypothesis suggests, or if it is a feature of general payment 
timing consistency.

For our falsification test, we define two alternative LPC measures. 
𝐿𝑃 𝐶0−50 is the percentage of instances where a firm’s PAYDEX score 
falls within the low region [0, 50] over the last sixty months. 𝐿𝑃 𝐶80−100
is the percentage of instances where the firm’s PAYDEX score falls 
within the high region [80, 100]. Then, using Fama-MacBeth regres-
sions, we examine whether 𝐿𝑃 𝐶0−50 and 𝐿𝑃 𝐶80−100 have explanatory 
power over returns. 𝐿𝑃 𝐶0−50 has a coefficient in column (10) of −0.85 
with a t-statistic of −0.94, while 𝐿𝑃 𝐶80−100 has a coefficient in column 
(11) of 0.08 with a t-statistic of 0.46. Importantly, these results are in 
stark contrast to those with our LPC measure, which, as shown earlier, 
has a statistically significant coefficient of 0.39 with a t-statistic of 3.14 
in column (2). These findings provide support to the idea that there is 
indeed inherent significance to firms consistently making moderately 
late payments, as opposed to general payment consistency or extreme 
payment behaviors.

4.2.3. The macro primitive of late payment consistency

We hypothesize in Section 2.2 that the 𝐿𝑃 𝐶 premium arises because 
firms with high vertical bargaining power extract rents from the sup-
ply chain, which limits their flexibility during times of supply chain 
disruptions. In this section, we examine a range of macro shocks as 
the source of 𝐿𝑃 𝐶’s predictability in standard GMM asset pricing tests 
following prior literature (e.g., Belo et al., 2019; Grigoris et al., 2023).

statistically significantly different from zero at conventional levels. The 
alphas on the spread portfolio for the rest of the models are roughly 
double, ranging between 45–51 basis points per month, with t-statistics

with the macro shocks (𝑀𝐴𝐶𝑅𝑂𝑡). It shows that high 𝐿𝑃 𝐶 firms, those 
with high vertical bargaining power, tend to have lower covariances 
with changes in the GSCPI. That is, high 𝐿𝑃 𝐶 stocks tend to perform 
relatively worse when the GSCPI goes up, which are times of supply 
chain pressures. Panel C of Table  12 shows that GSCPI carries a nega-
tive price of risk. We estimate 𝑏𝐺𝑆𝐶𝑃 𝐼  using two sets of test assets. For

reduction in Mean Absolute Error (MAE) from 0.17 to 0.09 for 𝐿𝑃 𝐶
portfolios and from 0.53 to 0.27 for broader test assets. Thus, high 
bargaining power is associated with lower exposure to the GSCPI factor, 
but due to the factor’s negative price of risk, high LPC stocks are riskier 
and, consequently, have higher expected returns.

These  results  imply  that  while  supply  chain  pressures  generally 
represent adverse economic conditions, stocks on average have positive 
exposures  to  the  GSCPI,  suggesting  that  equity  markets  can  hedge 
against global supply chain pressures. Arguably, a possible explanation 
of this result is that rising supply chain pressures increase firms’ growth 
option values due to, for example, pent-up demand. We further explore 
this conjecture in Table OA.A.5 of our Online Appendix A, which shows 
that  intensifying  supply  chain  pressures  are  indeed  associated  with 
increasing values of firms growth options. However, consistent with our 
hypothesis in Section 2.2, while we find that supply chain shocks are 
associated with increasing firm growth option values, high LPC firms 
do not see their growth options increase in value with rising GSCPI 
as much as other firms, offering an inferior hedge for investors during 
periods of intensifying supply chain pressures.

6. Conclusion

While trade credit has rich informational content, it has been largely 
under-explored in asset pricing. Trade credit payment behavior is im-
portant for several reasons. The use of trade credit is economically 
important, prevalent, and payment behavior varies significantly across 
firms. Understanding the information content of trade credit payment 
timeliness and how it relates to asset prices is thus important. The 
topic of supply chain payments is also of interest to policy makers. 
In fact, late trade credit payments are a global concern (e.g., Council 
of the European Union, 2011). In this paper, we provide direct tests 
of the informational content of trade credit by analyzing the speed 
of customer payment in the context of equity prices. In doing so, we 
use a proprietary dataset of trade credit offerings between suppliers 
and buyers from Dun & Bradstreet’s corporate trade credit payment 
program.

We offer two sets of core results. First, we show that a sudden delay 
in a firm’s payment to its suppliers predicts the firm’s substantially 
lower future stock returns. We interpret these shocks to payment time-
liness as an early indication that a company is struggling to generate 
cash flow and is facing distress risk. Consistent with a slow diffusion 
of information channel, it takes time for investors to incorporate in-
formation contained in payment delays into stock prices. Second, we 
show that firms that pay bills moderately and consistently late relative 
to terms earn substantially higher stock returns. We conjecture that this 
behavior captures customer bargaining power in the supply chain. This 
consistent late payment behavior, while advantageous in stabilizing 
operations,  may  limit  the  firm’s  ability  to  adapt  quickly  to  supply 
chain disruptions, increasing perceived risk and contributing to higher 
expected returns as compensation for reduced operational flexibility. 
Overall, we confirm the rich informational content of trade credit and 
show two direct asset pricing implications. Our results are novel in 
that they span two disparate channels with very different underlying 
economic mechanisms.

This Appendix presents an example illustrating how the PAYDEX 
score is calculated. In this case, the focal firm has seven qualifying 
payment records shown in Table  A.1, Panel A (see Section 3.2 for a 
discussion of qualifying payment records). D&B starts by separating 
the payment records into payment classes (i.e., ‘‘discount’’, ‘‘prompt’’, 
‘‘slow 30’’, ‘‘slow 60’’). D&B then sums all the high credits for each 
payment class and calculates the percentages of total amount. (A ‘‘high 
credit’’ is the highest amount owed by the focal firm to a supplier over 
the life of a payment record transaction.) This is illustrated with an 
example in Table  A.1, Panel B.

D&B then proceeds by multiplying each percentage of total amount 
for each payment class by the corresponding Index weight for that 
payment class, which determines a class score. Class scores are then 
added to calculate an overall PAYDEX score. This process, along with
