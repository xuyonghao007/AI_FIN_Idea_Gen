1. Introduction

Short sellers are widely known to be informed, which would typically suggest that they demand liquidity. 
We obtain comprehensive transaction-level data to decompose daily short volume into liquidity-demanding 
and liquidity-supplying components. Contrary to conventional wisdom, we show that the most informed short 
sellers are actually liquidity suppliers, not liquidity demanders. They are particularly informative about future 
returns on news days and trade on prominent cross-sectional return anomalies. Our analysis suggests that 
market making and opportunistic risk-bearing are unlikely to explain these findings. Instead, our results align 
with recent market microstructure theory, pointing to strategic liquidity provision by informed traders.

There is a long-standing paradox in the short selling literature: Short 
sellers, despite being widely recognized as informed investors, are also 
associated with improved market liquidity.1 This puzzle arises because, 
in the standard microstructure literature, market makers react to the 
presence of informed trading by widening bid–ask spreads to protect 
against adverse selection, thereby reducing liquidity (e.g., Glosten and 
Milgrom, 1985 and Kyle, 1985). This raises an important question: How 
can short sellers, as a whole, simultaneously be informed and contribute 
positively to liquidity?

The  natural  explanation  for  this  paradox  is  that  there  are  two 
types of short sellers. One type comprises the informed short sellers 
who use market orders and demand liquidity to act quickly on their 
informational advantage. The other type comprises (voluntary) market 
makers who place uninformed limit orders, thereby supplying liquidity 
(Boehmer et al., 2008 and Comerton-Forde et al., 2016).

In this paper, we shed light on the informational advantage of short 
sellers and examine which short trades are more informative about 
future returns: those that demand liquidity or those that supply it. 
Surprisingly, we find that liquidity-supplying short sellers are actually 
more  informed  than  liquidity-demanding  short  sellers,  a  result  that 
contradicts much of the existing literature in this area.

One of the main challenges in separating short sellers into liquidity 
demanders and liquidity suppliers is the limited granularity of com-
monly used short sale data, which are available at the stock level on 
a daily basis and do not distinguish between different motives driving 
short seller behavior. These aggregated measures potentially mask the 
heterogeneous effects of different types of short sales on return pre-
dictability. To overcome this challenge, we (1) obtain comprehensive 
transaction-level short sale data from each of the eleven main U.S. 
equity exchanges over a five-year period from 2014 through 2018 and 
(2) employ a three-step matching algorithm to link each short sale 
trade to the prevailing market quotes. By using trade and quote prices

We begin our analysis by replicating a well-documented pattern 
in  the  cross-section  of  stock  returns:  stocks  with  a  high  short  sale 
volume to total trading volume ratio underperform those with a low 
short  volume  ratio  (see,  e.g.,  Boehmer  et  al.,  2008;  Diether  et  al., 
2009; and Rapach et al., 2016). More importantly, when we decom-

liquidity-demanding short volume ratio (𝐿𝐷𝑆), we uncover interesting 
heterogeneous patterns. Contrary to conventional wisdom, our portfolio 
analysis shows that only 𝐿𝑆𝑆 negatively predicts future equity returns. 
In particular, stocks in the highest 𝐿𝑆𝑆 quintile underperform those in 
the lowest quintile by a risk-adjusted return of 38 basis points (bps) 
over  a  21-day  holding  period.  In  contrast,  the  predictive  power  of 
𝐿𝐷𝑆 over the same holding period is much weaker, at just 12 bps, 
which is statistically indistinguishable from zero and driven entirely 
by the return on the day after portfolio formation. In cross-sectional 
regressions, we show that the return predictability associated with 𝐿𝑆𝑆
is not subsumed when controlling for other well-known short selling 
metrics and standard return predictors. This suggests that liquidity-
supplying short sales contain unique information about future stock 
returns. In additional tests, we find that our documented predictabil-
ity is neither concentrated in stocks with specific characteristics, nor 
driven by particular time periods or samples of stocks, and it holds 
for various alternative holding periods. Overall, our results indicate 
strong predictability of future returns from liquidity-supplying short 
sales, while such predictability is absent for liquidity-demanding short 
sales over holding periods longer than one day. These findings suggest 
that liquidity-supplying short sales may represent informed trading by 
short sellers with relatively long-lived information.

In addition to informed trading, the return predictability associated 
with liquidity-supplying short sales might partly reflect compensation 
for voluntary market making or opportunistic risk bearing (Diether 
et  al.,  2009).  To  explore  this  possibility,  we  use  a  decomposition 
method  similar  to  that  of  Boehmer  et  al.  (2021b)  to  separate  the 
variation in 𝐿𝑆𝑆 into proxies for these two components and a residual 
component. Our findings show that only the residual component drives 
the  predictive  power  of  𝐿𝑆𝑆  for  future  returns,  while  the  market-
making  and  risk-bearing  components  do  not  predict  cross-sectional 
stock returns in a statistically significant way.

We shed further light on the nature of return predictability and 
its link to the informational advantage inherent in liquidity-supplying 
short sales. First, we use a framework similar to that of Engelberg et al. 
(2012), and examine the predictive power of 𝐿𝑆𝑆 for stock returns 
around  firm-specific  news  release  days.  If  the  predictive  ability  of 
liquidity-supplying short sales stems from an informational advantage, 
then 𝐿𝑆𝑆 should predict returns particularly on those days when news 
is released and information is incorporated into stock prices. Consistent 
with this hypothesis, we find that liquidity-supplying short sales are 
particularly informative about future returns on news days compared 
to non-news days. Additionally, as with our decomposition method, we 
demonstrate that this increased predictive power is present exclusively 
in the residual component of 𝐿𝑆𝑆, further supporting the notion that 
liquidity-supplying shorts are, on average, informed about company 
fundamentals.

As a second empirical exercise, we study whether liquidity-supplying 
short  sellers  use  their  informational  advantage  and  trade  on  long-
term information embedded in cross-sectional equity anomalies. We 
follow  Wang  et  al.  (2020)  in  analyzing  over-  and  undervaluation

ranks of stocks in portfolios sorted on short volume ratio. We find 
that liquidity-supplying short sellers react to information embedded in 
anomalies by shorting overvalued stocks more than undervalued ones. 
The same is not true for liquidity-demanding shorting flows. Overall, 
our findings indicate that informed short sellers capitalize on longer-
term price decreases by supplying liquidity rather than taking it out of 
the market (see, e.g., Kaniel and Liu, 2006 and Riccò et al., 2024).

To corroborate that liquidity-supplying short sellers trade on long-
term  information,  we  examine  whether  the  return  predictability  of 
liquidity-supplying short sales is concentrated on trading venues that 
favor patient trading strategies. In particular, we exploit heterogene-
ity in exchange fee structures and find that the predictive power of 
𝐿𝑆𝑆  is  driven  almost  entirely  by  trades  executed  on  maker-taker 
venues–venues  that  reward  passive  limit  order  submission  and  are 
more attractive to traders acting on long-lived information. In contrast, 
𝐿𝑆𝑆 on inverted venues, which incentivize more aggressive limit order 
placement, is not predictive of longer-run returns. These findings are 
consistent with the theoretical predictions in Malinova and Park (2015) 
and Battalio et al. (2016), who emphasize how exchange fee structures 
influence the behavior of informed traders.

Our findings contribute to several important strands of literature. 
First, we add to the extensive body of research on the role of short 
sellers as informed traders. While previous studies have documented 
that short selling activity predicts lower future returns and reflects 
valuable information about firm fundamentals (see, e.g., Aitken et al., 
1998; Asquith et al., 2005; Boehmer et al., 2008; Christophe et al., 
2010;  Dechow  et  al.,  2001;  Desai  et  al.,  2002;  Deshmukh  et  al., 
2015; Diether et al., 2009; Engelberg et al., 2025; and Rapach et al., 
2016), these studies typically rely on aggregated short sale measures, 
which mask the different strategies short sellers employ to leverage 
their informational advantage. By disaggregating short sale activity into 
liquidity-supplying  and  liquidity-demanding  components,  our  paper 
advances the understanding of how informed short sellers operate and 
how their trades contribute to the efficient incorporation of information 
into  stock  prices.  This  distinction  is  crucial  for  evaluating  whether 
informed short sellers are inherently detrimental to market liquidity 
or if they can simultaneously enhance liquidity through their trading 
activities.

The study closest to ours is Comerton-Forde et al. (2016), who also 
differentiate between short sales that provide liquidity and those that 
demand it. Since these authors find that liquidity-demanding shorts 
are followed by lower returns, the clever reader might notice that our 
results seem at odds with theirs. Appendix  C provides a reconciliation 
of our results with those of Comerton-Forde et al. (2016). The key 
distinction  is  that  their  work  focuses  on  intraday  trading,  whereas 
we study longer-term return predictability. Our results suggest that 
informed short sellers capitalize on longer-term information by sup-
plying liquidity. In general, our findings are consistent with the idea 
that informed short sellers play a key role in price efficiency, without 
necessarily compromising market liquidity.

Second, our research engages with the microstructure literature on 
the interaction between informed trading and liquidity provision. Tra-
ditional models, such as those proposed by Glosten (1994), Glosten and 
Milgrom (1985) and Kyle (1985), suggest that informed traders typi-
cally demand liquidity, which contrasts with our findings. Our findings 
align with more recent theoretical work (see, e.g., Brolley and Mali-
nova, 2021; Chakravarty and Holden, 1995; Goettler et al., 2009; Hol-
lifield et al., 2004; Kaniel and Liu, 2006; Riccò et al., 2024; Roşu, 
2020; and Seppi and Kumar, 1994), which posits that informed traders 
may strategically supply liquidity, particularly when their information 
is  long-lived  and  there  is  no  immediate  need  for  trade  execution. 
Similarly, this perspective is supported by a handful of recent empirical 
studies that show informed traders, such as insiders, often use limit 
orders or time their trades to minimize market impact and avoid reveal-
ing their informational advantage (see, e.g., Ahern, 2020; Akey et al., 
2022; Collin-Dufresne and Fos, 2015; and Kacperczyk and Pagnotta,

2. Data

This section introduces our sample and provides descriptive statis-
tics. Section  Section 2.1 explains our sample construction of intraday

2.1. Sample construction

We use the NYSE Trade and Quote (TAQ) dataset, which provides 
comprehensive  transaction  level  data  for  trades  and  quotes  on  the 
U.S. equity market. However, the TAQ data do not include flags to 
identify short sales, presenting a significant limitation for understand-
ing short selling activities at the intraday level. To address this gap, we 
obtain proprietary short sale information at the transaction level from 
each of the three largest exchange groups in the U.S., viz. the NYSE, 
Nasdaq, and Cboe (which acquired BATS Global Markets in 2017). By 
the end of our sample period, the eleven exchanges comprising the 
NYSE, Nasdaq, and Cboe collectively accounted for approximately 97% 
of the exchange trading volume in U.S. equity markets.2 The short 
selling  data  include  the  ticker  symbol,  exchange  code,  trade  price, 
trade size, sales conditions (i.e., exempt or non-exempt from the price 
test of SEC’s Regulation SHO, Rule 201), date, and a timestamp at 
second or millisecond level. We use these variables to match every 
single short sale transaction with the corresponding trade in the TAQ 
data. Following Hu et al. (2025), we include non-exempt short sales 
during regular market hours (9:30 am to 4:00 pm).3 These data allow 
us to identify transactions of opening short sale trades, but we do not 
observe when short positions are reversed or closed. Our dataset covers 
a sample period from 2014 through 2018.4

2.2. Identification of liquidity-supplying and liquidity-demanding short sales

The objective of our paper is to examine the dynamics and the 
informational content of two distinct types of short sales: liquidity-
supplying short sales and liquidity-demanding short sales. To this end, 
we first match each short sale transaction to the corresponding ob-
servation in the TAQ data, using all transaction details observable in 
both datasets. In particular, we employ the WRDS Consolidated Trades 
(WCT) database, which contains not only all equity trades but also the 
best available national bid and offer (NBBO) prices at the time of each 
transaction.7 We employ a three-step matching procedure to link every 
short sale transaction to a TAQ trade and to determine the prevailing 
NBBO quote, as detailed in Appendix  B.

The classification of each short sale transaction relies on inferring 
the  trade  direction  by  comparing  the  trade  price  of  the  short  sale 
to the NBBO quoted prices at the intraday level. We apply the Lee 
and Ready (1991) algorithm, which integrates both the quote and the 
tick tests to determine the direction for each trade at the prevailing 
midpoint quote. Short sale transactions below the prevailing midpoint 
quote are categorized as short seller-initiated, while those above it are 
classified as buyer-initiated. For the remaining short trades executed 
at the midquote, we apply the tick test and classify a short trade as 
seller-initiated (buyer-initiated) if the trade price is below (above) the 
preceding trade price. Using INET order data and a subsample of Nas-
daq stocks, Chakrabarty et al. (2012) compare the true percentage of

2.3. Summary statistics

Panel A of Table  1 presents daily stock-level summary statistics of 
the short sale data and other stock characteristics. We present the time-

On average, short selling represents 45.5% of total trading volume 
in our sample. This magnitude of shorting flow is significantly larger 
compared to that in earlier samples (see, e.g., Diether et al., 2009) but 
comparable to estimates reported in recent studies (see, e.g., Reed et al., 
2020 and Wang et al., 2020). After we apply the trade classification al-
gorithm to our short sale data, we find that liquidity-supplying (21.4%) 
and liquidity-demanding (22.5%) shorts contribute almost equally to 
the overall trading volume.8 Studies that use more limited and less 
recent data report somewhat larger shares of liquidity-supplying short 
sales (see, e.g., Chakrabarty et al., 2012 and Comerton-Forde et al., 
2016). For instance, for a sample of 200 Nasdaq-listed stocks during 
two months in 2005, Chakrabarty et al. (2012) find that 43.3% of 
short sales are seller-initiated or liquidity-demanding. This difference 
decreases for a sample in 2008, in which the share of short sales exe-
cuted at the ask price (39.8%) is similar to that executed at the bid price 
(35.9%) (Comerton-Forde et al., 2016). Overall, recent data indicate 
that short sales have gained significant importance over the years, with 
both buyer- and seller-initiated shorts being equally important.

fees  𝑆ℎ𝑜𝑟𝑡𝑖𝑛𝑔𝐹 𝑒𝑒.  This  positive  relationship  aligns  with  the  notion 
that utilization and shorting fees are equilibrium outcome variables 
that increase with heightened shorting demand. The correlations with 
market capitalization (𝑆𝑖𝑧𝑒) suggest that short sellers are more likely 
to demand rather than supply liquidity for bigger stocks.

3. Empirical results

There  is  ample  evidence  that  short  selling  activity  predicts  fu-
ture negative returns (see, e.g., Asquith et al., 2005; Boehmer et al., 
2008; Boehmer and Wu, 2013; Diether et al., 2009; and Engelberg 
et al., 2012). The literature implicitly treats informed short sales as 
liquidity-demanding  (see,  e.g.,  Comerton-Forde  et  al.,  2016).  Intu-
itively, liquidity-demanding short sales are likely to arise from informed 
traders, potentially those with short-lived information. On the other 
hand, while liquidity-supplying short sales would arise from market 
makers providing liquidity, it is also possible that these short sales 
come from other informed traders with longer-lived information and no 
need for immediacy (Boehmer et al., 2020). The previous literature’s 
aggregation of these two categories masks the potentially heteroge-
neous  impact  of  these  short  sales  on  predictability  of  returns.  Our

3.1. Portfolio sorts

3.2. Cross-sectional regressions

We start by examining the predictive ability of daily 𝐿𝑆𝑆 and 𝐿𝐷𝑆
by following the methodology of Boehmer et al. (2008). Specifically, 
we form quintile portfolios each day 𝑡 − 1 based on that day’s short 
volume ratio. We hold these portfolios for 21 days from day 𝑡 through 
day 𝑡 + 20. We use the overlapping portfolio approach of Jegadeesh and 
Titman (1993) to calculate overlapping period returns. The portfolios 
are value-weighted based on the market capitalization at the end of 
day 𝑡 − 1. We calculate alphas from CAPM and a six-factor model that 
includes the five factors of Fama and French (2015) and the momentum 
factor.

We report cumulative six-factor alphas for spread portfolios formed 
based on sorts of 𝐿𝑆𝑆 or 𝐿𝐷𝑆 for future horizons varying from 1 to 
21 days in Fig.  2. Consistent with prior literature (see, e.g., Boehmer 
et al., 2008 and Engelberg et al., 2012), we find that both 𝐿𝑆𝑆 and 
𝐿𝐷𝑆 significantly and negatively predict next day 𝑡 return, with daily 
six-factor alphas of around 0.10%. Increasing the holding period, we 
find that the predictive power of 𝐿𝐷𝑆 weakens, with no statistically 
significant predictability after seven days. In contrast, we find that 𝐿𝑆𝑆
is a strong negative predictor of future equity returns across all holding 
periods ranging from 1 to 21 days. For a 21-day holding period, the 
spread portfolio based on 𝐿𝑆𝑆 has a cumulative six-factor alpha of 
almost 0.40%. Thus, this figure documents a striking empirical pat-
tern: liquidity-supplying shorts negatively predict future equity returns, 
whereas liquidity-demanding shorts do not, at least for horizons of 
longer than a week.

portfolio for a horizon of 21 days in Table  2. Panels A, B, and C sort

Overall, while we do find that liquidity-demanding short sales are 
associated with short-lived price impacts (as in Comerton-Forde et al., 
2016), we show that liquidity-supplying short sales also predict neg-
ative returns, and do so more strongly and persistently. This finding 
challenges traditional market microstructure models, which associate 
informed  trading  with  liquidity-demanding  orders  (e.g.,  Easley  and 
O’Hara, 1987; Glosten, 1994; and Glosten and Milgrom, 1985).

In  contrast,  our  findings  align  with  a  more  recent,  growing  lit-
erature in which informed traders may optimally choose to provide 
liquidity by submitting limit orders. Models such as Kaniel and Liu 

(2006) and Riccò et al. (2024) are particularly relevant in our context. 
Both frameworks allow informed traders to submit limit orders when 
their private information is long-lived, and show that such liquidity-
supplying trades can be not only informative, but in some cases more 
informative than liquidity-demanding trades. Our evidence that 𝐿𝑆𝑆
predict returns more strongly and over longer horizons is consistent 
with these predictions.11

4. What explains the return predictability?

The evidence reported in Section 3 indicates that liquidity-supplying 
short sales negatively predict long-term equity returns. Moreover, we 
do not find such predictive power for liquidity-demanding short sales. 
In this section we discuss and test three hypotheses to explain our 
results.

16 𝐿𝑆𝐿𝑆 is defined as the ratio of buyer-initiated long sales to total trading 
volume, while 𝐿𝐷𝐿𝑆 is the ratio of seller-initiated long sales to total trading 
volume.
17 The sum of the four components (𝐿𝑆𝑆, 𝐿𝐷𝑆, 𝐿𝑆𝐿𝑆, and 𝐿𝐷𝐿𝑆) is by

and 𝐿𝐷𝐿𝑆 are added separately in two different specifications. Similarly, we 
omit the order imbalance (𝑂𝐼𝑀𝐵) variable from these specifications.

The first hypothesis builds on previous work and the general per-
ception that short sellers are informed (see, e.g., Asquith et al., 2005;
Boehmer et al., 2008; Christophe et al., 2004; Christophe et al., 2010; De-
sai et al., 2006; Diether et al., 2009; Figlewski, 1981; and Senchack 
and Starks, 1993). Some informed traders may find it optimal under 
certain  conditions  to  submit  limit  orders  and  supply  liquidity  (see, 
e.g., Chakravarty and Holden, 1995; Garriott and Riordan, 2024; O’Hara, 
2015; and Seppi and Kumar, 1994). They may prefer limit orders to 
trade based on fundamental news, especially if these are long-lived 
(Collin-Dufresne and Fos, 2015; Kaniel and Liu, 2006; and Riccò et al., 
2024). As short sellers have become more profitable trading on long-
term  information  that  is  gradually  incorporated  into  prices  (Wang 
et al., 2020), the return predictability based on 𝐿𝑆𝑆 is consistent with 
informed trading.

In  addition  to  the  informed  trading  hypothesis,  two  alternative 
mechanisms could also explain the reported return predictability: mar-
ket making and opportunistic risk-bearing. Diether et al. (2009) show 
that short selling activity correlates positively with contemporaneous 
returns. Comerton-Forde et al. (2016) find that this contrarian trading 
at daily horizon is driven by buyer-initiated short sales. These findings 
are consistent with short sellers stepping in to serve as market makers. 
When stock prices rise following a sequence of buy orders, market 
makers reduce their long positions and may even shift to short positions 
to continue providing liquidity. This surge in selling activity caters 
to buyers willing to pay for immediacy. As buying pressure subsides, 
stock prices are likely to revert and compensate short sellers for their 
market-making activity.

The third hypothesis to explain the results is that short sellers are 
opportunistic risk-bearers (Diether et al., 2009). For stocks and time pe-
riods with higher price uncertainty, short sellers increase their activity 
to opportunistically supply liquidity. Once the heightened uncertainty 
is resolved, the stock price is expected to revert to its long-term level, 
thus providing a return to the short seller for bearing this risk. In the 
following subsections, we run a number of empirical exercises to shed 
light on which of the three non-mutually exclusive hypotheses most 
likely applies to liquidity-supplying short sales.

4.2. Liquidity provision and news

In this section, we further explore the predictive power of 𝐿𝑆𝑆 in 
the cross-section of stock returns. In particular, we ask whether the 
return predictability shown in Section 3 arises from the informational 
advantage and skill of liquidity-supplying short sellers in identifying 
mispriced stocks. To address this question, we employ a framework 
akin to that of Engelberg et al. (2012). The rationale behind our test is 
as follows: if the predictive ability of liquidity-supplying short sales is 
indeed due to an informational advantage, characterized by trading in 
overpriced stocks and avoiding underpriced ones, then this mispricing 

should be corrected upon the release of news. In other words, 𝐿𝑆𝑆
should predict returns particularly on days with news releases.18

To test this hypothesis, we compile a sample of news events using 
the RavenPack Analytics (RPA) database and study the short selling 
patterns around public news releases. The RPA database contains a 
comprehensive sample of firm specific news stories from thousands of 
news sources, such as Dow Jones Newswires, The Wall Street Journal, 
Barron’s, and other leading financial websites. To identify important 
news events specific to a given firm, we require both the Relevance and
Event Relevance measure to be above 70 (out of 100).19 Moreover, we 
consider only news events with an Event Similarity Day score above 90 
(up to 365 days), thereby requiring at least 90 days since a similar event 
was detected in the past. Following Reed et al. (2020), we remove (1) 
news stories relating to trading and market activity, such as ‘‘technical-
analysis’’, ‘‘stock-prices’’, and ‘‘insider-trading"; and (2) news in the 
categories ‘‘revenues’’ and ‘‘investor-relations’’, so as to avoid a large 
overlap  with  earnings  news  announcements  days.  Furthermore,  we 
augment the news sample with earnings announcement dates from the 
Institutional Brokers’ Estimate System (IBES) if they are not already 
included in the RPA database. We define a stock-day news indicator 
variable (𝑁𝑒𝑤𝑠) as one if a news event occurred on that day for a 
particular firm and zero otherwise. Following Engelberg et al. (2012), 
if a news story is released after 4:00 pm, then we define the following 
trading day as the event day.

We then extend the Fama and MacBeth (1973) regressions of Sec-
tion 3.2 by including the news indicator variable and interaction terms 
with our two short selling measures, 𝐿𝑆𝑆 and 𝐿𝐷𝑆. We report the 
results in Table  7. As a starting point, column (1) simply replicates the

the 𝑁𝑒𝑤𝑠 indicator variable is small and insignificant, the coefficient

significantly less informal market making by short sellers.21

tional advantage held by short sellers over other market participants. 
The return predictability is particularly pronounced (1) on days when 
fundamental information is disclosed to the market and (2) when we 
exclude the market-making and risk-bearing components from our short 
selling measure.

4.4. Short sellers and cross-sectional return anomalies

Our results so far indicate that the predictive power of short sales 
for the cross-section of equity returns stems from informed liquidity 
provision by short sellers. Moreover, the graphical representation of the 
return predictability for varying holding periods in Fig.  2 suggests that 
these short sales trade on information that is slowly incorporated into 
stock prices.

In more recent and dynamic market microstructure theories (see, 
e.g. Kaniel and Liu, 2006 and Riccò et al., 2024) informed investors 
may strategically supply liquidity by submitting limit orders, particu-
larly when their information pertains to long-horizon mispricing and 

Journal of Financial Economics 172 (2025) 104155 

when execution risk is low. In these settings, patient informed traders 
optimize  execution  by  waiting  for  liquidity  takers  to  arrive  while 
avoiding the immediate costs of demanding liquidity.

To further shed light on the nature of the information that liquidity-
supplying  short  sellers  exploit,  we  study  whether  their  short  sales 
align with well-known cross-sectional equity anomalies. These anomaly 
variables,  derived  from  accounting,  price,  and/or  trading  data,  are 
known  to  predict  returns  over  extended  periods  —  often  spanning 
months to years — and are thought to reflect slow-moving information 
about  firm  fundamentals  (see,  e.g.  Arnott  et  al.,  2023  and  Ehsani 
and Linnainmaa, 2022). If liquidity-supplying short sellers are indeed 
informed investors who trade based on long-term mispricing signals, we 
would expect them to disproportionately sell stocks in the short legs of 
these anomalies and avoid stocks in the long legs.

To this end, we examine 10 prominent anomaly variables that the 
previous literature has found to be related to the cross-section of stock 
returns.22 If liquidity-supplying short sellers are informed investors who 
understand the return predictability based on these anomalies, then 
we expect these short sellers to sell stocks in the short leg of these 
anomalies and to avoid stocks in the long leg.

We  follow  the  procedure  of  Wang  et  al.  (2020)  in  testing  this 
hypothesis. Each anomaly variable is signed, based on prior literature, 
such that the future mean returns increase in the signed characteristic. 
We sort all stocks in our sample into deciles based on each signed 
anomaly  variable.  We  follow  previous  literature  in  forming  the  re-
balancing and holding frequencies. For anomalies constructed using 
annual Compustat data, we form portfolios at the end of each June 
in  year  𝑦  by  using  accounting  data  from  the  fiscal  year  ending  in 
calendar year 𝑦 − 1. Furthermore, we hold the portfolio from July of 
year 𝑦 through June of year 𝑦 + 1. For anomalies constructed using 
quarterly Compustat data (such as those using ROE), we form portfolios 
at the end of quarter 𝑞 using data from the fiscal quarter ending in 
calendar quarter 𝑞 −1 and hold the portfolio over calendar quarter 𝑞 +1. 
Lastly, for anomalies constructed using monthly CRSP data, we form 
portfolios every month 𝑚 and hold the portfolio for the next month 
𝑚 + 1. Using this portfolio formation procedure, for each stock-month 
and for each anomaly, we calculate the decile rank for that stock based 
on its corresponding portfolio assignment. Next, for each day 𝑡, we sort 
stocks into quintiles based on either 𝐿𝑆𝑆 or 𝐿𝐷𝑆. For each shorting 
quintile and for each anomaly, we subsequently compute the average 
anomaly decile rank at the end of the previous month. Since decile 
10 (1) of each anomaly represents the long (short) leg, we expect the 
average rank of the high shorting quintile to be significantly lower than 
the average rank of the low shorting quintile.

anomaly rank of 𝐿𝑆𝑆 quintile 1 is higher than that of 𝐿𝑆𝑆 quintile 5

Issues,  the  average  rank  of  quintiles  1  and  5  of  𝐿𝑆𝑆  portfolios  is 
7.07 and 6.11, respectively. This means that stocks for which liquidity-
supplying short sales are high (quintile 5) are more likely to correspond 
to stocks that have low Net Stock Issues and thus are overpriced. The 
difference in these ranks is −0.96 with a 𝑡-statistic of −11.67 calculated 
using Newey and West (1987) errors with 60 lags. In fact, the difference 
in average anomaly ranks between 𝐿𝑆𝑆 quintiles 5 and 1 is negative 
and statistically significant across all anomalies. The average difference 
across all anomalies is −0.72 (𝑡-statistic = −23.65).

4.5. Short sellers and venue choice

So far, our analysis aggregates short sales across trading venues into 
daily stock-level measures of liquidity-supplying (𝐿𝑆𝑆) and liquidity-
demanding (𝐿𝐷𝑆) short volume. This aggregation obscures the fact 
that trading is spread across multiple trading venues, and these trading 
venues are heterogeneous in ways that could be related to the patterns 
of liquidity and informativeness we observe in the results above.23 In 

particular, our previous results suggest that liquidity-supplying short 
sellers trade patiently on long-lived information and submit limit orders 
(see, e.g., Kaniel and Liu, 2006 and Riccò et al., 2024). However, not all 
limit orders are equally passive: depending on the venue, traders may 
submit limit orders more aggressively to expedite execution. Therefore, 
to more directly examine whether liquidity-supplying shorts are truly 
patient, as our findings imply, we exploit differences across trading 
venues, where distinct fee structures create incentives for patient versus 
aggressive liquidity provision.

One  key  difference  between  trading  venues  is  their  fee  model, 
which directly affects trading behavior and liquidity provision. The 
eleven exchange venues that we cover in our sample have mainly op-
erated under two primary fee structures during our sample period: the 
maker-taker fee model and the inverted (taker-marker) fee model. In 
maker-taker venues, liquidity providers who submit limit orders receive 
a  rebate,  thereby  reducing  the  effective  cost  of  liquidity  provision, 
while liquidity takers incur a fee. In contrast, inverted venues impose 
a  fee  on  liquidity  providers  and  pay  a  rebate  to  liquidity  takers.24 
These structural differences create important strategic considerations 
for informed traders. In particular, while submitting limit orders is

5. Conclusion

Our  research  provides  significant  insights  into  the  role  of  short 
sellers in financial markets, particularly in relation to their impact on 
market liquidity and their informativeness about trades. By distinguish-
ing between liquidity-supplying and liquidity-demanding short sales, 
we  challenge  the  conventional  wisdom  that  only  those  demanding 
liquidity are informed. We show that liquidity-supplying short sellers 
are, in fact, better at predicting future stock returns, particularly over 
longer holding periods. Our study aligns with more recent theoretical 
work that posits a dual role for informed traders, one that includes 
liquidity provision as a strategy to capitalize on long-lived information.

Regulators have long faced a difficult challenge in regulating short 
sellers. Regulators must balance the benefits of short-selling—such as 
enhanced liquidity and price discovery—with concerns about market 
stability  and  potential  price  distortions.  While  restrictions  such  as 
short-sale bans or price tests are often introduced to prevent exces-
sive  downward  pressure,  empirical  evidence  suggests  that  they  can 
also reduce market efficiency and harm liquidity. These trade-offs, as 
discussed in Edwards et al. (2024), are likely to persist well into the 
future.

The fact that the same short sellers both supply liquidity and im-
prove market efficiency adds significantly to the challenge. Our paper 
shows that short sellers who trade in the direction of their information, 
which presumably affects prices, are, more often than not, doing so 
through liquidity-providing trades. In other words, our results show 
that the liquidity provision aspect of short selling is not separable from 
the price discovery aspect of short selling, at least along the liquidity 
supply/demand dimension.

Regulations relying on microstructure price mechanisms, including 
price  tests  such  as  the  uptick  rule,  assume  informed  short  sellers 
are liquidity demanders. It is through the mechanism of identifying 
liquidity-demanding short sales that these regulations work to achieve 
their aim of reducing the price impact of short sales (see Jain, Jain, 
McInish, and McKenzie, 2013). Since the results in our paper indi-
cate that liquidity-supplying short sales can be more informed than 
liquidity-demanding short sales, the price mechanisms central to these 
regulations  might  be  ill-suited  to  reduce  the  price  impact  of  short 
selling.

Given  our  results,  future  regulation  might  prioritize  disclosure-
based regulations over price tests. For example, new regulation might 
take the form of the US SEC’s recent expansion of short-sale disclosure, 
which amends the consolidated audit trail (CAT) to include an addi-
tional short sale related field, or they might take the form of the UK’s 
recent proposal to publicly disclose aggregated short positions.26 These 
examples of disclosure-based regulations do not rest on the assumption 
that informed short sellers are always liquidity-demanding short sellers.

Appendix B. Matching short sale transactions

We match our intraday short sale data with all trades and quotes 
from the same period using the TAQ data. Specifically, as in Barber 
et al. (2024), we use the WRDS Consolidated Trades (WCT) files, which 
provides us with the National Best Bid and Offer (NBBO) at the time 
of each trade. The WCT files include the NBBO quotes following the 
convention established by Holden and Jacobsen (2014). We exclude 
trades associated with locked or crossed bid–ask spreads from the WCT 
files, also in line with (Holden and Jacobsen, 2014). For all remaining 
trades in the WCT files, we classify trades following the (Lee and Ready, 
1991) (henceforth LR) method, using trade prices and the prevailing 
NBBO.

We next match each short sale trade across all U.S. exchanges to 
its corresponding trade in the WCT universe. Our matching procedure 
allows us to classify each short sale as either buyer- or seller-initiated 
based on the NBBO and the LR methodology. Our procedure consists 
of three steps, detailed below.

Step 1: Exact Matching

In the first step, we perform an exact match between short sales and 
WCT trades based on the following grouping variables: ticker symbol, 
exchange code, trade price, trade size, and date/time (at the second 
or tenth-of-a-second level, depending on the provided time precision). 
We match trades that have identical values for these variables in both 
datasets. Table  B.1 provides an example using a hypothetical stock, 
’XYZ,’ traded on exchange ‘ABC’ on a given trading day. In this case, 
three trades in the short sales file match exactly with three trades in the 
WCT file at 10:28:48 across all above-mentioned grouping variables. 
Consequently, these three trades are joined, and we assign the NBBO 
and LR trade classification from the WCT file to the corresponding short 
sales.

Step 2: Approximate matching

In the second step, we address cases where the number of trades 
in the short sales file is fewer than that in the WCT file with identical 
characteristics. For these instances, we match short sale trades to the 
first few corresponding WCT trades.

Table   B.2  illustrates  this  scenario.  The  short  sales  file  contains 
two trades at 10:28:48, while the WCT file contains three trades at 
the same time. In this case, the two short sales are matched to the 
first two WCT trades. Although we cannot definitively identify which 
WCT trades are short sales, the NBBO information for these trades is 
likely consistent within this grouping, as per the NBBO construction 
methodology (Holden and Jacobsen, 2014).

Step 3: Residual matching

After applying steps 1 and 2, a very small subset of short sales 
remains  unmatched.  For  these  trades,  we  use  a  residual  matching 
approach. Specifically, if no WCT trade has the exact timestamp as a 
short sale trade, we match the short sale to the prevailing NBBO quote 
in the WCT file, based on the timestamp.

Table  B.3 provides an example. A short sale occurs at 10:28:48, but 
no corresponding WCT trade is recorded at that time. In this case, we 
use the NBBO and LR classification from the most recent WCT NBBO 
quote (10:28:47).

Overall, this procedure allows us to merge quoted data into our 
short sales data and subsequently classify short sales into liquidity-
supplying and liquidity-demanding trades. We are able to match 97.5% 
of  the  short  sales  to  trades,  comparable  to  the  98.2%  match  rate 
reported by Comerton-Forde et al. (2016). As in their study, we dis-
card unmatched observations, which represent a small fraction of the 
sample.

Appendix C. Price impact of liquidity-demanding and liquidity-
supplying (short) sales

In this appendix, we complement our main findings with an in-
traday  analysis  in  the  spirit  of  Comerton-Forde  et  al.  (2016).  The 
purpose of this exercise is to examine whether the price impact patterns 
of short sales in our 2014–2018 sample resemble those documented 
by Comerton-Forde et al. (2016) for 2008. Although our main contri-
bution focuses on longer-term return predictability, this analysis helps 
assess whether potential discrepancies with earlier work are due to 
differences in return horizons or evolving short-seller behavior over 
time.

We follow the procedure outlined in Section 2.2 and Appendix  B 
to match each short sale transaction to its corresponding trade in the 
TAQ database using all available transaction details. Specifically, we 
employ the WCT database, which contains all equity trades alongside 
the best available NBBO quotes at the time of each trade. Using the Lee 
and Ready (1991) algorithm, we infer trade direction not only for all 
short sales but also for long sales across U.S. exchanges. This allows 
us to classify all trades into four distinct order flow categories: buyer-
initiated short sales (liquidity-supplying short sales, 𝑙𝑠𝑠), seller-initiated 
short sales (liquidity-demanding short sales, 𝑙𝑑𝑠), buyer-initiated long 
sales (liquidity-supplying long sales, 𝑙𝑠𝑙𝑠), and seller-initiated long sales 
(liquidity-demanding long sales, 𝑙𝑑𝑙𝑠).27 Our sample comprises over 17 
billion trades (compared to about 273 million trades in Comerton-Forde 
et al. (2016).).

Following Comerton-Forde et al. (2016, Table 6), we estimate each 
order type’s average price impact. More specifically, for a given stock 
𝑖  and  post-trade  horizon  𝑘,  we  regress  cumulative  market-adjusted

trades:

fixed effects, Model 2 with stock fixed effects, and Model 3 with five-

Taken together, these results show that both liquidity-demanding 
and liquidity-supplying short sellers in our sample exhibit similar price 
impact patterns in the 24-h post-trade period. At the same time, the 
negative price drifts after the initial five-minute price impact are similar 
for the two types of short sales in our sample. This similarity is consis-
tent with the one-day ahead return predictability based on 𝐿𝑆𝑆 and 
𝐿𝐷𝑆 depicted in Fig.  2. Thus, the apparent contrast between our long-
horizon findings and the intraday findings in Comerton-Forde et al. 
(2016) is likely driven by the return horizon difference that is one 
of the key ways in which our paper’s primary focus differs from that 
of Comerton-Forde et al. (2016).
