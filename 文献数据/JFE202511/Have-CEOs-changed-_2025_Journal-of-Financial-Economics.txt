1. Introduction

Using more than 4900 personality assessments, we study changes in the characteristics of CEOs and top exec-
utives since 2001. The same four factors explain roughly half of the variation in executive characteristics in this 
larger  sample  of  assessments  as  in  Kaplan  and  Sorensen  (2021).  In  later  years,  CEO  candidates  have  shown 
declining  general  ability,  are  increasingly  execution-oriented,  less  interpersonal,  less  charismatic,  and  less 
creative-strategic,  and  many  of  these  differences  persist  for  hired  CEOs.  We  find  no  evidence  of  increasing 
prevalence  or  importance  of  interpersonal  and  softer  skills.  Executives  assessed  for  the  same  company  have 
positively  correlated  abilities,  suggesting  that  high-ability  executives  complement  each  other.  Finally,  we 
consider corporate objectives and CEO characteristics.

There is a sense in the media and among some academics that CEOs 
and top executives today should focus more on softer and interpersonal 
skills.  Deming  (2017) argues  that  the  demand  for  social  skills  has 
increased  because  jobs  increasingly  involve  team  production  and 
trading  of  tasks  among  workers.  Fuller  et  al.  (2021) investigate  de-
scriptions of executive positions as part of search processes and find an 
increasing demand for social skills for CEOs, where social skills include 
motivating,  persuading,  listening  to  and  empathizing  with  others.  In 
contrast,  Pfeffer  (2015,  2022)  argues  that  little  has  changed  funda-
mentally  and  that  successful  leaders  always  have  “built  their  power 
bases, embraced ambiguity, eschewed popularity contests, adapted and 
mastered the science of influence.”

In this paper, we study trends in the characteristics and objectives of 
CEOs and other executives. We use a sample of over 4900 assessments of 
C-level executives from 2000 through 2019 to study trends in charac-
teristics of candidates assessed for and hired into CEO and other exec-
utive positions. The sample substantially extends the sample of about 
2600 assessments used by Kaplan and Sorensen (2021) and allows us to 

consider  changes  and  trends  in  managerial  characteristics  and  hiring 
decisions over this period.

The  assessments  are  performed  by  ghSMART  as  part  of  corporate 
hiring  or  retention  processes.  Each  assessment  contains  a  detailed 
description  of  the  candidate’s  background  and  personality,  including 
ratings  for  about  thirty  specific  characteristics.  The  candidates  are 
considered  for  a  range  of  positions  in  the  hiring  companies,  which 
include  both  public  and  private  companies.  A  typical  assessment  is 
requested by a private equity investor as part of its due diligence when 
evaluating a potential investment in a company. More than half of the 
CEOs in the sample are assessed as part of such a due diligence process.
As in Kaplan and Sorensen (2021), we use factor analysis to sum-
marize the main variation in the characteristics (see Fabrigar et al. 1999; 
Borghans et al., 2008; and Adams et al., 2018). The same four factors – 
which we interpret as (1) overall ability, (2) execution vs. interpersonal, 
(3)  charisma  vs.  analytical,  and  (4)  creative  /  strategic  vs. 
detail-oriented – explain roughly half the total variation in the charac-
teristics for this larger sample of executive assessments.

We focus on changes over time. In the later part of the sample, the 
average  CEO  candidate  has  lower  overall  ability,  is  more  execution-

and more detail-oriented and less creative-strategic than earlier. All of 
these  changes  are  statistically  significant.  CEO  candidates  who  are 
actually hired to become CEOs are also more analytical and less char-
ismatic as well as more detail oriented and less creative / strategic in the 
later period. At the same time, they are similar in overall ability and 
execution  versus  interpersonal  orientation  compared  to  CEOs  hired 
earlier.

In the later period, therefore, we find no evidence of an increase in 
the importance or prevalence of interpersonal and “softer” skills. These 
skills remain effectively unchanged in the subset of hired CEOs and they 
have weakened in the larger pool of assessed CEO candidates. Overall, 
our evidence is more consistent with the view of Pfeffer (2015, 2022) 
that little has changed fundamentally.

The dataset also allows us to study how the characteristics of CEOs 
are related to the characteristics of other top executives considered by 
the  same  company.  For  example,  do  high-ability  CEOs  attract  other 
higher  ability  executives?  The  answer  appears  to  be  yes.  Both  in  the 
earlier and later periods, we find a strong positive correlation between 
the abilities of CEOs and other executives assessed for the same hiring 
company,  suggesting  that  higher-ability  executives  complement  each 
other.  We  do  not  find  consistent  relations  between  other  factors  for 
executives at the same company.

Finally, it is possible that a company’s situation affects its demand 
for a certain type of CEO and other executives. For example, one CEO 
may be better at a business in crisis that needs to fundamentally reshape 
its organization, while another may be better at a business that wants to 
streamline  its  ongoing  operations.  Most  of  the  assessments  include  a 
scorecard that lists the main challenges that the hired executive would 
be  facing.  In  general,  the  scorecards  indicate  increasing  demand  for 
CEOs  with  skills  in  organic  growth,  operations,  and  strategic  issues. 
While still prevalent, there is decreasing demand for CEOs with skills in 
staffing. Moreover, we find only a modest relation between the content 
of the scorecard and the characteristics of the assessed and hired CEO 
candidates.

Our  study  complements  Deming  (2017) who  documents  the 
increasing  importance  of  social  skills  in  the  general  US  economy, 
arguing that this is consistent with the increasing importance of workers 
“trading tasks” to exploit comparative advantage. Deming’s analysis is 
based on two questions in the NLSY79 and NLSY97 surveys of 14- to 
22-year-olds who report their self-assessed sociability both at the time of 
the survey and previously at age 6. While Deming (2017) looks at the 
general population, our sample focuses on senior executives, especially 
CEO candidates. Our data also contain about 30 specific characteristics 
that  are  coded  after  a  comprehensive  assessment  of  the  candidate, 
allowing  a  more nuanced  analysis  of  the  candidate’s social  skills and 
other personality traits. Unlike Deming (2017), we do not find any in-
crease in the prevalence or importance of interpersonal skills in our data.
Our analysis also complements Fuller et al. (2021), who study ex-
ecutive search descriptions and find an increasing demand for CEO so-
cial skills, where social skills include motivating, persuading, listening 
to, and empathizing with others. A main difference is that Fuller et al. 
(2021) study  the  stated  demand  for  certain  corporate  executives  as 
inferred from the hiring company’s description of an ideal candidate in 
the search description. In contrast, our data and analysis focus on the 
supply of potential CEO candidates, as inferred from the characteristics 
of the candidates that are actually assessed and considered for a given 
position.  The  different  empirical  results  are  therefore  consistent  with 
diverging trends in the supply and demand for corporate executives.

As  a  methodological  difference,  Fuller  et  al.  (2021) use  cluster 
analysis, whereas our main analysis uses factor analysis. In Appendix B, 
we find that using a similar cluster analysis does not change our main 
results.  Therefore, the  different  empirical results  are  unlikely  to  arise 
from this methodological difference. That said, it is difficult to precisely 
compare the clusters in Fuller et al. (2021) to our factors because the 
studies  use  fundamentally  different  data  with  inherently  different 

classifications and codings of executive personalities and characteristics.
Two additional studies are closely related. Frydman (2019) argues 
that there has been a long-term increase in the relative importance of 
general managerial skills accompanied by a decline in the importance of 
firm-specific executive skills. In contrast, Cziraki and Jenter (2022) find 
that publicly-traded companies hire CEOs from a small pool of familiar 
candidates  and  argue  that  this  is  evidence  of  the  importance  of 
firm-specific  skills  as  well  as  asymmetric  learning  for  the  hiring  de-
cisions of these companies.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 1 describes our data. Section 2
reports trends in CEO characteristics. Section 3 reports the relation of 
characteristics of executives considered by the same company. Section 4
looks at the difference between executives who are hired and not hired 
in more detail. Section 5 describes corporate objectives, their relation to 
CEO characteristics, and their trends over time. Section 6 concludes.

2. Data

We use data from ghSMART, a consulting firm that provides detailed 
assessments  of  top  executive  candidates,  typically  in  connection  with 
hiring  and  retention  decisions.  Kaplan  et  al.  (2012),  Kaplan  and  Sor-
ensen (2021) and Kaplan et al. (2021) use earlier versions of this dataset 
with fewer observations and a shorter sample period. In contrast to the 
earlier  studies,  our  twenty-year  sample  period  allows  us  to  consider 
changes  and  trends  in  executive  characteristics.  Botelho  and  Powell 
(2018), building on the results in Kaplan et al. (2012) and preliminary 
results  from  this  study,  also  use  the  ghSMART  data  to  study  the  de-
terminants of CEO success.

Assessments are typically done as part of the due diligence by private 
equity firms evaluating investments in companies and as part of exec-
utive search processes by public company boards. Consequently, most 
assessments are done ex-ante, before the investment, retention, or hiring 
decision is made. Moreover, ghSMART only assesses candidates. It does 
not source or recommend individual candidates and, therefore, has no 
vested interest in whether a particular candidate is hired or a particular 
investment is made.

As shown in Panels A and B of Table 1, our data contain 4939 can-
didates assessed for positions in 1394 companies during the period from 
2000  to  2019  with  a  fairly  consistent  annual  number  of  assessments 
since  2002.  Our  data  is  a  random  subsample  of  all  the  assessments 
performed by ghSMART over this period with the caveat that candidates 
considered for publicly-traded companies are underweighted in the later 
part of the sample due to confidentiality concerns. However, we observe 
whether hiring companies are publicly traded, and we control for this 
ownership form in most of our analysis.

The most common position is CEO (1299 candidates), followed by 
CFO (602), VP (463, not reported), and COO (274). More than half of the 
assessed  CEO  candidates  are  considered  for  private  equity  portfolio 
companies. Venture capital and growth equity portfolio companies are 
the  next  two  largest  sources  of  candidates.  Unaffiliated  private  and 
public companies each provide about five percent of the companies and 
candidates.

Panel C of Table 1 shows that more than 10 % of all candidates, but 
fewer than 5 % of CEO candidates, are female. For comparison, He and 
Whited  (2023) report  that 6.6  % of  S&P  500  companies  have  female 
CEOs and analyze corporate demand for these female CEOs. The column 
with Other candidates shows candidates considered for positions other 
than CEO and CFO positions, and the Total column shows all candidates. 
Panel D shows that about half of the assessed CEO candidates are hired 
and that the hiring rate is lower for non-CEO candidates.

2.2. Reliability

An  important  concern  is  whether  candidates  can  manipulate  the 
assessment process by presenting themselves more favorably. In orga-
nizational and personal psychology, this is known as “faking.” ghSMART 
uses best practices from personnel psychology to mitigate this concern, 
including  using  external  interviewers  instead  of  self-assessments 
(Dohmen  and  Jagelka,  2024),  and  using  extensive  structured  in-
terviews instead of questionnaires (Ones et al., 2007). Assessments are 
expensive, yet ghSMART has seen its business grow substantially, indi-
cating that ghSMART’s customers find the assessments to be valuable 
and informative. Finally, the results in previous studies have found that 
the assessments are informative and with significant predictive power. 
For  example,  Kaplan and  Sorensen (2021) show  that candidates  with 
assessed  characteristics  that  are  more  typical  for  CEOs  are  also  more 
likely  to  later  become  CEOs  in  other  companies  and  through  hiring 
processes that do not involve ghSMART.

2.3. Interpretation of factors

The candidates’ grades across the specific characteristics are highly 
correlated,  and  we  use  factor  analysis  to  extract  the  low-dimensional 
variation in these grades.1 A factor is typically considered valid when 
its eigenvalue exceeds one, and we find four such factors.

Panel A of Table 2 shows the estimated factor loadings.2 Note that 
factors are orthogonal by construction, and we do not apply any rota-
tions to the factors.3 Combined, the four factors capture 49.6 % of the 
variation in the specific characteristics, as shown in Panel B of Table 2.
The four factors have natural interpretations that are consistent with 
prevailing theories of CEO types. The first factor loads positively on all 
specific characteristics, suggesting that the characteristics tend to move 
together. This is a common feature of personality studies, dating back to 
Spearman (1904). Hence, we interpret this first factor as measuring a 
candidate’s general ability in the spirit of Rosen (1981).

loads most negatively on aggressive, moves fast, proactive, and holds 
people  accountable.  Arguably,  this  second  factor,  therefore,  contrasts 
candidates with greater interpersonal skills – who have positive scores 
on the second factor –  with candidates with greater execution skills – 
who have negative scores. Bolton et al. (2013) model a tradeoff between 
resolute leaders (top-down information flow) and leaders who listen to 
and learn from their subordinates (bottom-up information flow) when 
aggregating  information  and  coordinating  actions  in  an  organization. 
Interestingly, this theoretical characterization appears closely related to 
the distinction captured by our second factor. Moreover, positive scores 
on the second factor also appear to be related to the social skills cluster 
in  Fuller  et  al.  (2021),  whereas  negative  scores  on  the  second  factor 
appear to be more closely related to the information skills and moni-
toring clusters.

The third factor has the most positive loadings on analysis, attention 
to detail, organization, and brainpower. It has the most negative load-
ings  on  enthusiasm,  persuasion,  and  aggressive.  Arguably,  the  third 
factor contrasts candidates with greater analytical personalities –  who 
have  positive  scores  on  this  factor  –  with  candidates  having  more 
charismatic personalities – who have negative scores. Hermalin (2023)
models charismatic versus analytical managers as being either “inspi-
rational” or “informative” when communicating, and he derives condi-
tions  where  rational  agents  respond to  charismatic leaders.  Our  third 
factor  seems  to  capture  a  related  distinction.  Enthusiasm  and  persua-
sion, negative contributors to the third factor, also appear to be related 
to the social skills cluster in Fuller et al. (2021).

The fourth factor loads most positively on strategic vision, creative, 

and  brainpower while loading most  negatively on attention to detail, 
holds  people  accountable,  and  organization.  The  fourth  factor  thus 
contrasts candidates with a more creative-strategic perspective –  who 
have positive scores – with candidates who are more detail-oriented – 
and have negative scores. This fourth factor is more marginal, however, 
and it explains just 3.9 % of the variation. It seems related to the in-
formation skills and human resources clusters in Fuller et al. (2021).

To  summarize,  the  factor  analysis  suggests  a  four-dimensional 
characterization  of  managerial  personalities.  The  first  dimension  is 
general ability, as theorized by Rosen (1981). The second dimension is 
how the manager aggregates information and coordinates action in the 
organization, either in a resolute, top-down manner or using an inter-
personal style, as described by Bolton et al. (2013). The third dimension 
is managerial communication style, either through charisma and inspi-
ration or by being analytical and informative, as analyzed by Hermalin 
(2023).  Finally,  the  fourth  dimension  contrasts  detail-oriented  with 
creative-strategic candidates. The ordering of the factors is estimated by 
the statistical factor analysis and reflects both the variation of the latent 
traits in the sample and how well these latent traits are identified by the 
specific characteristics. The ordering does not necessarily reflect which 
factors are more important for a particular outcome.

For each candidate, the factor analysis also estimates a score for each 
of the four factors. The factor score is an ordinal measure of the extent to 
which the candidate’s specific characteristics indicate the presence or 
absence  of  the  corresponding  latent  factor  or  underlying  trait.  We 
therefore  interpret  a  higher  score  on  factor  1  as  indicating  that  the 
candidate has more general ability. A positive score on factor 2 indicates 
that  the  candidate’s  specific  characteristics  indicate  a  more  interper-
sonal  and  less  execution-oriented  personality.  Conversely,  a  negative 
score on factor 2 indicates that the candidate is more execution oriented 
and less interpersonal. For the third factor, a positive score indicates that 
the candidate is more analytical and less charismatic. Finally, a positive 
score on the fourth factor indicates that the candidate is more creative- 
strategic and less detail-oriented (conversely for negative scores).4

Some  additional  comments  are  in  order  about  the  estimation  and 
interpretation of the factors. First, the naming and interpretation of the 
factors is somewhat subjective. To illustrate, using the second factor as 
an example, the empirical factor analysis finds that candidates tend to 
receive  similar  scores  (either  higher  or  lower)  on  the  characteristics 
treats people with respect, open to criticism, teamwork, and listening 
skill. Candidates who tend to score higher on these competencies are 
assigned a positive score on the second factor. Hence, when we denote 
candidates with a positive score on the second factor as being “inter-
personal”, the term “interpersonal” should be read as shorthand for the 
type of personality who receives high grades for treating people with 
respect,  being  open  to  criticism,  being  a  team  player,  and  with  good 
listening skills. Other terms might also describe this personality type, 
such as being “empathetic”  or “collaborative”. Conversely, candidates 
with higher grades on being aggressive, moving fast, being proactive, 
and  holding  people  accountable  are  assigned  negative  scores  on  the 
second factor, and we use the term “execution oriented” as shorthand for 
this personality type. Other terms might also be appropriate, such as, for 
example, being “resolute,” “assertive,” or “decisive.”

Second, the contrasts reflected in the factors are empirical estimates 
and are not imposed. For example, the contrast between “interpersonal” 
and “execution oriented” captured by the second factor is an empirical 
finding due to candidates being either strong on interpersonal charac-
teristics and weak on execution characteristics or, conversely, weak on 
interpersonal  characteristics  and  strong  on  execution  characteristics. 
Similarly, the third factor finds a contrast between the “charismatic” and

2.4. Scorecards

In addition to the information about the candidate’s personality and 
characteristics,  the  reports  also  contain  a  “scorecard”  section.  The 
scorecard is provided by the hiring company to help ghSMART identify 
the best candidate, and it lists the most important challenges the new 
executive  would  be  facing.  A  typical  scorecard  lists  five  to  ten  such 
challenges ordered by importance. To illustrate, a common entry for a 
CEO position is to grow revenues from X to Y within Z years. The entries 
often contain additional details and background, such as whether the 
growth  should  be  organic  or  through  acquisitions.  Other  examples  of 
common  scorecard  entries  are  to  formulate  a  strategic  plan,  manage 
operational costs, and improve efficiency. The content of the scorecard 
is communicated by the hiring company to ghSMART to be considered 

when evaluating the candidate.

We manually code and classify the scorecard entries into eight topics: 
Staffing, Organic Growth, Strategy, Operations, Relations, Exit, Acqui-
sitions and Costs. Staffing are entries that relate to identifying strong 
performers  and  replacing  weak  performers  as  well  as  leading  and 
building a stronger management team. Organic items focus on organic 
revenue growth, either through expanding operations or by increasing 
revenue per customer. Strategy includes entries such as developing and 
executing a multi-year strategic plan for the company. Operations refer 
to improving operational efficiency and productivity along with supply 
chain and inventory management. Relations are entries that describe a 
need for the CEO to work with private equity investors and the com-
pany’s board of directors. Exit are entries that identify a need for the 
CEO to facilitate an exit of the company for its private equity investors, 
either through a public listing or a sale to a corporate acquirer or other 
private equity firm. Acquisitions refer to the need to identify attractive 
acquisition targets and integrate them into the company. Finally, Costs 
are about reducing costs, typically using language such as closing plants 
or implementing layoffs.

among the following three entries, it is 2. If it is present among any of the 
remaining entries, it is 1. If it is not listed, the topic is coded with an 
importance score of 0 on this scorecard. The Top-3 indicator equals one

Panel A of Table 3 also indicates the statistical significance of two- 
sided t-tests. The first hypothesis (“A = 0″) tests that the factor score 
equals zero, the sample average. This hypothesis is strongly rejected for 
all factors, and CEO candidates are clearly different from the average 
candidate.

The  second  hypothesis  (“E  = L”)  is  that the  factor  scores  for  CEO 
candidates are the same in the early and late periods. The t-tests confirm 
that the trends in Fig. 1 are statistically significant, including the decline 
in the general ability of assessed CEO candidates (decreasing factor 1), 
the move towards less interpersonal and more execution-oriented can-
didates (decreasing factor 2), the change towards less charismatic and 
more analytical candidates (increasing factor 3), and the change from 
more creative-strategic to more detail-oriented candidates (decreasing 
factor 4).

The third hypothesis (“H = N”) tests whether the factor scores for the 
hired  CEO  candidates  equal  those  for  the  non-hired  CEO  candidates, 
providing  evidence  of  how  companies  screen  and  choose  among 
considered  CEOs.  Not  surprisingly,  hired  CEOs  have  higher  general 
ability (higher factor 1), although this selection is more concentrated in 
the late period. Hired CEO candidates are also less execution-oriented 
and  more  interpersonal  (higher  factor  2)  than  non-hired  CEO  candi-
dates, and this selection is also stronger in the late period. Further, hired 
CEOs are more charismatic and less analytical (lower factor 3) and more 
creative-strategic and less detail-oriented (higher factor 4) than the non- 
hired CEO candidates. These differences are all statistically significant.
The fourth hypothesis (“E = L”) compares the factor scores for hired 
CEO candidates in the early and late periods. While we see clear trends 
in the pool of considered candidates, there could also be trends in the 
screening  and  selection  behavior  of  the  hiring  companies  that  would 
contribute to the trends in hired CEOs. For example, focusing on female 
CEO candidates, He and Whited (2024) argue that corporate demand for 
these  particular  executives  can  depend  on  productivity  differences, 
search  costs,  and  board  distaste.  If  these  determinants  vary  over  the 
sample period, this could lead to varying corporate demand for these 
particular  executives.  Interestingly,  for  general  ability  (factor  1)  and 
interpersonal versus execution skills (factor 2), the factor scores of the 
hired CEOs are largely similar in the early and late periods. We see no 
evidence of changes in the hired CEOs across these two factors, which is 
notable because there are substantial trends in these two factors in the 
pool of assessed CEO candidates. This evidence is consistent with the 
presence of two concurrent trends. One trend is a decline in the supply of 
general  ability  and  interpersonal  skills  in  the  pool  of  assessed  CEO 
candidates.  The  other  is  an  increased  screening  for  candidates  with 
better  general  ability  (higher  factor  1)  and  more  interpersonal  skills 
(higher  factor  2)  when  hiring  companies  select  among  potential  CEO 
candidates.  These  two  trends  seem  to  offset,  resulting  in  only  small 
changes in the hired CEOs’ general ability (factor 1) and interpersonal 
skills (factor 2). Importantly, under this interpretation, the hiring com-
panies’  increased  selectivity  and  screening  for  general  ability  and 
interpersonal skills is due to a decline in the supply of these skills in the 
pool of potential CEO candidates, not due to technological or organi-
zational changes that require a different kind of CEO.

Turning to factors 3 and 4, the last columns of Panel A of Table 3
reveal  significant  changes.  In  the  late  period,  hired  CEOs  are  more 
analytical and less charismatic (increasing factor 3), although the hired 
CEOs are still more charismatic than the average candidate (negative 
factor 3). Hired CEOs are also less creative-strategic and more detail- 
oriented  (decreasing  factor  4)  while  remaining  more  strategic  than

3.4. Individual characteristics

For  an  additional  robustness  check,  we  also  analyze  trends  in  the 
individual  characteristics.  Table  5 shows  the  average  grades  in  the 
specific characteristics for the assessed and hired CEO candidates, and 
the trends are consistent with those from the factor analysis. Consistent 
with a decline in interpersonal skills (decreasing factor 2), the average 
grades for the specific characteristics “calm under pressure,” “flexible,” 
“teamwork,”  “oral  communication,”  and  “treats  people  with  respect” 
decreased significantly in the late period. The average grades of listening 
skills  and  open  to  criticism  remain  essentially  unchanged  in  the  late 
period. Consistent with a decline in charisma in the general pool of CEO 
candidates, the average grades for “enthusiasm” and “persuasion” also 
decrease.

For  the  hired  CEOs,  interpersonal  and  social  skills  also  appear  to 
decline,  although  not  as  strongly  as  in  the  pool  of  all  assessed  CEO 
candidates, possibly because corporations increasingly screen for these 
characteristics  in  their  hiring  decisions.  The  grades  for  “calm  under 
pressure,”  “oral  communication,”  “enthusiasm”  and  “persuasion”  all 
decrease  significantly  in  the  late  period.  The  grades  for  “flexible,” 
“teamwork,” “treats people with respect,” and “listening skills” do not 
change  significantly,  while  the  grade  for  “open  to  criticism”  actually 

increases.

Consistent with the factor analysis, the grades for the specific char-
acteristics confirm that interpersonal and social skills for the assessed 
and  hired  CEO  candidates  have  not  increased  over  time.  If  anything, 
they have decreased.

4. Executives considered for the same company

Several  companies  in  our  dataset  assess  multiple  candidates.  This 
allows us to analyze company-specific relations for the candidates who 
are  assessed  and  hired.  This  analysis  serves  two  purposes.  First,  it  is 
useful, in general, to consider whether, for example, high-ability CEOs 
tend to coincide with other high-ability executives or whether the skills 
in  the  executive  office  typically  differ  across  executives.  We  are  not 
aware  of  existing  studies  of  this  issue,  likely  due  to  the  difficulty  of 
collecting  the  needed  data.  Second,  the  matching  patterns  over  the 
sample  period indicate  that companies increasingly  match candidates 
based on their general ability (factor 1) and decreasingly match based on 
interpersonal characteristics (factor 2). These trends provide supporting 
evidence of the increased importance of general ability (positive Factor 
1) and the decreasing importance of interpersonal characteristics (factor 
2) when companies evaluate managerial candidates.

For the analysis of company-specific matching, we distinguish situ-
ations  where  there  are  several  CEO  candidates  assessed  by  the  same 
hiring  company  from  situations  where  a  hiring  company  considers 
several candidates for different positions. When there are several CEO 
candidates, we calculate the pair-wise correlations of their factor scores 
by forming all possible (unordered) pairs of these candidates. To illus-
trate, if CEO candidates A, B, and C are assessed for a company, and 
candidates D and E are assessed for another company, then the four pairs 
are:  AB,  AC,  BC,  and  DE.  Mostly,  when  several  CEO  candidates  are 
assessed for the same company, they are assessed as part of the same 
search. Of the 709 CEO–CEO pairs, 419 are assessed within a 90-day 
window.

period (Panel C). The total number of pairs in Panels B and C is smaller 
than in Panel A, as pairs spanning both periods are excluded from these 
panels. Despite the smaller sample size in the early period, the corre-
lations for  factors two  and three  remain positive  and statistically sig-
nificant. In the late period, however, factor 1 becomes significant while 
factor 2 loses significance. This shift suggests that companies increas-
ingly prioritize general ability (factor 1) over the traits represented by 
factor  2  (interpersonal  and  execution  skills)  when  evaluating  CEO 
candidates.

A similar tendency is seen in Panels D, E, and F, which show pair- 
wise  correlations  for  the  (ordered)  pairs  of  CEO  and  CFO  candidates 
considered  for  the  same  company  (this  is  slightly  different  from  the 
CEO–CEO correlations that consider unordered pairs). To illustrate, if a 
company considers candidates A and B for the CEO position and can-
didates C and D for the CFO position, the four CEO–CFO pairs are AC, 
AD, BC, and BD. Naturally, these assessments are less clustered in time 
than the CEO–CEO pairs, and only 142 of the 581 CEO–CFO pairs in 
the sample are assessed within a 90-day window.

Panel D of Table 6 again finds a strong positive correlation for gen-
eral ability (factor 1), and companies that consider CEOs with higher 
general ability also consider CFOs with higher general ability. Moreover, 
the positive and significant correlation between the CEOs’ factor 3 and 
the CFOs’  factor 1 indicates that more analytical and less charismatic 
(higher factor 3) CEOs tend to be paired with CFOs with higher general 
ability  (higher  factor  1).  The  negative  and  significant  correlation  be-
tween  the  CEOs’  factor  2  and  the  CFOs’  factor  4  indicates  that  more 
execution-oriented  CEOs  (lower  factor  2)  pair  with  more  creative- 
strategic  CFOs  (higher  factor  4),  and  conversely,  that  more  interper-
sonal CEOs (higher factor 2) pair with more detail-oriented CFOs (lower 
factor 4).

Panels E and F of Table 6 present the correlations for the early and 
late  periods  separately.  The  significant  correlation  in  general  ability 
(factor 1) is evident in both periods. Again, this suggests that general 
ability is an important factor when evaluating managerial talent, and 
there  is  no  evidence  that  the  hiring  companies  have  reduced  their 
attention to this factor. Moreover, the CEO and CFO candidates’ inter-
personal skills (factor 2) are significantly correlated (albeit negatively) 
in  the  early  period,  suggesting  a  level  of  substitutability  between  the 
execution and interpersonal skills captured by this factor. However, this 
(negative) correlation becomes insignificant in the late period, which is 
consistent  with  interpersonal  skills  becoming  less  relevant  when 
selecting candidates in the later sample period.

We also  analyze,  but do not report,  the  283 (ordered)  CEO–COO 
pairs  (100  pairs  are  assessed  within  a  90-day  window).  While  the 
number of CEO–COO pairs is smaller, we still find a positive and sta-
tistically  significant  (at  the  1  %  level)  correlation  in  general  ability 
(factor 1).

To summarize, we find a positive and significant correlation in the 
general ability (factor 1) for candidates that are considered for the same 
company, and this correlation is robust across positions and periods. It is 
consistent  with  assortative  matching  of  executives,  such  that  better 
CEOs, CFOs, and COOs tend to be considered by the same, presumably 

better, companies (see Gabaix and Landier, 2008; Tervio, 2008 for as-
sortative matching between CEOs and companies). We also see corre-
lations involving interpersonal skills (factor 2) in the early period, but 
these correlations weaken in the late period, which is consistent with 
interpersonal  skills  becoming 
important  when  evaluating 
less 
candidates.

5. Executive hiring

To analyze trends in hiring decisions, Panel A of Table 7 presents 
two-sided t-tests that compare the factor scores of the hired and non- 
hired CEO candidates. In the early period, hired CEO candidates were 
significantly  more  charismatic  (lower  factor  3)  and  more  creative- 
strategic  (higher  factor  4)  than  non-hired  CEO  candidates.  However, 
hiring decisions change in the late sample period, and hired CEO can-
didates  have  more  general  ability  (higher  factor  1)  and  are  less 
execution-oriented and more interpersonal (higher factor 2) than non- 
hired CEO candidates. However, despite this change in the selection in 
the late period, the hired candidates do not have significantly different 
scores  on factors  1  and  2,  as  shown in  the  last  column  of  Panel  A in 
Table 3. This result is consistent with Kaplan et al. (2012), who find that 
more  interpersonal  CEO  candidates  are  more  likely  to  be  hired  in  a 
sample  of  316  CEO  candidates  for  private  equity  and  venture  capital 
financed companies.

Considering CFO candidates, Panel B of Table 7 shows that, in the 
late  period,  hired  CFO  candidates  also  have  higher  general  ability 
(higher factor 1) and are less execution-oriented and more interpersonal 
(higher factor 2) than non-hired candidates.

Panel  C  of  Table  7 shows  stronger  results  for  Other  candidates, 
possibly due to the larger number of observations. In both periods, the 
hired Other candidates have more general ability (higher factor 1) and 
are  more  interpersonal  and  less  execution-oriented  (higher  factor  2)

6. Corporate objectives: trends and relation to CEO 
characteristics

A company’s situation might affect its demand for a particular ex-
ecutive  type.  Accordingly,  the  trends  in  the  characteristics  of  the 
assessed and hired executives could arise in response to changes in the 
hiring  companies.  For  example,  one  type  of  CEO  may  be  better  at 
running companies that want to grow, while another type may be better 
at running companies that want to streamline their operations. If there 
are increasing numbers of, for example, the former kind of companies, 
this increase in the corporate demand for CEOs that can manage growth 
could, in turn, lead to more of these CEOs being hired and possibly to a 
greater  number  of  this  type  among  the  potential  CEO  candidates.  In 
short,  trends  in  the  supply  of  executive  candidates  could  arise  in 
response to trends in the corporate demand for executives.

Most assessments include a scorecard that lists the main challenges 
facing the hiring companies. Accordingly, we look at the stated objec-
tives, how the objectives have changed over time, and how CEO char-
acteristics  are  related  to  those  objectives,  both  for  CEOs  who  are 
assessed and for those who are hired.

6.3. Demand and supply of CEOs

To evaluate whether the trends in the supply of executive charac-
teristics  are  consistent  with  the  trends  in  corporate  concerns  and 
changing demand for certain CEO types, note that the scorecard topics 
that are significantly related to the candidates’ factor scores are Strat-
egy,  Staffing,  and  Operations.  Thus,  we  consider  whether  trends  in 
corporate concerns for these issues explain the trends in the candidates’ 
factor scores.

Starting  with  Strategy,  Table  11 shows  an  increase  in  corporate 
concerns about Strategy. Moreover, companies with Strategy concerns 
tend to hire CEOs with greater general ability (higher factor 1) and who 
are  more  creative-strategic  (higher  factor  4),  as  shown  in  Panel  B  of 
Table 12. However, both general ability and creative-strategic abilities 
have  declined  (decreasing  factors  1  and  4)  in  the  pool  of  potential 
candidates,  and  thus  the  decreases  in  general  ability  and  creative- 
strategic  candidates  do  not  seem  to  arise  from  trends  in  corporate 
demand.

In  contrast,  corporate  concerns  about  Staffing  have  decreased,  as 
seen  in  Table  11,  and  companies  with  Staffing  concerns  tend  to  hire 
CEOs  with  greater  interpersonal  skills  (positive  factor  2),  as  seen  in 
Panel B of Table 12. Thus, the decline in executive interpersonal skills 
(declining factor 2) is consistent with a declining corporate demand for 
executives with the interpersonal skills to manage Staffing concerns.

Similarly, Operational concerns have increased, as seen in Table 11, 
and companies with operational concerns tend to hire more analytical 
CEOs  (higher  factor  3),  as  seen  in  Panel  B  of  Table  12.  Thus,  the 
increasing trend in analytical skills (increasing factor 3) in the pool of 
CEO  candidates  seems  consistent  with  increasing  corporate  concerns 
about Operations and an increasing demand for more analytical CEOs.
To summarize, the trend towards more execution-oriented and less 
interpersonal (decreasing factor 2) CEO candidates is consistent with a 
decreasing  trend  in  corporate  concerns  about  Staffing.  The  trend  to-
wards  more  analytical  and  less  charismatic  (increasing  factor  3)  CEO 
candidates  is  consistent  with  increasing  corporate  concerns  about 

Operations. However, the declining trend in general ability (decreasing 
factor 1) and decrease in creative-strategic abilities (decreasing factor 4) 
are not consistent with the changes in corporate demand. In fact, the 
increasing  corporate  concerns  about  Strategy  should  increase  the 
corporate demand for CEOs with greater general ability (higher factor 1) 
and  more  creative-strategic  abilities  (higher  factor  4).  Thus,  the 
declining  trends  in  general  ability  (decreasing  factor  1)  and  creative- 
strategic  (decreasing  factor  4)  CEO  candidates  appear  to  be  due  to  a 
generally deteriorating supply of executive candidates with these skills.

7. Conclusion and discussion

We analyze more than 4900 executive assessments to study changes 
in the characteristics and objectives of CEOs in the twenty-year period 
from 2000 to 2019. As in Kaplan and Sorensen (2021), we find that four 
factors–which  we  interpret  as  general  ability,  execution  vs.  interper-
sonal skills, charisma vs. analytical skills, and being creative-strategic 
vs. detail-oriented–explain roughly half the total variation in the spe-
cific characteristics for this sample of executive assessments.

We focus on trends over time. In the late period (candidates assessed 
in or after 2009), the average CEO candidate has lower overall ability, is 
more execution oriented and less interpersonal, more analytical and less 
charismatic, and more detail-oriented and less creative-strategic than in 
the early period. The CEO candidates who are actually hired to become 
CEOs are also more analytical and less charismatic as well as more detail 
oriented and less creative-strategic in the late period. At the same time, 
they  are  similar  in  overall  ability  and  execution  vs.  interpersonal 
orientation  compared  to  CEOs  hired  earlier.  In  the  late  period,  we 
therefore find no evidence of an increase in interpersonal and “softer” 
skills. Those skills are similar in the subset of hired CEOs and weaker in 
the larger pool of assessed CEO candidates.

Deming (2017) argues that the demand for social skills has increased 
because jobs increasingly involve team production and trading of tasks 
among workers. Fuller et al. (2021) investigate descriptions of executive 
positions as part of search processes and find an increasing demand for 
social skills for CEOs, where social skills include motivating, persuading, 
listening to, and empathizing with others. In our data, we do not find any 
increase in the importance of interpersonal skills for the hired CEOs. In 
our later sample period, CEO candidates have lower interpersonal skills, 
and hired CEO candidates have roughly the same interpersonal skills.

Our  results  are  more  consistent  with  Pfeffer  (2015,  2022),  who 
suggests  that  little  has  changed  fundamentally  for  CEOs.  Successful 
leaders  have  always  “built  their  power  bases,  embraced  ambiguity, 
eschewed  popularity  contests,  adapted  and  mastered  the  science  of 
influence.”

Our larger dataset also allows us to compare the executive candidates 
that are considered for the same hiring company. For example, do high- 
ability CEOs attract other higher-ability executives? The answer appears 
to be yes. In both the early and late periods, we find a strong positive 
correlation between the general ability of assessed CEOs and other ex-
ecutives assessed for positions in the same hiring company, suggesting 
that higher-ability executives complement each other. We do not find 
consistent  relations  between  other  factors  for  executives  at  the  same 
company.

Finally, it is possible that a company’s situation affects its demand 
for a certain type of CEO and other executives. For example, one CEO 
may be better at managing a business that wants to grow while another 
may be better at a business that wants to streamline its operations. Most 
of  the  assessments  include  the  objectives  and  challenges  facing  the 
hiring  companies.  Accordingly,  we  study  how  those  objectives  and 
challenges relate to the characteristics of the CEOs who are assessed and 
hired. We find an increase in demand for CEOs with skills in organic 
growth, operations and strategy. We find a decrease in demand for CEOs 
with skills in staffing. Again, these results are not obviously consistent 
with an increase in demand for CEOs with interpersonal or social skills.
The  trend  towards  more  execution-oriented  and  less  interpersonal

B. Cluster Analysis
Cluster analysis is a statistical technique used to group individual candidates into clusters based on similarities and differences in their charac-
teristics, aiming to identify underlying groupings in the data. Unlike factor analysis, which reduces data dimensionality by identifying continuous 
latent factors, cluster analysis categorizes the candidates into mutually exclusive groups where intra-cluster similarity is maximized. Therefore, cluster 
analysis is more suited for identifying groups of similar candidates and analyzing trends in the relative frequency of candidates being attributed to 
each cluster rather than gradual shifts in the clusters themselves. In contrast, factor analysis is more suited for analyzing trends over time in the relative 
averages of the factors because the candidates are scored on a continuous metric.

In our implementation, we use Stata’s “cluster kmeans” command. This algorithm randomly picks k observations from the data as starting centers 
for the k clusters. Then, the algorithm assigns each observation to its closest cluster center and, for each cluster, calculates the mean of the assigned 
observations. In the next iteration, these means become the new cluster centers. This process continues until no observations change cluster. The 
clustering algorithm outputs the final cluster means and the cluster to which each observation belongs.

Ideally, if the data is naturally clustered, the starting point of the algorithm will have no impact on the final cluster centers and the cluster that each 
observation belongs to. However, if the data is not naturally organized into k clusters, it is possible for the algorithm to arrive at slightly different final 
clusters depending on the randomly selected starting centers.

A challenge with our data is the large positive correlation among all the characteristics, as captured by the first factor in the factor analysis. 
Therefore, the cluster algorithm will be predisposed to differentiate clusters based on general ability rather than differences in relative characteristics. 
To address this issue, we demean the candidates’ grades across all the candidates’ characteristics before conducting the cluster analysis and include the 
individual candidate means in any trend analysis.

Using a k-means algorithm, we find three consistent clusters in the demeaned data, which we interpret as: interpersonal, execution-oriented, and 
charismatic candidates. When we increase the number of clusters, allowing for up to seven clusters, the mean competencies for the clusters are no 
longer stable, and the k-means algorithm returns different cluster means and groupings when started from different starting points. This indicates that 
the data are not naturally grouped into more than three clusters, and that more than three clusters overfits the data. For the remaining of the analysis, 
we use three clusters.

Table B.1 presents the loadings of our clusters. Cluster 1 contains candidates with positives means for Listening Skills, Open to Criticism, and Treats 
People with Respect. It has negative means for Aggressive and Fast. And we interpret Cluster 1 as containing candidates who are predominantly 
interpersonal. Cluster 2 has a positive mean for Aggressive, Holds People Accountable, and Removes Underperformers and a negative mean for 
Listening Skills, Open to Criticism, and Treats People with Respect. We interpret Cluster 2 as containing execution-oriented candidates. Cluster 3 has a 
positive mean for Aggressive, Enthusiasm, and Persuasion. It has a negative mean for Analytical Skills, Attention to Detail, and Organization. We 
interpret Cluster 3 as containing charismatic candidates. As seen in Table B.2, there are 478 CEOs in the interpersonal cluster, 468 in the execution 
cluster, and 353 CEO candidates in the charisma cluster.

Table B.3 shows the results from regressing the hiring decision on the mean competency score and indicator variables for the cluster the candidate 
is assigned to. The mean competency is highly significant, but the cluster indicators are not. This is consistent with the results in Table 8 that show that 
Factor 1 contains the most predictive power out of all the factors.

Table B.4 tests whether CEO candidates are increasingly allocated to certain clusters over time. In the later period, the CEO candidates’ mean 
competency rating decreases by 0.149 standard deviations compared to the earlier period. Additionally, late CEOs demonstrate a 9 percentage-point 
decrease in the likelihood of being assigned to the charisma cluster and a 9 percentage-point increase in the likelihood of being assigned to the 
execution cluster. These results reflect the analysis in Table 4, Panel A, which shows that CEO candidates in the Late period have lower general ability,
