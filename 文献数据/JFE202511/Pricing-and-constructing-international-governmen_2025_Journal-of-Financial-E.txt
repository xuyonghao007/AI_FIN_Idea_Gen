This paper derives a stochastic discount factor for currency-hedged government bonds of developed markets 
by  projecting  returns  onto  the  unconditional  mean–variance  efficient  (UMVE)  portfolio.  Priced  risks  of 
international bonds differ fundamentally from those of currencies. The UMVE portfolio achieves a Sharpe 
ratio over twice the average of individual markets, with the market price of risk peaking during crises and 
periods with high inflation dispersion. While bond returns exhibit a strong factor structure, common sources of 
variation are only weakly connected to priced risks. Hedging unpriced risks in naive or factor-based strategies 
significantly improves Sharpe ratios, even under portfolio weight constraints.

1. Introduction

Sovereign bond markets represent one of the largest and most liquid 
asset classes globally, second only to public equity markets. Yet, little 
existing academic research exists on how internationally diversified 
government bond portfolios are priced, their risk-return characteris-
tics, or the efficiency of alternative portfolio construction and trading 
strategies. In fact, sovereign bond pricing models are almost exclu-
sively developed in single-country settings. Similarly, the literature on 
active government bond portfolio management primarily focuses on 
optimal duration choices or relative mispricing within a fixed-income 
market, with little discussion of allocation across bond markets (see, 
for example, the relevant chapters in Bodie et al. (2024)).

In contrast to the single-country focus of academic research, the 
availability of globally diversified government bond funds and exch-
ange-traded funds (ETFs) has increased over the past years and the 
demand for these products is on the rise.1

In this paper, we depart from the traditional single-country focus 
and take an asset class perspective to investigate the risk factors driving 
multi-country bond portfolios. We hereby assume that the positions 
in  the  different  bond  markets  are  financed  in  the  respective  local 
currencies, i.e. via short positions in the local money markets. Since 
this strategy is equivalent to the widely observed practice of managing 
foreign exchange (FX) risk by hedging via a FX-forward contract, we 

will refer to these portfolios as FX-risk-hedged.2 We derive several key 
results. To our knowledge, we are first to provide stylized facts on 
internationally diversified developed markets government bond port-
folios.  Our  main  sample  comprises  ten  major  developed  sovereign 
debt markets, denoted as G10∗, collectively representing approximately 
70%  of  the  global  market  capitalization  for  local-currency  debt.  In 
particular, we show that substantial diversification benefits arise even 
from naive diversification strategies, such as GDP-weighted portfolios, 
simply due to imperfectly correlated term-structure movements in the 
different markets.

Second,  we  construct  the  unconditional  mean–variance  efficient 
(UMVE) portfolio from the G10∗ markets, thus providing a stochastic 
discount factor (SDF). We find that the UMVE portfolio can be ob-
tained using expected return forecasts based on forward spreads and 
real  yields  and  a  time-varying  covariance  matrix,  estimated  with  a 
shrinkage method. The resulting SDF successfully prices all individual 
G10∗ bond markets as well as a range of dynamic trading strategies.

Third, we analyze the properties of the UMVE portfolio. We find 
that switching from single-country bond markets to the UMVE portfolio 
improves the Sharpe ratio substantially, from an average of 0.46 for 
individual markets to a value that exceeds 1. We also show that the 
expected Sharpe ratio of the UMVE exhibits substantial time-variation. 
Specifically, it increases around the Global Financial Crisis (GFC) in

2012 and during the COVID-19 crisis. The highest expected Sharpe 
ratio  is  observed  in  the  fourth  quarter  of  2022,  a  period  marked 
by inflation shocks, tightening monetary policies, and declining asset 
market valuations, which likely increased investors’ marginal utility 
of  wealth.  Furthermore,  we  find  that  the  expected  Sharpe  ratio  of 
the UMVE portfolio is significantly related to inflation and that this

inflation is associated with lower expected Sharpe ratios whereas the 
opposite is the case afterwards. In the post-GFC sample, we also observe 
a  strong  positive  relation  between  the  dispersion  of  inflation  rates

Fourth, the analysis reveals that there is very little relation between 
common sources of variation in international bond market returns and 
priced  risks.  A  principal  component  analysis  (PCA)  shows  that  the 
G10∗ government bond returns exhibit a strong factor structure, with 
the  first  3  principal  components  (PCs)  explaining  86%  of  the  total 
variance. However, these common sources of variation represent mostly 
unpriced risks, since all PCs combined account for only about 30% of 
the variation in UMVE returns.

We  demonstrate  that  the  UMVE  portfolio’s  exposure  to  FX  risk 
is economically negligible. Consistent with this, the UMVE portfolio 
returns are largely uncorrelated with an SDF that prices currencies. 
Notably,  the  component  of  the  UMVE  return  that  is  orthogonal  to 
the currency SDF prices international bond portfolios as well as the 
original UMVE portfolio. These results indicate that the priced risks of 
international bond portfolios are fundamentally distinct from those of 
currency portfolios.

Finally, we offer valuable insights for bond investors. Our analysis 
reveals  that  strategies  such  as  naive  diversification  or  factor-based 
approaches like carry, value, and momentum exhibit large amounts of 
unpriced risk. By constructing portfolios that hedge out these unpriced 
components,  Sharpe  ratios  improve  markedly.  For  instance,  apply-
ing such a hedging strategy to a naively diversified (1∕𝑁) portfolio 
nearly doubles its Sharpe ratio. Substantial improvements are observed 
for other popular bond portfolio strategies, such as carry or value. 
Moreover, we find that, for practical implementation, accurately esti-
mating expected returns is crucial, while estimation of variances plays 
a comparatively lesser role.

Implementing bond portfolio strategies that hedge unpriced risks 
often involves large absolute portfolio weights across different markets. 
To assess the feasibility of such strategies, we conduct sensitivity analy-
ses and impose restrictions on the absolute values of portfolio weights. 
Our  results  indicate  that  portfolio  performance  remains  remarkably 
robust under such constraints. Significant drops in performance are 
observed only when long-only restrictions are applied. However, such 
constraints appear excessively restrictive, as bond futures are available 
in  all  but  one  market  in  the  sample,  allowing  professional  market 
participants to easily short specific markets.

Our paper contributes to several strands of literature. First, it relates 
to research on return prediction in single domestic government bond 
markets. This literature has grown substantially since Fama and Bliss 
(1987), who document the empirical failure of the expectations hy-
pothesis, showing that returns can be predicted using maturity-specific 
forward spreads. Cochrane and Piazzesi (2005) make fuller use of the 
forward  curve  for  return  prediction.  They  provide  evidence  that  a 
common risk factor prices bonds of various maturities. Ludvigson and 
Ng (2009) show that macroeconomic and financial variables substan-
tially improve the predictability of bond risk premia. Similarly, Cieslak 
and Povala (2015) highlight the critical role of inflation in predicting 
government bond returns, showing that deviations of bond yields from 
trend inflation carry valuable information about future returns.

Second, a limited number of papers examine international bond 
return predictability and yield curve fluctuation across different mar-
kets (e.g., Jotikasthira et al., 2015; Bekaert and Ermolov, 2023). Ilma-
nen (1995) analyzes bond predictability in six countries, finding that

expected bond returns tend to be high when the term spread and the 
real bond yield are elevated. Dahlquist and Hasseltoft (2013) extend 
the  Cochrane–Piazzesi  framework  by  constructing  local  and  global

Third,  our  paper  contributes  to  the  literature  on  SDFs  derived 
from mean–variance efficient portfolios, as developed by Hansen and 
Richard (1987), Ferson and Siegel (2001), Ferson and Siegel (2003), Fer-

the unconditional mean–variance efficient portfolio, but we analyze 
a  previously  unexplored  yet  important  class  of  assets:  international 
government bonds.

In summary, the existing related literature primarily adopts a single-
market perspective on government bonds or focuses on entirely other 
asset classes, such as the stock market or currency markets. Our ap-
proach differs, as we analyze the pricing of developed countries’ gov-
ernment bonds as a distinct asset class. We derive the UMVE portfolio 
out-of-sample, provide insights about its ability to price bond markets 
and strategies, about its average realized Sharpe ratio, the time series 
properties of the resulting expected risk premia, and on how common 
sources of return variation in international bond portfolios are related 
to priced risks.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 outlines the theoretical 
foundations. Section 3 describes the empirical strategy and presents 
stylized evidence. Section 4 presents the main empirical findings. The 
role of exchange rates is analyzed in Section 5. Section 6 explores 
the implications for bond portfolio strategies, and Section 7 provides 
robustness checks. Section 8 concludes.

3. Empirical strategy and stylized facts

Empirical implementation of the approach described in Section 2 to 
construct the UMVE portfolio requires the estimation of (i) expected re-
turns and (ii) the conditional variance. To determine the weights of the 
UMVE portfolio at any time 𝑡, we will use only information available 
at that point in time, while returns are observed out-of-sample, in the 
subsequent period.

The estimation of conditional covariances faces substantial chal-
lenges in a high-dimensional asset space, such as in the equity market. 
Indeed, the estimated covariance matrix might not have full rank or 
even exhibit negative eigenvalues. Therefore, the low-dimensional asset 
space  resulting  from  our  focus  on  a  small  cross-section  of  10  ma-
jor government bond markets provides an important advantage when 
estimating the variance–covariance matrix.

The remainder of this section explains how bond excess returns 
are  defined  and  presents  stylized  evidence  for  the  markets  in  our 
sample. Furthermore, it describes how expected excess returns and their 
conditional covariances are estimated and reports the data sources.

In our main empirical analysis we focus on government bond mar-
kets of the following countries, which we refer to as G10∗: Australia, 
Canada, Germany, Japan, New Zealand, Norway, Sweden, Switzerland, 
the United Kingdom, and the United States. The currencies of these 
countries are the ones which are frequently considered in the foreign 
exchange literature, which refers to them as ‘‘G10 currencies’’ (see, for 
example, Lustig et al. (2019) and Chernov et al. (2023)).

Government bonds of these countries are typically issued in local 
markets and denominated in local currencies (Chen et al., 2019). In 
fact, 99.8% of the outstanding long-term central government debt of 
the countries in our main sample is denominated in domestic currency, 
according to the Bank for International Settlements (BIS).6 The analysis 
in this study is therefore based on local currency bonds. The Eurozone 
is represented by German government bonds, arguably the safest and 
most liquid ones.

The countries in the main sample represent about 70% of the global 
local-currency long-term debt in the BIS statistic, as of the end of 2022. 
As we use only German bonds to represent the Eurozone, and since 
Euro area government bonds tend to be highly correlated, our implicit 
coverage of world government bond markets is plausibly even larger. 
Finally, the issuing countries in the sample are very unlikely to default, 
such  that  their  government  bonds  can  be  considered  as  practically 
default-risk free assets.

it  becomes  clear  that  international  diversification  matters  for  bond 
investors.  By  definition,  naive  (1∕𝑁)  diversification  across  markets 
achieves an average excess return identical to the cross-sectional av-
erage of the single-countries in the sample, which equals 3.3% p.a. 
The individual countries’ average standard deviation equals 7.4%. By 
diversifying, investors could have lowered the standard deviation of 
their portfolio considerably by almost 20% to 6.1%, thereby achieving a 
substantially higher Sharpe ratio. While the individual countries’ aver-
age Sharpe ratio equals 0.46 in our sample, naive diversification across 
the 10 markets increases the Sharpe ratio to 0.54. A GDP-weighted 
strategy yields an only marginally worse risk-return trade-off, with a 
Sharpe ratio of 0.53. Panel C of Table  2 explores whether simple port-
folio strategies already realize most of the benefits of internationally 
diversified bond portfolios, or whether conditional strategies, which 
should be easily accessible to bond portfolio managers, lead to further 
improvements. To this end, we investigate long-short portfolios sorted 
on carry, value and momentum. Returns to these strategies have been 
shown to contribute significantly to portfolio performance in different 
asset classes, such as equity, currencies, and fixed income (e.g., Fama 
and French, 2012; Asness et al., 2013; Koijen et al., 2018). We therefore 
use the following characteristics to construct simple dynamic portfolio 
strategies:  the  slope  of  the  yield  curve,  i.e.  the  difference  between 
the 10-year yield and the local 1-month yield (carry), the real bond 
yield, i.e. the difference between the 10-year yield and inflation (value), 
and momentum, i.e. the cumulative return over 12 months skipping 
the  most  recent  one  (momentum).  We  document  that  all  long-short 
bond portfolios achieve positive performance. The Sharpe ratios for 
carry, value and momentum are 0.81, 0.47, and 0.28, respectively. 
Interestingly, the carry strategy outperforms all others substantially. 
This evidence is consistent with the literature highlighting that returns 
from carry generate positive alpha for a host of asset classes (Koijen 
et al., 2018).

4.3. Pricing other countries

Since a global SDF should not only price the assets from which 
it has been constructed, we go beyond the original set of the G10∗
markets, and implement tests using a wider universe of bond markets. 
Specifically, we test if the UMVE portfolio prices single-country bonds 
and strategies based on an extended set of countries, which are not 
used to construct the UMVE portfolio. We therefore run regression tests, 
as specified in Section 4.2, using the excess returns of other countries’ 
bond markets and the trading strategies associated with most of these 
markets13 as the dependent variable in Eq.  (12).

We report the results in Table  9. While, on average, alphas are 
larger  in  magnitude  compared  to  the  results  for  the  main  markets 
(see Table  6), none is statistically significant at the 5% level. This is 
confirmed both for markets pegged to the Euro and, importantly, also 
for other markets. The regression for Italy exhibits the largest 𝑡-stat, 
with a value of 1.65. This might reflect the different behavior of Italian 
bonds during and after the European sovereign debt crisis, when their 
status as a safe asset was in doubt. The picture does not materially 
change when we consider bond strategies constructed from this set of 
bonds. Table  10 shows that most alphas are insignificant (at the 5% 
level), with the exception of cross-sectional carry and cross-sectional 
12-month momentum. In summary, there is little evidence of market 
segmentation, as the UMVE portfolio constructed from the main bond 
markets does not generally lead to large pricing errors even in a broader 
asset universe.

4.5. Sharpe ratio decomposition

Section  4.4  documents  that  the  Sharpe  ratios  of  almost  all  tra-
ditional trading strategies can be substantially improved by hedging 
out risk components unrelated to UMVE portfolio returns. This section 
analyzes whether this improvement is mainly due to the SDF capturing 
the cross-sectional and time series variation of the various markets’ 
expected returns, the time-varying return dependence, or to both being 
of similar importance.

To this end, we successively shut off elements in the estimation 
of the UMVE portfolio (i.e., expected returns, variances, correlations) 
and only let the others vary. Comparing hedging strategies based on 
the  restricted  UMVE  portfolios  helps  to  identify  the  sources  of  the 
Sharpe ratio improvements. Specifically, our approach considers the 
following alternatives: First, we fix expected returns at the historical 
average across all markets over the estimation window, but use the 
estimates for the conditional variance from the full model (as described 
in Section 3.4.2). Thus, we shut off any cross-sectional variation in 
conditional expected returns when constructing the UMVE portfolio. 
Second, we use estimates for expected returns for each market from 
the full model (as described in Section 3.4.1), but employ a covariance 
matrix V ̄𝜎
𝑡  where we set each market’s variance equal to the cross-
sectional average. Thus, here we shut off any cross-sectional variation 

in conditional variances when constructing the UMVE portfolio. Third, 
we again estimate expected returns based on the full model (as de-
scribed in Section 3.4.1), but use a covariance matrix V ̄𝜌
𝑡  where we 
set all correlations equal to the average estimated pairwise correlation. 
Thus,  here  we  shut  off  any  cross-sectional  variation  in  conditional 
correlations. Note that all three described estimates are out-of-sample 
since the values at which a component of the estimation is fixed only 
depends on past returns. We describe our approach in more detail in 
Appendix F.

We refer to the hedged portfolios resulting from the three restricted 
SDF estimations as Hedged: Constant Expected Returns, Hedged: Constant 
Sigma and Hedged: Constant Correlation. Fig.  8 shows the annualized 
Sharpe ratios for these portfolios. For easier interpretation, the first 
bar displays the Sharpe ratios of the hedged portfolios based on the 
unrestricted UMVE portfolio returns, as already shown in Fig.  3. As 
can be seen from Fig.  8, only the portfolios Hedged: Constant Correlation
have Sharpe ratios close to the ones obtained from the hedged strategies 
that make full use of the estimated parameters, as is done in Fig.  3. In 
contrast, Sharpe ratios are much lower when using an SDF that is esti-
mated from the UMVE portfolios that assume constant expected returns 
or constant standard deviations across markets. When comparing the 
second (green) and third (turquoise) bars in the charts for the different 
strategies, we see that modeling the cross-section of expected returns 
is more important in defining the UMVE portfolio than estimating the 
cross-section of variances. Indeed, the hedged portfolios when using 
UMVE portfolio estimations based on constant variances have higher

4.6.1. Pre and post 2008 periods

Summary statistics for the sub-samples are presented in Table  12. 
We first observe that the excess returns of the UMVE portfolio analyzed 
in this paper do not exhibit a structural break around the financial 
crisis, as their mean values remain quite similar across the two sample 
periods.21 In contrast to Andrews et al. (2024), the Sharpe ratio of 
the UMVE portfolio in the earlier period is even higher than in the 
post-crisis period.

However, our results align with Andrews et al. (2024) in the sense 
that the term-structure slope turns out to be a key predictive variable 
in both studies. In the context of the UMVE portfolio, this variable 
has predictive power in both subsample periods, whereas in Andrews 
et al. (2024) this is only the case in the period after the great financial 
crisis. This highlights a key difference between a bond portfolio based 
on  term-structure  changes,  which  is  funded  in  the  respective  local 
money markets, and a strategy that is fully exposed to exchange rates 
movements.

Next, we re-run the regression analysis for the two sub-periods and 
report the results in Table  14. When comparing the subsample results 
to the ones for the full sample displayed in Table  13, the most im-
portant differences are related to the inflation variables. In accordance 
with Andrews et al. (2024), inflation affects expected Sharpe ratios of 
the UMVE portfolio differently in the pre- and the post-crisis periods. As 
shown in Table  14, the dispersion of inflation rates is not significantly 
associated with the risk premium before 2008, most likely reflecting the 
generally stable and more homogeneous environment among developed 
economies in a period sometimes termed the ‘‘great moderation’’. In 
contrast, there is a strong positive relationship in the period after the 
financial crisis.

Table  14 also shows that the level of trend inflation affects expected 
Sharpe  ratios  differently  before  and  after  August  2008:  before,  the 
sign is negative, whereas it is positive afterwards, and in both cases 
statistically significant. Thus, in the pre-crisis sample period, higher 
trend inflation is negatively related to expected Sharpe ratios, while 
the opposite it true in the second subsample period.

These results are consistent with a pre-crisis world, where higher 
inflation signals higher economic risk, as central banks’ inflation tar-
gets  were  frequently  exceeded  by  realized  rates.  Thus,  during  this 
period, a higher trend inflation may have led to a higher demand for 
precautionary savings, leading to lower expected risk adjusted bond 
portfolio returns. In contrast, parts of the post-crisis period are char-
acterized by low inflation rates, undershooting central banks’ targets 
and even creating the risk of deflation (see, e.g. Christensen et al., 
2015;  Fleckenstein  et  al.,  2017).  In  such  a  setting,  an  increase  in 
average trend inflation rates is likely to represent a positive signal about 
future economic growth, rather than the risk of excessive inflation. In 
response to an increase in trend inflation, investors are therefore likely 
to require higher risk adjusted returns for transferring wealth to the 
future, consistent with higher expected Sharpe ratios.

This  finding  relates  to  Andrews  et  al.  (2024),  as  they  find  that

5. The role of exchange rates

The portfolios analyzed in this paper are largely insulated from FX 
risk, since bond positions are always financed in the local currency. 
However, excess returns and ultimately UMVE returns may still be 
related to exchange rates. This section therefore investigates to what 
extent this is the case and whether any remaining exposure to FX risk 
is priced.

5.1. International bond returns and exchange rates

We begin by regressing bond excess returns on changes in exchange 
rates. We hereby consider three alternative definitions of bond returns: 
(i) bond excess returns as defined in Eq. (6), (ii) local currency excess 
returns, i.e. returns that would result from a ‘‘quanto-position’’ where 
the local currency excess return is converted into a USD excess return 
at a fixed exchange rate; and (iii) FX-unhedged bond excess returns, 
resulting from borrowing in USD and investing in the foreign bond.

Table  15 presents the results, highlighting two key findings. First, 
the currency risk exposure of bond excess returns, as defined in Eq. (6), 
closely mirrors that of local currency excess returns. In both cases, 
exchange rate changes explain only a marginal proportion of the vari-
ation in bond returns, with adjusted 𝑅2’s ranging from 0% to 2%. This 
contrasts with the regression results for unhedged bond returns, which 
show a strong relationship with exchange rate movements. In this case, 
exchange rate changes explain between 44% and 83% of bond excess 
returns within our sample. Consequently, FX-hedging, as implicit in 
the construction of bond excess returns defined in Eq. (6), effectively 
eliminates the majority of FX risk. 

22 Andrews et al. (2024) propose an SDF where the heterogeneity across 
countries is caused by their different exposures to real growth news shocks. 
In their model, a country’s real expected growth can change because expected 
global growth changes or, indirectly, because expected global inflation changes 
and this in turn affects expected global growth. The model parameters are 
calibrated such that shocks to expected global inflation are associated with an 
increase in the representative agent’s marginal utility, whereas the opposite is 
true for shocks to expected growth. In the pre-crisis period, the slope carry

The second finding from Table  15 is that, while exchange rates only 
explain a small fraction of the overall variation in FX-risk-hedged bond

In summary, bonds returns as defined in Eq. (6) exhibit only a weak 
relationship with exchange rate changes.

5.2. PCA’s of bond and currency returns

We next conduct a PCA of currency excess returns and compare 
the  resulting  loadings  with  those  from  FX-risk-hedged  bond  excess 
returns. As expected, in both cases, the first principal component (PC) 
reflects a level factor, with all markets loading positively. However, the 
correlation between the loadings on the first bond and the first currency 
PC is low, at 0.16. The second PC of currency excess returns represents 
a slope (or carry) factor, with positive loadings on high-interest-rate 
currencies and negative loadings on low-interest-rate currencies. This 
interpretation does not extend to the second PC of bond excess returns, 
as discussed in Section 4.4. The correlation between the loadings on 
the second bond and the second currency PCs is approximately 0.5.

Finally, the third PC lacks a clear economic interpretation, with only 
modest correlations between the loadings across bonds and currencies. 
A detailed exposition of this analysis is provided in Appendix H.

In summary, the PCA results highlight that FX-risk-hedged bond 
returns, as defined in Eq. (6), and currency returns exhibit distinctly 
different risk structures.

When relating the bond UMVE and the FX UMVE to the macroe-
conomy, we find distinct patterns. While the expected Sharpe ratios of 
the former are primarily related to inflation dynamics across countries 
(see Section 4.6), Chernov et al. (2023) find that FX UMVE returns 
are primarily related to US consumption growth. The important role 
of inflation for the bond UMVE is intuitive. Excess returns of long-
term  bonds  are  almost  mechanically  linked  to  inflation  shocks,  as 
inflation erodes the purchasing power of fixed future cash flows and 
correlates with the level of interest rates. Consequently, it is natural 
for inflation risk to be priced in international bond markets. This is 
consistent with Fang et al. (2025), who show that government bonds 
have negative exposure to both core and energy inflation. In contrast, 
these authors find that currencies behave differently: While they exhibit 
positive  and  sizable  exposure  to  energy  inflation,  exposure  to  core 
inflation is negative, albeit not statistically significant. This fact likely 
reflects the tendency of central banks to intervene – either directly 
in currency markets or indirectly through monetary policy decisions 
– thereby mitigating some of the effects of core inflation shocks on 
exchange rates. Thus, while Fang et al. (2025) find that certain FX trad-
ing strategies – such as carry – are exposed to inflation, the opposing 
signs of currencies’ exposures to core and energy inflation, which tend 
to offset each other, may explain why headline inflation has not been 
found to be significantly related to the FX UMVE.

6. Implications for bond portfolio strategies

In this section, we discuss implications of our findings for bond port-
folio management. We start by illustrating the time-varying expected 
risk premia of traditional bond portfolio strategies. We then provide 
information about the time-varying composition of the UMVE portfolio, 
and discuss to what extent extreme long and short positions, as shown 
in Table  11, are required to obtain the attractive risk-return relation of 
the UMVE.

Once the portfolio projection of the SDF is known, one can calculate 
the  expected  risk  premium  of  various  bond  strategies  in  real  time. 
Specifically,  for  each  bond  strategy  considered  by  an  investor,  she 
can obtain the expected Sharpe ratio based on estimates of expected 
excess returns and the covariance matrix. Fig.  11 illustrates the cross-
sectional  and  the  time  variation  of  expected  Sharpe  ratios  for  the 
trading strategies described in Section 4.1. Positive (negative) values 
are shown in green (red) color; darker areas indicate larger absolute 
values. First, Fig.  11 reveals considerable heterogeneity: simple diver-
sification (naive and GDP-weighted), and carry strategies have almost 
always positive expected Sharpe ratios, while momentum strategies 
show notable fluctuations between positive and negative values. The 
attractiveness of value strategies appears to vary at lower frequency.

While a real-time assessment of the risk-return properties of a given 
trading strategy, as illustrated in Fig.  11, may be useful for investors, 
it does not speak to the overall optimal portfolio. We thus turn to 
the analysis of the practical implementability of the UMVE portfolio. 
We have shown that hedged portfolio strategies lead to economically 
attractive risk-return ratios which, in most cases, beat the original trad-
ing strategies by a wide margin. It is therefore natural to ask whether 
these strategies would be implementable in practice. Such a practical 
implementation may face two challenges. First, the hedged portfolio 

Journal of Financial Economics 173 (2025) 104152 

strategies may require extreme position weights to achieve their high 
performance. To see how sensitive the results are with regard to a 
portfolio manager’s ability to take extreme market positions, we impose 
minimum and maximum weights for the hedged portfolio strategies 
and compare the resulting Sharpe ratios with the ones of the original 
strategies. A second potential concern relates to the feasibility of taking 
short positions. In principle, short positions should be easily achievable, 
since long-term government bond futures are available in all markets 
of our main sample with the exception of Norway.24 However, many 
asset managers, such as conventional mutual fund managers, face legal 
or investor-imposed short-selling constraints. We therefore also analyze 
strategies with minimum weights of zero in each market as a robustness 
check. Specifically, we implement the following two lower and upper 
bounds on portfolio weights: First, we restrict portfolio weights for each 
country between 0% and 20% and second, we consider a restricted 
strategy with portfolio weights in the range between −20% to +20%. 
We report the results in Fig.  12. Remarkably, even when we apply such 
relatively conservative weight restrictions, the hedged strategies beat 
the original trading strategies with Sharpe ratios close to those achieved 
by the unrestricted portfolios. However, it appears that a considerable 
part of the performance comes from short positions. While 13 out of 
14 long-only strategies still outperform their unhedged counterparts, 
their Sharpe ratios tend to be lower than those of portfolios that allow 
at  least  limited  short  positions.  This  observation  does  not  hold  for 
the momentum portfolios. Here, restricting investors from taking short 
positions even turns out to be beneficial. Overall, the hedged portfolios 
improve Sharpe ratios considerably, even when large portfolio weights 
are not allowed.

Finally, Table  11 shows that implementation of the UMVE requires 
high portfolio turnover which might raise concerns about trading costs. 
Yet, this is not likely to be an important issue for major government 
bond markets since they all feature derivatives markets. Trading costs 
for bond futures are very low, typically in the order of magnitude of 1 
basis point. Transactions in the cash market tend to be slightly more 
expensive. Favero et al. (2010) quantify the median cost of trading 
German government bonds with 10 year maturity with 3 basis points. 
Using a range from 1 to 3 basis points, trading costs will lower the 
return of the UMVE by only about 0.2% to 0.6% p.a.

7.2. Estimation of expected returns: Country-by-country dynamics

We  estimate  the  predictive  regression  specified  in  Eq.  (7)  using 
pooled OLS, which provides a single estimate for ̂𝛾𝑡 and ̂𝛿𝑡, respectively. 

While this is efficient if the underlying relationships are expected to 
be similar or identical across countries, it might bias results if there 
are cross-country differences. As a robustness check, we therefore run 
country-by-country regressions over expanding time windows, allowing 
coefficients ̂𝛾𝑖,𝑡 and ̂𝛿𝑖,𝑡 to vary across countries 𝑖 and time 𝑡. Appendix H 
presents the resulting time series of gamma and delta coefficients for 
each G10∗ market, as well as cross-sectional averages and standard de-
viations. Compared to the pooled OLS estimation, individual countries’ 
estimates are more unstable at the beginning of the sample, with much 
wider confidence intervals. While the point estimates of coefficients 
vary across countries and time, the general pattern is remarkably simi-
lar, and the cross-sectional mean of the individual countries’ estimates 
is close to the estimate when using the pooled OLS approach.

8. Conclusion

This paper investigates the pricing of currency-hedged government 
bond portfolios and presents several key findings. We document sub-
stantial diversification benefits for investors due to the imperfect cor-
relation between term-structure movements across bond markets, even 
when focusing only on developed markets and simple strategies, such 
as GDP-weighted or equally weighted portfolios.

We find that the unconditional mean–variance efficient portfolio 
of G10∗ government bonds can be constructed using expected return 
forecasts based on forward spreads and real yields, along with a time-
varying covariance matrix, estimated with a shrinkage method. The 
resulting SDF prices individual G10∗ bond markets and dynamic bond 
trading strategies effectively. Although the SDF derived in this paper 
is based on 10-year bond portfolios, we demonstrate that it also prices 
bonds of other maturities.

We explore several model extensions, incorporating additional vari-
ables into the expected return models, such as CDS-based sovereign 
credit risk measures, the term structure of forward interest rates and al-
ternative definitions of carry and value. We also confirm the results us-
ing alternative measurement currencies. Remarkably, the parsimonious 
UMVE model from the main analysis prices this asset class surprisingly 
well, even when compared to these more complex extensions.

We also investigate to what extent the bond pricing kernel captures 
FX risk. First, we demonstrate that, as bond positions are financed in 
their respective local currencies, the UMVE portfolio’s exposure to FX 
risk is economically negligible. Moreover, we show that the returns of 
the UMVE portfolio are largely unrelated to an SDF that prices curren-
cies. Consequently, using only the component of the UMVE return that 
is orthogonal to the currency SDF prices international bond portfolio 
equally well as the original UMVE. These findings highlight that the 
priced risks of international bond portfolios are fundamentally distinct 
from those of currency portfolios.

The UMVE portfolio has several notable properties. First, the opti-
mal international government bond portfolio achieves a large improve-
ment in the Sharpe ratio, increasing it from an average of approxi-
mately 0.46 for individual markets to a value exceeding 1. However, 
the UMVE exhibits substantial time-variation in the implied market 
price of risk. Specifically, it increases around recessions and crises, with 
the highest expected Sharpe ratio observed in the fourth quarter of 
2022, when concerns about inflation and tightening monetary policies 
raised investors’ marginal utility of wealth.

Additionally, the paper finds that the expected Sharpe ratio of the 
UMVE portfolio is strongly positively related to the absolute level and 
to the dispersion of inflation rates across the G10∗ countries. These 
relationships seem to be largely driven by the period after August 2008.
While bond returns exhibit a strong factor structure, with 86% of 
the variance being explained by the first three principal components, 
there is very little relation between common sources of variation in 
international bond market returns and priced risk. The first 10 principal 
components only explain around 30% of the UMVE portfolio return 
variation.

Consistent with this finding, portfolio strategies, such as equally 
weighting or various factor-based portfolio strategies, such as carry, 
value, and momentum strategies, all exhibit large amounts of unpriced 
risks. By constructing portfolios that hedge out such unpriced compo-
nents from bond strategies, Sharpe ratios improve substantially. Our 
analysis  also  suggests  that,  for  successful  practical  implementation, 
the  estimation  of  expected  returns  is  essential,  while  estimation  of 
variances matters to a lesser degree.

To shed light on the feasibility of strategies that hedge out un-
priced risks, we provide sensitivity analyses and impose restrictions on 
the absolute values of portfolio weights. We document that portfolio 
performance is remarkably robust to imposing such limits. Only when 
long-only restrictions are imposed, the portfolio performance drops. 
However, bond futures exist in almost all of the G10∗ markets, which 
should make shorting a particular market easy for professional market 
participants.

Recognizing that the existing finance literature provides little the-
oretical and empirical foundation on which international government 
bond portfolio strategies can be built, this paper provides a first step to 
fill this gap. However, given the growing size and economic importance 
of this asset class, this is likely to remain an active area of research. One 
obvious extension is to analyze pricing kernels for a broader universe 
of bond markets which also includes emerging markets, where default 
risks are likely to play a role.
