We assess how nine different categories of market participants trade relative to a comprehensive forecasted-
return variable based on 193 predictors. Firms and short sellers tend to be the smart money—both sell stocks
with low-forecasted returns, and their trades predict returns in the intended direction. Retail investors trade
against forecasted returns. Retail investors’ and institutions’ trades predict returns opposite to the intended
direction. This poor trading performance is driven by trades in stocks with either high- or low-forecasted returns.
The forecasted-return variable predicts returns more strongly in stocks with more intense retail trading,
consistent with retail investors exacerbating mispricing.

This paper provides a comprehensive investigation of how nine
categories of market participants—retail investors, short sellers, firms,
and six types of institutions—trade with respect to forecasted returns.

known return predictability. Our forecasted-return measure is based on
an adaptive group LASSO (least absolute shrinkage and selection oper-

and estimates a single expected return variable.

We then study how the return-predictability of each group’s trades is
impacted once we control for forecasted returns. We independently sort
stocks into quintiles based on each of the participant’s trades and the
forecasted return variable. We report the average monthly returns of the
resulting 25 cross-sorted portfolios.

One consistent result for all of the market participants is weaker or
insignificant return-predictability among stocks with average forecasted
returns. For all market participants, trades tend to predict returns more
strongly in stocks with either high or low forecasted returns, i.e., stocks
that anomaly strategies characterize as mispriced.

The weekly retail trade imbalance is a different story. Like Boehmer
et al. (2021), we find the retail trade imbalance predicts returns in the
intended direction. We further find that this predictability is robust
across all five of the forecasted-return quintiles, so the information
embedded in retail trade imbalances is largely orthogonal to the infor-
mation captured by forecasted returns. However, when we aggregate
retail trades over three years, we find negative return predictability, but
only in stocks with either high or low forecasted returns.

Like earlier studies, we find that aggregate institutional trades pre-
dict returns in the unintended direction. We further show that stocks
with high-forecasted returns drive this poor trading performance. In-
stitutions tend to sell stocks with high-forecasted returns that subse-

We then examine things from the perspective of forecasted returns,
and ask whether the predictability of our forecasted-return variable,
which is based on 193 characteristics or anomalies, is stronger when
there is more trading. Our findings show that it is related to more intense
retail trading, especially over the 3-year horizon, but not trades from the
other groups. This suggests that anomaly strategies may identify mis-
pricing generated by retail investors.

Our paper contributes to several literatures. We find that firms are
the most informed market participants. This has not been shown pre-
viously. Some earlier work relates share issuance to anomaly variables.
The relations are sometimes in the direction of anomaly-forecasted
returns and sometimes opposite to anomaly-forecasted returns. Baker
and Wurgler (2002) find that less profitable firms, larger firms, and firms
with high market-to-book ratios issue more shares. In these cases, share
issuance aligns with anomaly-forecasted returns. Pontiff and Woodgate
(2008) and McLean, Pontiff, and Watanabe (2009) find that firms with
positive stock return momentum issue more shares. In this case, share
issuance contradicts anomaly-expected returns. Our findings broaden
this investigation and show that when the entire universe of anomalies is
considered, share issues align with anomaly-forecasted returns. We also
show that forecasted returns cannot fully account for the strong return
predictability of share issues. Thus, share issuance decisions also reflect
managers’ private information.3

Our paper contributes to a growing literature on retail investors.
Barber and Odean (2013) point out that over short horizons (e.g.,
1-week, up to 1-month) retail trade imbalances predict returns in the
intended direction (see Kaniel, Saar, and Titman, 2008, Barber, Odean,
and Zhu, 2009a, Kaniel, Saar, Liu, and Titman, 2012, Kelly and Tetlock,
2013, Boehmer et al., 2021), whereas over longer horizons (e.g., 1-year)
retail trades predict returns opposite to the intended direction (see
Odean, 1999, Barber and Odean, 2000, Grinblatt and Keloharju, 2000,
Hvidkjaer, 2008, and Barber, Odean, and Zhu, 2009a and 2009b). We
confirm that temporary spikes in retail trading (i.e., weekly trade im-
balances) positively predict returns, whereas accumulated retail trades
for one year and three years predict returns in the wrong direction. We
show that the positive predictability is not caused by a positive corre-
lation with forecasted returns; instead, spikes in retail trades positively
predict returns despite being poorly positioned with respect to the factor
zoo. We also find that the poor retail trading performance over longer
horizons manifests primarily in stocks that anomalies characterize as
mispriced.

Griffin and Xu (2009) find that hedge fund’s reported equity hold-

results confirm their findings in a later sample;hedge fund long-position

An emerging literature utilizes quantities in addition to returns when
studying investors’ asset demands (e.g., Koijen and Yogo, 2019). This
approach highlights that investors’ elasticities to price or equity char-
acteristics and portfolio size are important for understanding their im-
pacts on asset markets. While our paper does not estimate elasticities, it
documents equilibrium quantities for a broad set of investors and pro-
vides new evidence on equilibrium quantities for retail investors.

Our paper has two important implications for the asset demand
literature. First, when estimating investor-level demand, it is common to
label the residual of 13F-reported ownership as the “household sector.”
Recent studies have found the “household sector” to be one of the most
price-elastic investors, implying households correct mispricing induced
by other investors (see Koijen, Richmond, and Yogo, 2023, and Li,
Sokolinksi and Tamoni, 2023). Yet, we find retail trades identified using
sub-penny pricing are poorly positioned with respect to anomaly port-
folios and thus likely contribute to mispricing in equilibrium. Therefore,
an investor type other than retail investors wthat do not file form 13F (e.
g., some foreign insitutions or market makers) is likely the source of this
price elasticity.

Second, when engaging in counterfactual exercises, the literature
assumes the supply of shares (total shares outstanding) is exogenously
determined (for example, Koijen and Yogo, 2019). We show that firms’
net issuance decisions are different when their forecasted returns vary,
and thus, the supply of shares would likely also change in constructed
counterfactuals—lending support for the premise behind the recent in-
vestigations of strategic share issuance (Tamburelli, 2024, and Sammon
and Shim, 2024).

1. Sample and data

1.1. Trading overview

Our trading measures are calculated over frequencies of 1-quarter, 1-
year, and 3-years. Our trading measures reflect changes in ownership
over each horizon. The participants we consider are retail investors,
firms, short sellers, and 6 types of institutions that report their holdings
on form 13F. Given that our variables are constructed over horizons of 1
quarter or longer, they do not reveal potentially informed intra-quarter
trading, such as in Puckett and Yan (2011) and Kacpercyzk, Sialm, and
Zhang (2008).

1.2. Retail trading

We estimate retail trades via the methodology developed in Boehmer
et al. (2021), which identifies marketable orders from retail investors,
and sign those trades using contemporaneous quote midpoint prices as
suggested by Barber et al. (2024). Boehmer et al. (2021) show that due
to the modern characteristics of market structure and Regulation NMS
(National Market System) rules, one can identify retail orders based on
the sub-penny pricing of the execution. Retail marketable orders are
likely to be internalized and receive sub-penny price improvement
resulting in transaction prices that rarely occur for other investors. Thus,
as Boehmer et al. (2021) outline, we calculate the fraction of the penny

Pit is the transaction price for the stock. Trades reported to FINRA TRF

we do not classify trades with Zit in the range of (0.4,0.6) as retail trades
since some advanced order types, such as pegged orders, can result in
transaction prices at or near half pennies that do not involve retail
traders.4

To sign trades, we compare the transaction price to the contempo-
raneous quote midpoint constructed from TAQ data. Transactions that
occur at prices below the midpoint of the national best bid and ask are
signed as retail sales and transactions that occur at prices above the
midpoint are signed as retail buys. Barber et al. (2024) find that the use
of quotes data results in more accurate classification of trade direction.
We diverge from Boehmer et al. (2021) in how we aggregate buys
and sells from retail traders to form our retail trading measure, although
for comparison we also report results with their weekly trade imbalance
measure. We calculate the daily percent of equity purchased by retail
traders as (retail buys – retail sells) / shares outstanding as reported by
CRSP. We then aggregate this measure to periods ranging from 3 months
to 3 years. We choose to scale net retail buying volume by shares
outstanding because we believe a measure of the percent of equity
purchased by retailers will be a better proxy for how much they over-
weight or underweight stocks and thus their exposure to the anomaly
characteristics we include in this study. This scaling also facilitates
direct comparisons to the trading by the other market participants
(described below), which are also scaled by shares outstanding.

In order to construct our retail trading variable, we require that the
stock must have at least one retail-initiated trade every month during
the relevant period. This ensures that the stock was actively traded and
was not newly listed or temporarily delisted. The identification of retail
trade relies on Regulation NMS, so we restrict our sample to October
2006 through December 2021. We exclude stocks with prices under $1,
measured one month before the anomaly portfolios are constructed.
Such low-priced stocks are often excluded in anomaly studies. Lastly, we
restrict our sample to common stocks with share code 10 or 11 listed on
the NYSE, NYSE MKT (formerly Amex), or NASDAQ.

Our retail trading variables does not caputre retail limit orders, as
these are not internalized. There also may be retail market orders that
are not internalized. As such, we are aggregating a subset of the popu-
lation of retail trades, and the resulting variable may be noisier than the
institutional trading variables. That stated, Boehmer et al. (2021) vali-
date this methodology using actual retail trade data from both Kelley
and Tetlock (2013) and NASDAQ, and find that this retail trading esti-
mate is highly correlated with actual retail trades.

Our retail trading measure was affected by the SEC’s tick-size pilot
program, which ran from 2016 to September 2018. The program
affected tick sizes for some listings. Stock listings in the program were
divided into one control group and three test groups. Participation in the
control group and one of the test groups does not affect our retail trading
measure. Participation in two of the test groups will sometimes affect
our measure. During the program subsample, this affects 16.4 % of our
firm-month observations; during our entire sample, it affects 1.7 % of
our firm-month observations. Miscalculation induces an errors-in-
variables problem, so data from the tick program will produce test sta-
tistics biased towards not rejecting the null. In untabulated results, we
find that omitting data from the pilot program time period does not
affect our results.

1.3. Institutional trading

We obtain institutional holdings data from quarterly SEC 13F and
S12 data, and use these data to estimate our insitutional trading vari-
ables. Some readers have asked why we do not use the residual of 13F to
estimate retail trading at quarterly intervals. The reason is that the re-
sidual of 13F contains more than just retail holdings. U.S. institutions
that manage less than $100 million in 13F securities are not required to
file form 13F. Foreign institutions are required to file 13F if they both
pass the $100 million threshold and “use any means or instrumentality
of United States interstate commerce in the course of their business.”5
French (2008) reports that according to Fed Flow of Funds data, foreign
institutions own 16.3 % of U.S. equities, while 13Fs reflect foreign
institutional ownership of 7.6 %, so the majority of foreign institutional
holdings are not reflected in 13Fs. Market makers do not have to file
Form 13F. Non-profits that self-direct their portfolios also do not have
file 13F. Some institutions apply for SEC exemption from disclosing
some profitable positions (Agarwal, Jiang, Tang, and Yang, 2013 Ara-
gon, Hertzel, and Shi, 2013), so these positions are also not reflected in
13F. For these reasons, the residual of 13F holdings does not equate to
retail holdings.

We estimate mutual fund, bank, insurance, wealth management,
hedge fund, and “other” (unclassified) institutional
trading using
changes in institutional holdings reported in 13F filings.6 We utilize 13F
filings documented by Thomson Reuters and supplement them with SEC
13F filings in order to correct known issues with Thomson Reuters data
in the later parts of our sample. We use the following methods to classify
institutions into one of six types:

• To identify mutual fund institutions, we merge mutual fund holdings
reported in S12 filings and documented by Thomson Reuters with
13F filings. We classify the number of shares reported by mutual
funds as shares held by mutual fund institutions.

• We identify banks and insurance companies using type codes pro-
vided by Brain Bushee.7 The holdings that are denoted as bank
holdings are typically from trust accounts that are managed by a
financial advisor.

• If an institution is not a bank or insurance company and does not
have any mutual funds, we classify them as either a wealth manager
or a hedge fund using text criteria based on institution names.8
• Any remaining institutions are classified as “Other” institutions.

Regarding holdings classified as “Other,” these holdings appear to be
directed by large investment banks that do not have commercial banking
operations. We expect that most of these shares are held in separate
accounts or collective investment trusts (CIT). Separate accounts are
non-comingled managed accounts whereas CITs are commingled. CITs
have the appearance of a mutual fund and are often used in workplace
retirement plans. Some of the holdings classified as “Other” may reflect
proprietary trading.

1.4. Short sellers

Stocks exchanges report end-of-month short interest. We retrieve this
information from Compustat. As previously noted, Boehmer, Jones, and
Zhang (2008) document that the majority of short positions are held by
hedge funds. We calculate Short Seller Trading as changes in short in-
terest scaled by shares outstanding.10 We sign this variable such that
increases in short interest result in negative values of Short Seller Trading
and decreases in short interest (net closing of short positions) result in
positive values of Short Seller Trading. Table 1 shows that the mean of the
3-month, 1-year, and 3-year Short Seller Trading variables are (cid:0) 0.03 %,
(cid:0) 0.08 %, and 0.00 %, respectively. Thus, in our sample, aggregate short
interest increased mildly.

1.5. Firm trading

Firm trading is measured as the percentage change in the firm’s
shares outstanding (adjusted for splits and stock dividends). This follows
the method in Pontiff and Woodgate (2008) and McLean, Pontiff, and
Watanabe (2009). We scale the change in shares (share issues minus
share repurchases) by shares outstanding, and sign this variable such
that positive values of Firm Trading indicate a reduction in shares
outstanding, i.e., a firm buying back its shares. We create this variable
each month using the CRSP reported shares outstanding adjusted for
splits and stock dividends. Similar to our institutional trading variables,
shares outstanding data may only substantively update on a quarterly
basis, when firms release financial reports regarding the completion of
share repurchases. Table 1 shows that the mean of 3-month, 1-year and
3-year Firm Trading variables are (cid:0) 8.99 %, (cid:0) 20.62 % and (cid:0) 2.44 %
respectively. Thus, in our sample, the average firm issued more shares
than it repurchased (although larger firms may have been net repurch-
asers, as has been reported in the media).

1.7. Stock return anomalies

Our set of characteristics comes from openassetpricing.com, which is
described in Chen and Zimmerman (2022).11 We start by considering all
207 characteristics that Chen and Zimmerman classify as “clear” or
“likely” based on the predictability reported in the original paper. We

11 We downloaded version V1.2.0 of the data.

omit characteristics that are mechanically or closely related to our
trader categories, which include short interest, institutional ownership,
and share issuance. These omissions reduce our number of characteris-
tics to 193. Of these, 164 are continuous variables, while 29 are discrete.
Most of the discrete variables are indicators, such as whether a firm has
omitted its dividend (Michaely, Thaler and Womack, 1995), although
some are counts, such as Dichev’s O-score (1998).

We closely follow Freyberger, Neuhierl, and Weber (2020) and esti-
mate out-of-sample returns using an adaptive group LASSO (least absolute
shrinkage and selection operator) procedure that allows for characteristic
nonlinearities. LASSO estimation is well-suited for out-of-sample estima-
tion since, unlike ordinary least squares, estimation considers a penalty
parameter that restricts the inclusion of characteristics, thus reducing
specification overfit. As such, a large set of initial characteristics can be
considered. Freyberger et al. estimate their model on 62 characteristics in
a sample period from 1965 to June 2014 and demonstrate that this
methodology results in strong cross-sectional predictability.

Following Freyberger et al., we utilize monthly stock returns in
excess of the CRSP equal-weighted index return as our dependent vari-
able. We exclude firms that do not have a CRSP share code of either 10 or
11 and firms with stock prices less than $1. We utilize a rolling LASSO
estimation that is updated every December. The LASSO is estimated
using 10 years of monthly data, and the estimates are used to predict
returns in the 12 following months. For continuous characteristics, we
assign it the value of its monthly, cross-sectional, percentile rank. This
reduces estimation error from outliers. We include observations with
missing data by including shadow variables for each original variable
that are equal to one if the original variable is missing and zero other-
wise. If a continuous variable is missing, we assign it a value of 0.5. If a
discrete variable is missing, we assign it value of zero. Although these
shadow variables are highly colinear, this is not a concern since LASSO
estimation is well-suited to handle collinearity. Like Freyberger et al.,
the penalty or regularization parameter choice minimizes the Bayesian
information criterion (BIC).

All the independent variables in our LASSOs are additive, although
the “adaptive group” nature of the LASSO is used to model the 193
continuous variables as non-linear functions. This is done by using
quadradic splines to estimate seven parameters for each continuous
variable. The resulting function is continuous and differentiable. For the
continuous variables, the LASSO selects all seven of the parameters or
none, in which case the continuous variable is not included.

We use rolling LASSOs to estimate expected monthly returns from
2006 to 2021. Appendix Table 1 reports statistics on which predictors
are selected using the LASSO including a list of most frequently selected
predictors. The average (median) specification selects 18.8 (19) pre-
dictors. In the cases of continuous variables, a “predictor” is a group of 7
quadratic splines. Ignoring discrete predictors that are selected, the
LASSO selects, on average, 14.9 (15.5) continuous predictor groups.

In Table 3, we sort stocks each month into forecasted-return quin-
tiles. We report the average expected returns for each quintile at time t,
and for the three years before and after time t. One takeaway from
Table 3 is that most of the heterogeneity described by the expected-
return measure resides in the extreme quintiles. Moving from the low
to high forecasted-return quintiles, the average forecasted-return values
are (cid:0) 169.6, (cid:0) 63.7, (cid:0) 19.4, 28.6, and 135.8. So, there are some differ-
ences in values among quintiles 2, 3, and 4 and a large difference be-
tween quintiles 1 and 5.

Table 3 shows that forecasted returns are highly persistent in all
quintiles. The stocks in the low forecasted-return quintile have the
lowest forecasted returns three years before and three years after the
forecasted returns measurement year. Similarly, the stocks in the high
forecasted-return quintile have the highest forecasted returns three
years before and three years after the forecasted returns measurement
year. This isexpected, as we estimate monthly forecasted returns each
year using rolling 10-year periods, so there are nine years of overlap for
estimations one year apart.

2. Main findings

2.1. Trading before anomaly portfolio formation

In this section, we ask how each market participant trades prior to
stocks being assigned to anomaly portfolios. If a stock has high

forecasted returns at time t, the time of portfolio formation, which
participants increase or decrease their ownership of the stock before
time t? We answer this question in Table 4. Panel A studies trading one
year before time t, whereas Panel B studies trading three years before
time t. As explained in the previous section, the trading variables are
changes in ownership scaled by shares outstanding, i.e., buys minus sells
scaled by shares outstanding. In Panel C, we consider the weekly trade
imbalance measure from Boehmer et al. (2021). This variable is
measured as buys minus sells divided by buys plus sells, all measured
over the 5 trading days preceding time t.

The findings in Table 4 show that retail investors tend to do the worst
with respect to anomalies, as they build positions in eventual low-
forecasted-return stocks and reduce holdings
in eventual high-
forecasted-return stocks. Short sellers and firms do the best. Short
sellers increase short interest in eventual low forecasted-return stocks
and reduce short interest in eventual high-forecasted return stocks.

Firms are net issuers of all types of stock; however, firms with the
eventual low-forecasted returns issue the most shares. Firms are unlike
the other trading groups, as they may need to raise capital to operate.
The various long-side institutional investors tend to trade against
forecasted returns, but the results are usually insignificant. Hedge funds
have the strongest significance, with t-statistics of (cid:0) 1.9 and (cid:0) 1.4 at the
1-year and 3-year horizons, respectively. Overall, the results suggest that
firms and short sellers are the smart money, while retail investors put
themselves on the wrong side of forecasted returns.

Examining the results in more detail, Panel A shows that, in the year
investors’ value in the
before anomaly portfolio formation, retail
anomaly-short portfolio is 1.24 %, whereas the value in the anomaly-
long portfolio is 0.01 %. The difference between these two values is
statistically significant. So retail investors are overall net buyers, but
more so in stocks with low forecasted returns.

Although we do not estimate the elasticity of investors to price or
stock characteristics, in equilibria of demand-based models, elastic in-
vestors should be overweight underpriced stocks and underweight
overpriced stocks. Thus, our results suggest that firms and short sellers
are relatively price elastic and accommodate preferences for stock
characteristics or latent demand of retail traders. Importantly, existing
demand-based models (e.g. Koijen, Richmond, and Yogo, 2023 and Li,
Sokolinksi and Tamoni, 2023) often treat the supply of shares as exog-
enous and thus neither model nor estimate this elasticity in firms’ net
issuance decisions.

The only large difference between Panels A and B is with other

investors trade in the direction of forecasted returns. The effect is sta-

in Panels A and B show that during the year before the return forecast,
other institutions’ trades are not aligned with forecasted returns, but if
we look during the two years before that, they are tilting their portfolios
toward stocks that will eventually have higher forecasted returns.

Banks, insurance companies, wealth managers, and hedge funds all
trade contrary to forecasted returns, as they do in Panel A, although
none of the effects in Panel B are significant. Mutual funds trade in the
direction of forecasted returns, but the effect is small and insignificant.
In aggregate, institutional investors’ trades are not significantly
related to forecasted returns. In Appendix Table 9, we find that aggre-
gate institutions are larger net purchasers of stocks with low forecasted
returns than stocks with forecasted returns in the first half of our sample,
consistent with the findings of Edelen, Ince, and Kadlec (2016).12 Spe-
cifically, for almost all samples ending in 2014 or prior, aggregate in-
stitutions’ 3-year net purchases in low-forecasted return stocks is
significantly larger than in stocks with high-forecasted returns.

The change in institutional trading behavior in the later part of our
sample is consistent with a couple of effects. It may be that institutional
trading behavior changed over time. Calluzzo, Moneta, and Topaloglu
(2019), find that after an anomaly is featured in an academic publica-
tion, more active institutions make trades consistent with the anomaly’s
forecasted returns. More of the 193 characteristics in our sample are
post-publication in the latter half of our sample.

It is also the case that there has been a weakening of anomaly return-
predictability over time, as documented in Chordia, Subrahmanyam,
and Tong (2014) and McLean and Pontiff (2016). Consistent with this
effect, the LASSO procedure chooses fewer characteristics in the later
years of our sample period. Thus, institutions’ trading behavior may not
have changed relative to characteristics, but several characteristics
became less important for our forecasted return variable.

2.2. Regression evidence with individual predictors

In Table 5, we continue to study how each market participant trades

In Panel A of Table 5, the dependent variable is trading measured
over the last year. Future anomaly indicators explain significant trading
for firms and retail investors. The within-effect R2 is 21.67 % for retail
investors and 25.85 % for firms. For short sellers, the statistic is 7.22 %.
Table 4 shows that retail investors trade against the predictors, whereas
firms and short sellers trade with the predictors. The results here show
that cross-sectional trading decisions are mostly idiosyncratic. Similarly,
the R2 from regressions of stock returns on the indicators are typically
under 10 %, so stock returns are also mostly idiosyncratic.

s for banks

short sellers than institutional investors.

Panel B reports the results using trading over the last 3 years. The R2
statistics are larger as compared to those in Panel A. For firms, the R2

with balanced or little retail trading suggests that anomaly strategies
may identify mispricing generated by retail investors. This effect is
particularly strong in stocks purchased by retail traders. Taken in their
entirety, the results suggest that the predictability of anomalies is related
to aggregated retail trading, especially over the 3-year horizon, but not
trades from the other types of investors.

3. Conclusions

We examine how the trades of retail investors, institutional investors,
short sellers, and firms relate to stock return anomalies and future stock
returns. We find that firms and short sellers are the smart money. Both
firms and short sellers tend to trade in the direction of forecasted
returns, as both heavily sell anomaly-shorts but not anomaly-longs. Like
earlier studies, we find that trades of both firms and short sellers predict
returns in the intended direction. We further show that they do so more
strongly in stocks with either high- or low-forecasted returns; the pre-
dictability of both groups is weaker or insignificant when forecasted
returns are more neutral.

Like earlier studies, we find that retail and institution trades predict
returns in the unintended direction. We show that this is driven by trades

in stocks with high- or low-forecasted returns. In contrast, weekly retail
trade imbalances predict returns in the intended direction. They do so
robustly across different types of stocks, except for those with neutral-
forecasted returns.

Our forecasted-return variable, which is based on 193 characteris-
tics, predicts returns robustly across groups of stocks based on trading by
the various market participants. The one exception is with retail in-
vestors over the 3-year horizon. The forecasted-return variable predicts
returns more strongly in groups of stocks with heavy retail buying and,
to a lesser extent, retail selling. This suggests that retail trading exac-
erbates mispricing that is captured by the predictors.

Panel A: Trading Results with Hedge Fund Long-Short Combined Positions

This table reports results trading and return results for Hedge Fund Long and Short Trading. Panel A reports Hedge Fund Long and Short Trading over 1 (3) year(s) prior to quintile sorts of
Forecasted Return and 1 quarter after quintile sorts of Forecasted Return. Hedge Fund Long and Short Trading is the simple sum of Hedge Fund Long-Position Trading and Short Seller Trading
(the negative change in short interest / shares outstanding). We use 193 cross-sectional anomalies described in Chen and Zimmerman (2021). 29 anomalies are discrete variables (e.g.,
credit rating downgrades). We exclude anomalies based on 13F data, short interest and share issuances since they are used for the construction of our institutional trading, Short Seller
Trading and Firm Trading measures. Using a rolling window of 10 years, we use an adaptive group LASSO described by Freyberger et al. (2020) to select a model to utilize when creating
Forecasted Return. The adaptive group LASSO allows for a nonlinear model to create Forecasted Return as quadratic splines are included for continuous anomaly variables. Newey-West
standard errors with 12(36) lags are utilized for the t-statistics in parentheses. Panels B and C report results from a Fama-Macbeth regression of monthly Returns or Anomaly Residual
stock returns on the various trading variables. Anomaly Residual the difference between Return and Forecasted Return. These residuals represent the monthly return not explained by a
equity’s anomaly characteristics using the model selected by the adaptive group LASSO. Retail Trading is the percentage of common equity net purchased by retail traders during the
relevant time period (.01 = 1% of common equity). Retail net buying is constructed by summing the daily percentage of common equity purchased by retail traders for the relevant
period. Daily percentage of equity purchased by retail traders is calculated as (retail buyer initiated - retail seller initiated) / shares outstanding. Retail trades are identified by sub-
penny pricing as described by Boehmer et al. (2021) and signed using the midpoint of contemporaneous quotes as suggested by Barber et al. (2023). Mutual Fund Trading, Bank Trading,
Insurance Company Trading, Wealth Management Trading, and Other Institutional Trading are calculated as the changes in categorized 13F reported holdings between the most recent filing
and the filing 1 (3) years prior to the most recent filing. Firm Trading is calculated as the negative change in shares outstanding / beginning of period shares outstanding. Thus, a positive
value of Firm Trading indicates a decrease in the shares outstanding and vice versa. All trading variables are winsorized at the 1% level. Weekly order imbalance is calculated as the
average of (retail buyer initiated - retail seller initiated) / (retail buyer initiated + retail seller initiated) for the last five trading days of the month. Observations with missing
institutional or retail data are given a value of zero and a missing data indicator variable is included as a control in the regressions. Newey-West standard errors are utilized for the t-
statistics in parentheses. The sample period is from 183 months 2006:10 to 2021:12. *, **, and *** indicate significance at the 10%, 5% and 1% respectively.

Appendix 4
Returns Following 1-Year Trading Variables
This table reports results from a Fama-Macbeth regression of monthly stock returns on the Forecasted Return anomaly indicator, Retail Trading, Mutual Fund Trading,
Bank Trading, Insurance Company Trading, Wealth Management Trading, Hedge Fund Long-Position Trading, Other Institutional Trading, Short Seller Trading and Firm Trading
aggregated through the 1 year prior to the month of the anomaly stock sorts, log(Price) at the month of the anomaly stock sorts, and log(Size) as measured by the log of
the CRSP reported market capitalization of common equity at the month of the anomaly stock sorts. Monthly Returns are reported by CRSP and denoted as basis points.
Retail Trading is the percentage of common equity net purchased by retail traders during the relevant time period (.01 = 1% of common equity). Retail net buying is
constructed by summing the daily percentage of common equity purchased by retail traders for the relevant period. Daily percentage of equity purchased by retail
traders is calculated as (retail buyer initiated - retail seller initiated) / shares outstanding. Retail trades are identified by sub-penny pricing as described by Boehmer
et al. (2021) and signed using the midpoint of contemporaneous quotes as suggested by Barber et al. (2023). Mutual Fund Trading, Bank Trading, Insurance Company
Trading, Wealth Management Trading, Hedge Fund Long-Position Trading, and Other Institutional Trading are calculated as the changes in categorized 13F reported holdings
between the most recent filing and the filing 1 year prior to the most recent filing. Short Seller Trading is calculated as the negative change in short interest / shares
outstanding. Thus, a positive value of Short Seller Trading indicates a decrease in the short interest and vice versa. Firm Trading is calculated as the negative change in
shares outstanding / beginning of period shares outstanding. Thus, a positive value of Firm Trading indicates a decrease in the shares outstanding and vice versa. All
trading variables are winsorized at the 1% level. Weekly order imbalance is calculated as the average of (retail buyer initiated - retail seller initiated) / (retail buyer
initiated + retail seller initiated) for the last five trading days of the month. We use 193 cross-sectional anomalies described in Chen and Zimmerman (2021). 29
anomalies are discrete variables (e.g., credit rating downgrades). We exclude anomalies based on 13F data, short interest and share issuances since they are used for the
construction of our institutional trading, Short Seller Trading and Firm Trading measures. Using a rolling window of 10 years, we use an adaptive group LASSO described
by Freyberger et al. (2020) to select a model to utilize when creating Forecasted Return. The adaptive group LASSO allows for a nonlinear model to create Forecasted
Return as quadratic splines are included for continuous anomaly variables. Observations with missing institutional or retail data are given a value of zero and a missing
data indicator variable is included as a control in the regressions. Newey-West standard errors are utilized for the t-statistics in parentheses. The sample period is 183
months from 2006:10 to 2021:12. The number of observations ranges from 1,252,672 to 1,265,195. *, **, and *** indicate significance at the 10%, 5% and 1%
respectively.

Appendix 7
Returns Following 3-Year Trading Variables
Results from a Fama-Macbeth regression of monthly returns on the Forecasted Return anomaly indicator, Retail Trading, Mutual Fund Trading, Bank Trading, Insurance
Company Trading, Wealth Management Trading, Hedge Fund Long-Position Trading, Other Institutional Trading, Short Seller Trading, and Firm Trading aggregated through
the 3 years prior to the month of the anomaly stock sorts, log(Price) at the month of the anomaly stock sorts, and log(Size) as measured by the log of the CRSP reported
market capitalization of common equity at the month of the anomaly stock sorts. Monthly Returns are reported by CRSP and denoted as basis points. Retail Trading is
the percentage of common equity net purchased by retail traders during the relevant time period (.01 = 1% of common equity). Retail net buying is constructed by
summing the daily percentage of common equity purchased by retail traders for the relevant period. Daily percentage of equity purchased by retail traders is calculated
as (retail buyer initiated - retail seller initiated) / shares outstanding. Retail trades are identified by sub-penny pricing as described by Boehmer et al. (2021) and signed
using the midpoint of contemporaneous quotes as suggested by Barber et al. (2023). Mutual Fund Trading, Bank Trading, Insurance Company Trading, Wealth Management
Trading, Hedge Fund Long-Position Trading, and Other Institutional Trading are calculated as the changes in categorized 13F reported holdings between the most recent
filing and the filing 3 years prior to the most recent filing. Short Seller Trading is calculated as the negative change in short interest / shares outstanding. Thus, a positive
value of Short Seller Trading indicates a decrease in the short interest and vice versa. Firm Trading is calculated as the negative change in shares outstanding / beginning
of period shares outstanding. Thus, a positive value of Firm Trading indicates a decrease in the shares outstanding and vice versa. All trading variables are winsorized at
the 1% level. Weekly order imbalance is calculated as the average of (retail buyer initiated - retail seller initiated) / (retail buyer initiated + retail seller initiated) for
the last five trading days of the month. Observations with missing institutional or retail data are given a value of zero and a missing data indicator variable is included
as a control in the regressions. We use 193 cross-sectional anomalies described in Chen and Zimmerman (2021). 29 anomalies are discrete variables (e.g., credit rating
downgrades). We exclude anomalies based on 13F data, short interest and share issuances since they are used for the construction of our institutional trading, Short
Seller Trading and Firm Trading measures. Using a rolling window of 10 years, we use an adaptive group LASSO described by Freyberger et al. (2020) to select a model to
utilize when creating Forecasted Return. The adaptive group LASSO allows for a nonlinear model to create Forecasted Return as quadratic splines are included for
continuous anomaly variables. Newey-West standard errors are utilized for the t-statistics in parentheses. The sample period is 183 months from 2006:10 to 2021:12.
The number of observations ranges from 1,252,672 to 1,265,195. *, **, and *** indicate significance at the 10%, 5% and 1% respectively.
