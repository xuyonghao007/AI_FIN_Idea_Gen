A R T I C L E I N F O

A B S T R A C T

Investment-based asset pricing models typically predict a close link between a firm’s stock return and its
characteristics at any point in time. Yet, previous studies have primarily focused on the weaker prediction
that this link holds on average, finding substantial empirical support. We show how to incorporate the time-
series predictions in the estimation and testing of investment-based models using the generalized method of
moments. We find that standard specifications of investment-based models with one physical capital input fail
to match the time series properties of stock returns in the data, and discuss the implications of the findings
for future research.

1. Introduction

The neoclassical investment-based asset pricing model predicts that
a firm’s equilibrium stock return is closely related to its characteristics
at any point in time (Cochrane 1991, Restoy and Rockinger 1994).
Despite this strong time-series prediction, most of the structural work
in investment-based asset pricing to date, tests the model by the gener-
alized methods of moments (GMM) using the weaker prediction that a
firm’s stock return and its characteristics should be related on average.
Following this procedure, Liu et al. (2009) (henceforth LWZ) shows that
a standard neoclassical investment-based model with one (physical)
capital input and homogeneous of degree one technology matches
surprisingly well the cross-sectional dispersion in average stock returns
of several portfolio sorts. In this paper, we show how to incorporate
the stronger time-series predictions in the estimation and testing of
investment-based models using GMM. We find that standard specifica-
tions of the investment-based model with one (physical) capital input,
including LWZ, fail to capture the time-series properties of stock returns

in the data, and we discuss the implications of the findings for future
research.

We first apply our estimation approach to the standard specification
of the investment-based model with one (physical) capital input and
homogeneous of degree one technology, as the one examined in LWZ.
This model predicts that a firm’s realized stock return should be equal
to its (levered) realized investment return, which is a function of
firm-characteristics such as investment rate and output. As in a stan-
dard nonlinear least squares estimation approach, we add the average
squared distance between stock returns and model-implied investment
returns at each point in time as a new set of target moment conditions
in the GMM estimation of the model, which we label as time-series
moments. In addition, as in previous work, we also consider cross-
sectional moments, that is, the model’s prediction that stock returns
and model-implied investment returns should be equal on average. We
follow LWZ and estimate the baseline model at the portfolio-level, using
ten book-to-market portfolios, to reduce the impact of estimation noise.

The estimation targets twenty moments: ten cross-sectional moments
as in previous studies, and our novel ten time-series moments. To
understand the role of the two set of moments on the results, we
investigate how the estimates and model fit change when we vary
the relative weight of the two set of moments in the GMM weighting
matrix.

The estimation results of the baseline investment-based model can
be summarized as follows. First, consistent with LWZ, when we only
use cross-sectional moments in the GMM estimation, the model matches
these cross-sectional moments very well, with low cross-sectional pric-
ing errors (about 1.4% per year) and a high cross-sectional 𝑅2 (hence-
forth 𝑋𝑆 − 𝑅2) of the scatter plot of average portfolio-level stock
returns against average portfolio-level estimated investment returns,
of 71%. The time-series fit of model is very poor, however, with a
negative time-series 𝑅2 (henceforth 𝑇 𝑆 − 𝑅2), measured as the time-
series 𝑅2 of a linear projection of the realized stock return against the
estimated investment return, of −93% across portfolios. We conclude
that when estimating the baseline investment-based model using the
cross-sectional moments only, the model fails to capture its time series
implications.

Second, our estimation approach uncovers a novel trade-off between
cross-sectional fit and time-series fit in the baseline investment-based
model: the model cannot fit both sets of moments simultaneously. As
we increase the relative weight of the time-series moments in the GMM
estimation of the model to improve its time-series fit, the 𝑋𝑆 − 𝑅2
deteriorates significantly from 71% when only cross-sectional moments
are used in the estimation, to −134% when only time-series moments
are used in the estimation. As expected, the time-series fit of the model
improves when the weight of the time-series moments increases. How-
ever, and perhaps surprisingly, the model’s time-series fit remains poor
even when only the time-series moments are used in the estimation,
with a 𝑇 𝑆 − 𝑅2 of −4%. That is, the baseline investment-based model is
unable to capture the time-series behavior of stock returns in the data
even when the estimation is designed to maximize its time series fit.

We then investigate two potential concerns with the standard em-
pirical procedures and with the data that might be responsible for the
poor time-series fit of the baseline investment-based model. First, we
investigate the role of possible issues in the portfolio aggregation for the
results. As noted in Belo et al. (2022) (henceforth BGSV), and Gonçalves
et al. (2020) (henceforth GXZ), the portfolio-level aggregation proce-
dure in LWZ suffers from an aggregation bias (we discuss the source
of this aggregation bias in detail in Section 6.1). Following BGSV and
GXZ, we construct bias-free portfolio-level investment returns. Using
simulated data from a calibrated version of the baseline investment-
based model, we confirm that, in theory, the 𝑇 𝑆 − 𝑅2 should be perfect
when the portfolio-level investment returns are properly aggregated,
and that the LWZ biased portfolio-level aggregation does lead to very
low 𝑇 𝑆 − 𝑅2. However, when we re-estimate the model in the real data
with a proper bias-free portfolio-level aggregation, the fit of the model
in the time series remains very poor, 𝑇 𝑆 − 𝑅2 = −17%, even when
the estimation focuses only on the time-series moments to maximize
its time-series fit. Thus, the aggregation bias in LWZ does not appear
to be the main cause for the inability of the baseline investment-based
model to capture the time series behavior of stock returns in the data.
Second, we address the concern that asset prices and quantity
(e.g. investment) data might be misaligned. For example, stock prices
(and hence stock returns) might respond instantaneously to aggregate
shocks, whereas investment might take more time to adjust, in which
case investment returns lag stock returns (see, for example, Lamont
2000 for a more formal analysis of this issue). To address this concern,
we investigate the time-series fit of the model using time-smoothed
data, that is, (annualized) 5-year compounded returns. If the misalign-
ment in the data is relatively short lived, the misalignment between
stock and investment returns should be less pronounced at longer-
horizons. We provide support for this conjecture using again simulated
data from a calibrated version of the baseline investment-based model

in which we parameterize the data misalignment and show that, in the-
ory, the 𝑇 𝑆−𝑅2 is indeed significantly higher when we use (annualized)
compounded returns in the presence of data misalignment. However, in
the real data, the 𝑇 𝑆 − 𝑅2 using (annualized) 5-year compounded stock
and investment returns remains negative, 𝑇 𝑆 − 𝑅2 = −11%. Thus, the
misalignment in asset prices and real quantity data also does not appear
to be the main cause for the inability of the baseline investment-based
model to capture the time series behavior of stock returns in the data.
Finally, we show how to extend our approach to evaluate the
performance of a larger class of investment-based models in which the
stock-investment return equality might not hold. In general, it is natural
to expect investment-based models to imply a close link between a
firm’s equilibrium stock return and its characteristics. The strength
of this link can be estimated in any model by running simple time-
series and cross-sectional linear regressions of stock returns on firm
characteristics using simulated data. We can then compare these results
with the corresponding estimation results in the real data to evaluate
the model’s fit.

We apply this flexible evaluation approach of investment-based
models to two alternative model specifications with one (physical)
capital input in which the stock-investment return equality does not
hold. We consider the Lin and Zhang (2013) investment-based model
with decreasing returns to scale, non convex and asymmetric adjust-
ment costs, and operating fixed costs, and the Kogan and Papanikolaou
(2013) non-adjustment-cost model with investment-specific shocks. Us-
ing simulated data aggregated at the portfolio-level, we document that
these models also imply a strong (linear) relationship between equilib-
rium stock returns and firm-characteristics (here, marginal product of
physical capital, firm size, and the current and lagged physical capital
investment rates), both in the cross-section (𝑋𝑆 − 𝑅2 above 96%) and
in the time-series (𝑇 𝑆 − 𝑅2 above 84%). When applied to the real data,
however, the estimated time-series relationship between realized stock
returns and the same firm characteristics is weak (𝑇 𝑆 − 𝑅2 around
5%). Thus, the set of firm characteristics driving stock returns in these
investment-based models with one (physical) capital input differs from
those in the real data, indicating that important firm-characteristics
might be missing from these models.

Taken together, our findings highlight the importance of examining
the time-series implications of investment-based models for proper
model evaluation. We show that although standard specifications of
investment-based asset pricing models with one (physical) capital input
can match the cross section of average stock return quite well, these
models struggle to match the time series of stock returns observed in
the data.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 discusses the
related literature. Section 3 presents the neoclassical investment-based
model. Section 4 describes the econometric methodology. Section 5
reports the estimation and tests results of the model. Section 6 investi-
gates potential empirical reasons for the poor time-series fit. Section 7
proposes an extension of the approach that can be used to evaluate
more general specifications of investment-based models in which the
stock-investment return equality does not hold. Finally, Section 8 con-
cludes and discusses the implications of our findings for future research

results is available online.

2. Related literature

Our work is closely related to LWZ who first estimate the baseline
neoclassical investment-based model on the cross-section of stock re-
turns. Different from LWZ, our estimation procedure requires the model
to also match the time series of the observed stock returns as close
as possible (in the same spirit, BGSV requires the model to match the

2 

F. Belo et al.

time series of valuation ratios), and not just on average.1 LWZ use their
estimates to document that the implied stock and investment returns
have low correlation, which they label a correlation puzzle. Similarly,
the correlation puzzle is documented at the aggregate level in Kuehn
(2009). We show that this puzzle persists even when the estimation is
designed to maximize the time-series fit of the model. In addition, we
show how to incorporate the time series implications of investment-
based models directly in the estimation and evaluation of these models,
and we discuss potential alternative empirical reasons for the observed
poor time-series fit.

Li et al. (2021) estimate an investment-based model with two capital
inputs using firm-level data and Bayesian estimation methods. Different
from our work, they focus only on a specification of the investment-
based model in which the homogeneous of degree one assumption
holds, and they estimate the model to match firm-level stock returns.
In addition, and departing from baseline neoclassical investment-based
models with stable technologies, they estimate industry-specific and
time-varying technological parameters. They show that allowing for
time-varying parameters of the firm’s technology helps to significantly
improve the fit of the model in explaining the behavior of returns of
several anomaly portfolios.

GXZ document the aggregation bias in the original LWZ portfolio-
level aggregation approach (see also BGSV and Zhang, 2017, for earlier
discussions of this aggregation bias in LWZ). Our analysis shows that
the aggregation bias alone cannot explain the poor fit of the one
(physical) capital input investment-based model in the time series.

Delikouras and Dittmar

(2021) estimate and test

standard
investment-based models using GMM with cross-sectional moments
and investment Euler equations, which requires the specification of a
stochastic discount factor, and also find that the baseline investment-
based model is unable to match both sets of moments jointly. Our paper
shares the goal of testing investment-based models across a larger set
of model implications, but differs in the approach. Our analysis focuses
on the properties of the firm’s technology and does not require the
specification of a stochastic discount factor (at least for the estimation
of baseline model), thus avoiding the joint hypothesis testing problem.
Naturally, the ultimate goal
is to obtain a specification of firm’s
technology and of the stochastic discount factor that simultaneously
matches the cross-sectional, the time-series, and the investment Euler
equations as in Delikouras and Dittmar (2021). However, focusing only
on the properties of the firm’s technology allows us to narrow down
how its specification alone affects the time-series and cross-sectional fit
of the model. Our paper thus complements their approach by providing
the first step (focus on firm’s technology) towards that goal.

Finally, our work is related to the literature on estimation and test-
ing of structural models in financial economics, and to the strand of the
investment-based asset pricing literature that links firm characteristics
to asset returns.2 We contribute to these literatures by improving the
econometric methodology for estimating and testing investment-based
models.

4.1. Portfolio approach

Eq. (7) establishes an exact relationship between a firm’s observed
stock return and its model-implied levered investment return at each
point in time. Using this equation with firm-level data to estimate
the model parameters can be challenging because the firm-level data
can be very noisy. Thus, we estimate the model parameters using
portfolio-level moments as in LWZ. The use of portfolio-level moments,
a common practice in the asset pricing literature, has several attractive
features in our context. First, it allows us to reduce the noise in the
firm-level data, and hence obtain more accurate parameter estimates
and measures of model fit such as 𝑅2. Second, portfolio-level moments
are less sensitive, and hence more stable, to firm entry and exit, and to
missing firm-level observations. Finally, the use of portfolio-level mo-
ments allows us link our approach to the large empirical asset pricing
literature, and to characterize the data in a more parsimonious manner
as the number of portfolios is naturally smaller than the number of
firms in the data.

should not deviate too much and the deviation should be random. The

affect the interpretation of the chi-square tests for assessing the validity

model fit on economic grounds, with less emphasis on the statistical
tests of the model. Finally, the use of NLLS objective function as the
moment condition has the benefit of being parsimonious and easy to
interpret.4

4.4. Model evaluation

We evaluate the fit of the investment-based model on economic
grounds, with less emphasis on the statistical tests. As discussed
in Cochrane (1991), the baseline investment-based model should be
rejected at any level of significance because the model predicts that
stock returns and levered investment returns should be equal at each
point in time without any error term, which is not possible to achieve in
the data. Nevertheless, if the model provides a reasonably description
of the real world, the investment return generated by a successful
candidate model should match well the behavior of the stock returns in
the real data, and this matching can be assessed with standard goodness
of fit measures.

Specifically, we investigate how well the model is able to capture
the average cross-sectional variation and the time-series variation of
stock returns across portfolios. Accordingly, our analyses focuses on
first stage GMM estimates. (In the online appendix we show that our
conclusions are similar if we use second stage GMM.) The evaluation
of the model is then based on the properties of easy-to-interpret good-
ness of fit measures such as the cross-sectional 𝑅2 (𝑋𝑆 − 𝑅2) of the

5. Estimation and tests results

Section 5.1 describes the data. Section 5.2 reports the estimation
results of the baseline investment-based model from matching the cross-
sectional moments only. Section 5.3 reports the estimation and tests
results from matching the cross-sectional and the time-series moments
jointly.

5.1. Data

To facilitate the comparison with prior studies, our sample construc-
tion and data alignment largely follows LWZ and GXZ. Our sample
consists of all common stocks traded on NYSE, Amex, and Nasdaq from
1963 to 2020. The firm-level data are from the Center for Research in
Security Prices (CRSP) monthly stock files and the annual Standard and
Poor’s Compustat files. We exclude firms in financial and utility sectors
and firms with non positive total assets, capital, or sales. We include
only firms with a fiscal year end in December to align the accounting
data across firms.7

5.1.1. Testing portfolios

As noted, we use ten book-to-market (BM) portfolios as the test
assets. Following Fama and French (1992), at the end of June of each
year 𝑡, we sort all stocks based on BM, which is defined as the book
equity for the fiscal year ending in calendar year 𝑡 − 1 divided by
the market equity (from CRSP) at the end of December of 𝑡 − 1, into
deciles based on the NYSE breakpoints. We calculate equal-weighted
annual returns from July of each year 𝑡 to June of year 𝑡 + 1 for
the portfolios, which are re-balanced at the end of each June. (In the
online appendix we show that our main conclusions are similar to those
reported here if we use portfolios sorted on standardized unexpected
earnings, corporate investment, or asset growth as test assets, as in
LWZ.)

5.3. Matching cross-sectional and time-series moments jointly

To improve the time-series fit of the model, we add the time-series

moments to the GMM estimation and re-evaluate the model fit.

Table 1 reports the results. As we increase the relative weight of the
time series moments in the estimation from column (1) (zero weight on
TS moments, all weight on XS moments) to column (8) (all weight on
TS moments, zero weight on XS moments), the fit on the cross-sectional
moments deteriorates significantly: the mean absolute cross-sectional
error (alpha) monotonically increases from 1.37% per annum (column

1), to 3.90% (column 8); the mean absolute high-minus-low cross-
sectional error increases from 2.61% per annum (column 1) to 15.38%
(column 8), and the 𝑋𝑆 − 𝑅2 decreases from 71% (column 1) to

is an improvement in the fit of the model in the time-series: the
mean absolute time series error (almost) monotonically decreases from
24.24% per annum (column 1) to 18.14% (column 8) and the 𝑇 𝑆 − 𝑅2
increases from −93% (column 1) to −4% (column 8). Thus, these results

6. Potential empirical reasons for the poor time series fit

We investigate if empirical issues, in particular, portfolio-level ag-
gregation bias and the potential misalignment between asset prices
and real quantity (e.g. investment) data, might be responsible for the
poor time series fit of the baseline model. As we show here through
simulations, these empirical issues can generate a poor time series fit
even if the baseline model is the true model. We propose solutions to
address these empirical issues which we then use to re-estimate the
baseline model in the real data.

We only investigate empirical issues here given the focus of the
paper on the estimation and testing methodology of investment-based

6.1. Aggregation bias

As noted in BGSV and GXZ, the portfolio aggregation procedure
in LWZ suffers from an aggregation bias because the portfolio-level
stock and investment returns, which the estimation tries to match,
are not computed in a consistent manner. As a result, the parameter
estimates do not have a structural interpretation and the equality
between portfolio-level stock and investment returns does not have
to hold. Specifically, as discussed in Section 4.1, the portfolio-level
investment return in LWZ is obtained by first computing each portfolio-
level characteristic separately (e.g., the portfolio-level investment rate,
marginal product of capital), and then plugging each of these char-
acteristics directly in the investment return formula to obtain the
portfolio-level investment return at each point in time. In turn, the
estimation matches this portfolio-level investment return to the equal-
weighted average of firm-level stock returns in the portfolio. Given
the non-linearity of the investment return formula, however, the LWZ
aggregation procedure breaks the equality between portfolio-level stock
and investment returns (unless all the firms in the portfolio have exactly
the same characteristics, which is counterfactual).

To properly estimate the model and recover the structural pa-
rameters, the portfolio-level stock and investment return should be
computed in the same manner, as in GXZ. Specifically, given the
nonlinear nature of investment returns, we first compute the investment
return for each firm (indexed by the subscript 𝑗), and then compute
the portfolio-level (indexed by the subscript 𝑖) investment return as
the equal-weighted average of the firm-level investment returns of the

the portfolio-level aggregation bias.

6.1.2. The impact of aggregation bias in real data

We re-estimate the baseline investment-based model in the real
data using the proper portfolio-level aggregation. Panel A in Table 4
reports the estimation results. Although the time-series fit of the model
improves from 𝑇 𝑆 − 𝑅2 = −93% to −21% when the estimation targets
the cross-sectional moments only (column 1), the time-series fit remains
overall very poor with the proper portfolio-level aggregation. Even
when the estimation targets the time-series moments only (column 5),
the 𝑇 𝑆 − 𝑅2 = −17%.

6.2. Misalignment between asset prices and quantity data

The poor time series fit of the baseline investment-based model
might also arise because of the potential misalignment between as-
set prices and quantity (e.g. investment) data. For example, stock
prices (and hence stock returns) might respond instantaneously to
aggregate shocks, whereas firm investment might take more time to
adjust, in which case investment returns lag stock returns (see, for
example, Lamont 2000, for a more formal analysis of this issue). But
if the misalignment in the data is relatively short lived, the mis-
alignment should be less relevant for longer-horizon returns (i.e., for
time-smoothed data).

6.2.1. The impact of data misalignment in simulated data

We use simulated data to test the conjecture that compounded
multi-year (here, using a five-year horizon) stock and investment re-
turns should be less affected by data misalignment issues than one year
horizon returns.

7. Testing a broader class of models

The analysis so far has been restricted to the specification of the
investment-based model in which the Hayashi (1982) conditions (op-
erating profit and adjustment cost functions with constant returns to
scale) holds. In this case, stock returns and (levered) investment returns
should be equal at each point in time, and we use this result to estimate
the model.10 Although these Hayashi conditions should be interpreted
as approximations, they are nevertheless strong assumptions that are
not satisfied by a large class of investment-based models. For example,
the Hayashi conditions, and hence the stock-investment return equal-
ity, might not hold in models with decreasing returns to scale, fixed
adjustment and operating costs, and time-to-build, which might limit
the scope of our approach. It is also natural to question the validity
of the constant returns to scale technology assumption given the poor
time-series fit of the baseline investment-based model in the data.

7.1. A flexible evaluation approach

Here, we propose a more general approach to evaluate the perfor-
mance of investment-based asset pricing models that can be used in
practice even when the potentially restrictive Hayashi conditions, and
hence the stock-investment return equality, do not hold. We label this
more general method as flexible evaluation approach because it does
not rely so heavily on functional form restrictions. As a result, this
approach is useful to test the asset pricing implications of a broader
set of specifications of the investment-based model.

In any investment-based model, it is natural to investigate the link
between a firm’s equilibrium stock return and its characteristics (anal-
ogous to the stock-investment return equality in the baseline model).
We can determine the strength of these links in the model and in the
real data using simple linear regressions of the form:

𝑟𝑆
𝑖𝑡 = 𝛽𝑋𝑖𝑡,

(21)

in which 𝑋𝑖 is a vector of firm (or portfolio-level) characteristics.11 This
stock return-firm characteristics regression can be estimated both in
the time-series (using panel data), and in the cross-section (using the
time series average of stock returns and firm characteristics). In the
baseline neoclassical model with one (physical) capital input and in
which the Hayashi conditions hold, the key firm characteristics 𝑋𝑖𝑡 in
the investment return formula in Eq. (5) include current profitability
(sales-to-capital ratio), and current and lagged physical capital invest-
ment rate. In other investment-based models, the key relevant firm
characteristics might be different, but as long as the firm characteristics
can be measured both in the data and in the model, we can evaluate if
a given model generates a relationship between realized stock returns
and firm characteristics that is consistent with the data (in terms of
goodness of fit and slope coefficients).

The stock return-firm characteristics regression can be used to eval-
uate investment-based models in at least two ways. First, it can be used
as out-of-sample moments to evaluate the cross-sectional and time-
series implications for stock returns of a given model’s calibration.
We follow this approach here. More generally, the stock return-firm
characteristics regressions can be used to simultaneously estimate the
structural parameters and test a given model. The estimation can
be done using alternative estimation methods such as the Simulated
Method of Moments (SMM), adding the stock return-firm characteristics
regression results estimated in the real data as an additional set of
target moments for model estimation.

Similar approaches have been proposed in the 𝑞-investment litera-
ture. Eberly et al. (2008) show that when the Hayashi conditions do
not hold, Tobin’s q is not a sufficient statistic for investment, but the
optimal investment from a model featuring decreasing returns to scale
and a fixed cost can still be well approximated by a log-linear function
of q. Gala et al. (2020) show that even under very general assumptions
about the nature of markets, production and investment technologies,
optimal investments are functions of and well captured by the relevant
state variables such as firm size and productivity.

8. Conclusion and implications for future research

Investment-based asset pricing models typically predict a close link
between a firm’s stock return and its characteristics at any point in time,
not just on average, as examined in previous studies. We show how
to incorporate the time-series predictions in the estimation and testing
of investment-based models using the GMM. In contrast with previous
studies that find that standard specifications of investment-based asset
pricing models with one (physical) capital input can match the cross
section of stock returns quite well, we find that these specifications
struggle to match the time series of stock returns observed in the data.

Our findings highlight the importance of examining the time-series
implications of investment-based models for proper model evaluation.
Because we only test a limited set of specifications of investment-
based models with one (physical) capital input, our findings do not
imply that the investment-based paradigm cannot match the time-
series of stock return data well. Our findings suggest, however, that a
different specification of the model, and/or a change in the econometric
procedures, is needed to capture the time-series dimension of stock
returns in the real data, which we argue is an important dimension
to match. Going forward, additional capital inputs such as intangible
capital as in Peters and Taylor (2017) and BGSV, quasi-fixed labor
inputs as in BGSV, or short-term and long-term assets as in GXZ, might
be added to the model. In addition, accounting for firm- or industry-
level heterogeneity in the technologies, which are assumed to be similar
across firms in the baseline analyses, as well as more general functional
forms, should be investigated. It could also be useful to examine further
the impact of financial frictions as in Gomes et al. (2006), or time-to-
build features as in Kuehn (2009), on the correlation between realized
stock returns and firm characteristics at both short- and long-horizons.
Finally, a broad assessment of the impact of measurement error as
in Erickson and Whited (2000) on the time-series moments should be
investigated.
