A R T I C L E I N F O

A B S T R A C T

Using granular data on U.S. hotel investments over the past two decades, we show that industry-specialist PE
firms achieve higher net income from operations and higher capital gains from sale than generalist PE firms
for comparable properties. Those results are driven by specialists implementing more and larger cost savings
without compromising revenues. Fundamentally, specialists utilize their hotel-specific operating expertise to
produce superior performance outcomes. We show that specialists across investment sectors possess deeper
industry-specific operating expertise. Our results suggest that specialist PE firms can compete with their
generalist rivals by leveraging such expertise in a chosen market niche.

1. Introduction

The private equity (PE) industry in the United States is changing.
Notably, new firm entrants increasingly specialize by investment sector.
Panel A of Fig. 1 shows that, while approximately 30% of PE firms
founded between 1998 and 2002 can be classified as sector specialists,
that fraction has almost doubled twenty years on. Over the same period,
the mean number of investment sectors targeted by newly founded PE
firms has declined from seven to less than five. Panel B of Fig. 1 shows
that generalist PE firms are larger than their specialist peers in terms
of assets under management, the number of investments held, and the
number of investment professionals employed. By virtue of their size,
generalist PE firms are likely to possess several competitive advantages
over smaller, specialist firms. Those advantages include access to a

wider pool of investment opportunities (Hochberg and Westerfield,
2010) and access to cheaper financing (Demiroglu and James, 2010;
Ivashina and Kovner, 2011). Given those advantages, how can specialist
PE firms compete against their generalist rivals?

We make progress towards answering this question by studying
the investments of specialist and generalist PE firms in the U.S. hotel
industry. That setting has two useful features. First, acquiring a hotel
typically involves buying the real estate that houses the hotel opera-
tions. We can observe a large sample of hotel real estate transactions
completed over the past two decades by a broad range of investor
types in a database provided by MSCI. The MSCI database includes
an investor classification that allows us to distinguish between PE
and non-PE investors. It also includes information on exact investor

identities, which allows us to manually classify PE firms into hotel
specialists and generalists. Second, hotel operators commonly partic-
ipate in performance benchmarking surveys. We can observe detailed
accounting information on hotel operating performance in a database
provided by CBRE Hotels. The CBRE database includes annual data
on hotel revenues, expenses, and profits. It also includes a rich set of
hotel characteristics. Using those characteristics, we merge the CBRE
and MSCI records to create a novel data set that integrates asset-
level transactions and ownership information with annual operating
performance data. Based on that data set, we study asset selection, asset
management choices, and investment performance outcomes on the
asset level for specialist and generalist PE firms in the hotel industry.
We provide novel insights into the hotel investments of specialist
and generalist PE firms. First, we show that PE specialists acquire hotels
that tend to be larger, older, and in need of repositioning. Their hotels
also tend to have higher expenses, lower profits, more diverse sources
of revenue, and higher labor costs. Our results indicate that, on average,
PE specialists and PE generalists do not compete for the same assets: PE
specialists seem to favor operationally more challenging hotels.

Second, conditional on acquiring hotels that are comparable ex
ante, PE specialists earn higher net income from operations than do
PE generalists. We estimate a 5.4 percentage point increase in net
income margins for hotels acquired by PE specialists. Those findings
are derived from staggered difference-in-differences regressions across
characteristics-matched hotels owned by PE specialists and generalists.
Using repeat-sales regressions with a rich set of covariates and fixed
effects, we show that PE specialists also experience higher capital gains.
In our preferred specification, we estimate that the capital gains to PE
specialists exceed those to generalists by about 16 percentage points
over the holding period. Those superior capital gains are consistent
with the improvements in hotel operating performance under specialist
PE ownership that we document.

We assess the robustness of our baseline findings in several ways.
We verify that our results on operating performance and capital gains
are not driven by any individual (large) PE firm. We check that our
results on capital gains are robust to accounting for the types of
counterparties with whom different investors trade. We corroborate our
evidence on the value added by PE specialists by considering property
tax assessments as an alternative measure of long-term value creation.
The estimates from those robustness tests are fully consistent with our
main results.

We identify the drivers of the observed differences in performance
outcomes across PE specialists and generalists by examining hetero-
geneity in their asset management choices. We find no evidence that
PE specialists stoke post-acquisition growth in revenues. However, we
find that PE specialists significantly reduce the labor cost of servicing
rooms. Moreover, specialists achieve significantly larger cost savings
than generalists on a number of other dimensions: general and adminis-
trative expenses, property taxes and other non-operating expenses, and
charges related to interest, amortization, and depreciation. We revert to
the interpretation of those findings in the context of asset management
strategies commonly employed by PE specialists below. In sum, our
results imply that PE specialists implement larger cost savings than do
other investor types, consistent with the higher bottom-line cash flows
documented for hotels owned by PE specialists.

Using data from Tripadvisor, we document a decline in guest ex-
perience ratings for hotels owned by PE specialists. The cost savings
implemented by PE specialists thus appear to compromise service
quality. However, recall that we do not find evidence for a decline
in revenues under specialist PE ownership, which might arise as a
result of poor service quality. Those findings suggest that PE special-
ists can avoid the potential risks of declining service quality when
implementing cost savings. Using data on construction projects from
Dodge Data & Analytics, we show that PE specialists also invest more
in capital expenditures to improve the physical structure of their hotels.
Specifically, our estimates suggest that PE specialists are more likely to
complete hotel alteration projects (e.g., renovations of F&B and other
common areas or remodeling larger suites into regular guest rooms).
Those findings provide additional insights into the asset management
choices of PE specialists.

What fundamental advantages do PE specialists possess over gen-
eralists that allow them to manage their hotel investments more effi-
ciently? Hotel investments require a combination of real estate-specific
investment expertise and expertise in running hotel operations. We
assess the role of real estate-specific investment expertise by isolating
from the set of PE generalists those that focus on real estate invest-
ments. We find that real estate-focused PE firms earn significantly
lower net income from operations and lower capital gains than do hotel
industry specialists. Real estate-specific expertise thus cannot explain
the superior investment performance achieved by PE specialists.

We then assess the role of industry-specific operating expertise in
the performance outcomes experienced by PE specialists from several
angles. We conduct expert interviews with PE specialists in the hotel in-
dustry. Our interview evidence indicates that PE specialists themselves

perceive hotel-specific operating expertise to be the key factor giving
them an advantage over PE generalists. We corroborate that perception
by evaluating information from PitchBook on the professional back-
grounds of the lead partners in the hotel deals completed by the PE
firms in our sample. Those analyses suggest that PE specialists’ lead
deal partners are more likely to have significant hotel-specific operating
experience as part of their résumés. If such expertise is key to the
success of PE specialists, then we would expect those specialists to have
a larger impact on performance outcomes in hotels with more complex
operations. We test that conjecture by conditioning our estimations of
hotel profit margins on a set of proxies for the complexity of hotel
operations. The results from those tests show that PE specialists indeed
have a larger impact on the operating performance outcomes of more
complex hotels. In sum, our results suggest that industry-specific oper-
ating expertise is a fundamental advantage that allows PE specialists to
manage their hotels more efficiently and to achieve stronger asset-level
performance outcomes than do PE generalists.

To provide more insight into the operating strategies driving spe-
cialists’ outperformance, we expand our expert interviews. We synthe-
size the views expressed by our interview partners to identify three
sets of operating strategies favored by PE specialists. The first strategy
highlighted by our interview partners is focused on improving oper-
ating efficiency, notably in terms of rationalizing labor inputs. The
second strategy described to us relates to paring back activities that
may lead to higher guest experience ratings but harm net income. The
final strategy outlined by our experts includes a diverse set of measures
such as replacing the incumbent management company, introducing
tax-efficient sale-and-lease-back structures, and reducing property tax
liabilities by appealing tax assessments. The operating strategies out-
lined to us are extremely varied, and according to our expert interviews,
their implementation is tailored to the operational and structural details
of each hotel. Nonetheless, we find key aspects of those strategies
reflected in our empirical results.

In the final part of our paper, we discuss three implications of
our results. First, we address the external validity of our analyses. We
have documented that PE specialists in the hotel sector outperform
generalists based on the industry-specific operating expertise of their
investment professionals. Could such expertise help PE specialists com-
pete against generalists more broadly? To answer that question, we
obtain the résumés of a representative sample of investment profes-
sionals at generalist and specialist PE firms from PitchBook. We then
use ChatGPT to assess the operating expertise of those professionals
based on their résumés and to infer the industry in which they possess
the most expertise. We find that professionals at PE specialists have
significantly more industry-specific operating expertise than those at
generalists. The inferred industry for professionals at PE specialists is
also more likely to match their respective firms’ preferred investment
industry. Thus, it seems plausible that PE specialists across all sectors
possess superior industry-specific operating expertise that they can
leverage to achieve stronger investment performance outcomes.

Second, we need to acknowledge that we cannot observe the (risk-
adjusted) investment performance outcomes experienced by the limited
partners of the specialist and generalist PE firms included in our study.
Thus, it would be inappropriate to conclude from our results that
limited partners are strictly better off when investing with specialist
PE firms. To wit, we show that, conditional on observables, generalist
PE firms pay lower mortgage interest rates for the hotels they acquire.
Those results imply that while PE generalists may not match PE special-
ists on their depth of industry-specific operating expertise, they enjoy
other competitive advantages that may benefit their limited partners.
Third, our results allow limited conclusions on the reasons why
PE generalists do not seem to acquire the industry-specific operating
expertise of PE specialists. Prior studies point to a trade-off between
access to a wider pool of investment opportunities and the ability to add
more value in a smaller set of investment assets (see, e.g., Hochberg and
Westerfield, 2010). This question could be investigated further in future

studies. Our research setting is best suited to analyzing asset-level
investment strategies and performance outcomes across PE specialists
and generalists. On that basis, our results show that PE specialists
seem to enjoy productivity advantages stemming from industry-specific
operating expertise that allow them to achieve superior asset-level
performance outcomes.

Our paper contributes to the literature on the drivers of PE invest-
ment performance.1 Those drivers include market conditions at the time
of the investment (Davis et al., 2019), the competitive environment
faced by PE firms (Ljungqvist et al., 2020), as well as fundamental char-
acteristics of PE firms, such as their size (Lopez-de Silanes et al., 2015),
access to public capital markets (Gao et al., 2021), and investment
experience (Acharya et al., 2013; Bernstein and Sheen, 2016). We pro-
vide evidence that PE firms’ industry specialization and the associated
industry-specific operating expertise of their investment professionals
are first-order drivers of PE investment performance on the asset level.2
Further, existing studies show that PE investment strategies are shaped
by the characteristics of PE firms and their target companies (Kaplan
and Stromberg, 2009; Gompers et al., 2016, 2020; Biesinger et al.,
2020). We show that PE firms’ industry specialization and industry-
specific operating expertise influence their asset selection as well as the
asset management strategies employed. Notably, our results underline
that the notion of a homogeneous ‘‘PE model’’ implicit in much of the
academic research and public debate about PE needs to be revised.

We proceed as follows. Section 2 presents data and sample selection.
Sections 3, 4 and 5 document heterogeneity in the asset selection
choices, investment performance outcomes, and asset management
strategies of PE specialists and generalists in the hotel industry. We
relate our findings to fundamental differences between PE specialists
and generalists in Section 6. We discuss the wider implications of our
findings in Section 7. Section 8 offers concluding remarks.

2. Data and sample selection

We construct the data set for our main empirical analyses by com-
bining transaction-level information with annual hotel operating per-
formance data. We introduce each of those data sets in turn.

2.1. Transactions data

We obtain asset-level data on hotel transactions from MSCI (for-
merly Real Capital Analytics). We start with all hotel transactions com-
pleted in the U.S. over the 2001–2019 period. The MSCI database in-
cludes single-asset deals, portfolio deals, and entity-level deals (e.g., ac-
quisitions of legal entities that hold hotel properties).3 We exclude
transactions of minority interests, partial leaseholds, and other non-
standard conveyance types. The excluded records represent less than
10% of the transactions data. In total, our sample includes 26,875 hotel
transactions for 17,092 unique hotel properties.

Each transaction in the MSCI database is linked to up to four differ-
ent buyers and sellers. The presence of more than one buyer or seller

2.2. Operating performance data

We collect annual accounting data on hotel operations from CBRE
Hotels. CBRE gathers those data based on a voluntary survey inviting
participating hotels to submit their operating performance information
in return for access to industry benchmarking reports. CBRE reports
that their survey covers about 7,000 hotels, which represents 15% of
the hotels in the U.S. The survey is focused on institutional-grade hotel
assets; that is, those occupying the mid-market and higher chain scales,
that are brand-affiliated, and that operate under professional ownership
and management companies.

The structure of the CBRE survey follows the industry-standard Uni-
form System of Accounts for the Lodging Industry (USALI), which facili-
tates comparisons across hotels.5 The CBRE survey includes hundreds of
variables, covering general information about the hotel (e.g., location,
price segment, management, and ownership), top-line performance
indicators (e.g., average daily rate and occupancy), aggregate measures
of bottom-line hotel profitability, and granular data on revenues and
costs across all hotel departments (e.g., rooms, food and beverage, and
conference facilities).

We obtain data on two sub-samples of hotels from CBRE. First,
we obtain operating performance data for the period 2000–2018 for
all hotels with a PE buyer or seller in the MSCI database for which
CBRE has at least one year of performance data in the two years before
a PE transaction and at least one year of performance data in the
two years after such a transaction (the subject group). This sample
contains almost 19,000 observations for 1,274 hotels, representing 1,839
individual hotel transactions.6 Second, we obtain the corresponding
performance data for a set of peer hotels comparable to those in the
subject group. This control group is constructed by matching transacted
hotels in the subject group to peer hotels outside of that group based on
ex ante observable hotel characteristics. Those characteristics capture,
to the greatest extent possible, the revenue drivers and cost structures
of the sample hotels. Specifically, we choose control hotels to match the
subject hotels based on hotel location (ZIP code), product type (e.g., air-
port, conference, and resort hotels), quality category (e.g., economy,
upscale, and luxury hotels), and hotel size (room count). The control
group constructed from the criteria described above contains over
16,000 observations for 1,311 hotels.7

4. Heterogeneity in investment performance

We proceed with analyses of investment performance outcomes
experienced by specialist and generalist PE investors. For the purposes
of those analyses, we again consider an asset to be acquired by a PE
specialist (generalist) if any of the associated buyers are classified as
PE specialists (generalists).

4.1. Operating performance

The key challenge to identifying the causal effects of PE ownership
on hotel operating performance is the heterogeneity in asset selection
shown in the previous section. Our matching of subject hotels to control
hotels based on ex ante observable characteristics helps us address this
identification issue. The main goal of the matching procedure is to pair
each subject hotel with a control hotel that likely has similar future
growth prospects at the time of the PE acquisition. As outlined in
Section 2, we match hotels based on their location, hotel type, chain
scale, and hotel size, which together largely determine a hotel’s macro
environment, revenue potential, and cost structure.9

We formalize the analysis of hotel operating performance under PE
ownership using a staggered difference-in-differences strategy across
the subject hotels and their characteristics-matched control hotels
around the time of PE investments in the subject hotels. Our specifica-
tion is similar to that employed in, e.g., Biesinger et al. (2020) or Cohn
et al. (2021). In contrast to the specifications in those studies, our set-
up additionally accounts for different types of PE investors, namely,

4.3. Robustness checks

We assess the robustness of the results on operating performance
and capital gains presented in the previous two sections in three ways.
First, we verify that our results are not driven by the influence of any
single PE firm. To that end, we repeat key estimations of operating
performance outcomes and capital gains leaving out, in turn, the most-
frequently observed generalist PE (i.e., Blackstone) and the three most
frequently observed PE specialists (i.e., RLJ Development, Noble Invest-
ment Group, and Thayer Lodging). The results from those robustness
tests are reported in Online Appendix D. The estimates shown there
are fully consistent with our main findings.

Second, prior work highlights heterogeneity in real estate investor
preferences not only over the specific assets they acquire, but also
over the types of counterparties with whom they trade (see,e.g., Ghent,
2021; Badarinza et al., 2022). A corresponding narrative in the PE
industry would suggest that PE firms systematically buy from spe-
cific seller types, e.g., private hotel owners, and subsequently sell
to different investor types, such as institutional investors. In Online
Appendix E, we test that hypothesis in the data. While our analyses
reveal somewhat different distributions of counterparties for specialist
PE firms vs. generalist PE firms and non-PE investors, the results of
capital gains regressions that control for counterparty types are fully
consistent with those reported previously.

Lastly, there is a possibility that the capital gains realized by (spe-
cialist) PE firms reflect some form of arbitrage profits rather than
genuine long-term value creation. To address this potential concern,
we use property tax data from ATTOM to analyze alternative measures
of value creation. The results from those analyses, reported in Online
Appendix F, show that property tax bills and assessed values increase
more under specialist PE ownership than under other owner types.
Those results are consistent with the findings from our main capital
gains regressions.

5. Heterogeneity in asset management choices

We dig deeper into the drivers of the observed differences in oper-
ating performance outcomes across specialist and generalist PE firms
by examining heterogeneity in key asset management choices between
those investor types. Specifically, we analyze revenue growth and cost
savings, potential trade-offs between cost savings and service quality,
and follow-up investments into the assets acquired. While this set of
outcomes is not a comprehensive list of all possible asset management
choices, it allows us to explore key aspects of what different types of
PE firms do to their target assets in the hotel industry to achieve the
documented performance outcomes.

5.1. Revenue growth vs. cost savings

PE firms may experience superior investment performance out-
comes because they stoke revenue growth in their target companies.
Key drivers of hotel revenues are average daily rates and occupancy. PE
firms may also achieve stronger investment performance outcomes be-
cause they implement larger cost savings. To evaluate the evidence on

those potential drivers of operating performance under PE ownership,
we estimate Eq. (1) for total revenues, the above-mentioned determi-
nants of revenue growth, and a set of key hotel expense measures.
All expense measures included in our estimations are scaled by total
revenues.11 The data for those analyses are from the CBRE operating
performance survey. We summarize the results in Table 5. For brevity,
we report the key coefficients and statistics for each regression in rows
rather than columns.

The first set of rows in Table 5 presents the results from estimating
Eq. (1) for hotel revenues and key drivers of those revenues. The
coefficient estimates reported indicate that PE ownership has no sig-
nificant impact on hotel revenues overall. While we observe a slight
increase in average room rates for hotels owned by PE specialists, we
find no evidence that PE ownership has a significant impact on hotel
occupancy. The results presented suggest that the profitability gains at
hotels under specialist PE ownership are not driven by those investors
stoking revenue growth.

The second set of rows in Table 5 summarizes the estimation results
for different categories of labor expenses. We focus on labor expenses
because the results in Table 3 indicate a significant increase in depart-
mental profits for hotels owned by PE specialists, and labor expenses
account for the largest part of hotels’ departmental expenses (i.e., direct
operating expenses). The coefficient estimates presented indicate that
labor expenses in the rooms department (i.e., housekeeping staff) de-
cline significantly under specialist PE ownership. We find no evidence
that PE ownership is associated with reductions in labor expenses in the
food and beverage department or in any other operating departments.
We then assess the effects of PE ownership on the individual
components of undistributed expenses (i.e., overhead expenses), non-
operating expenses, and fixed charges to better understand what drives
the favorable impact of PE specialists on net income documented
earlier. The estimates reported indicate that generalist and specialist
PE ownership are both associated with lower management fees and
lower maintenance expenses. The estimates reported also indicate that
PE specialists achieve significantly larger cost savings than generalists
in several additional dimensions: general and administrative expenses,
property taxes and other non-operating expenses, and charges related
to interest, amortization, and depreciation. The impact of reductions
in the latter charges on net income from operations is somewhat offset
by an increase in rent expenses—a finding to which we revert later. We
also find a slight increase in IT expenses under specialist PE ownership.
In sum, the evidence presented in this section suggests that PE
specialists implement larger cost savings than do other types of hotel
investors. We find that effect to be most notable in terms of labor costs
in the rooms department and in terms of several key drivers of fixed
charges. The results discussed here also show that PE specialists exert
a favorable influence over a wider set of expense measures than do PE
generalists.

6. Fundamental differences between private equity investor types

Our results indicate that, conditional on selecting ex ante compa-
rable assets, PE specialists achieve superior performance outcomes in
terms of net income and capital gains. Those outcomes are consistent
with differences in asset management, where we show that PE spe-
cialists implement more and larger cost savings. What fundamental
advantages do PE specialists have that allow them to manage their
hotels differently, and seemingly to greater financial gain, than do PE
specialists?

Hotel investments require a combination of real estate-specific in-
vestment expertise and expertise in running hotel operations. PE spe-
cialists may possess fundamental advantages in either one of those
areas. To assess the role of real estate-specific investment expertise
in driving the investment performance outcomes of PE specialists, we
isolate from the set of PE generalists those that focus on real estate
investments. We identify real estate-focused PE firms by searching the
websites of all PE generalists in the sample for information on the types
of assets they invest in. We classify as real estate-focused PE firms those
that only invest in real estate, including but not limited to hotel real
estate. If real estate-specific investment expertise is the primary driver
behind the superior performance outcomes achieved by PE specialists,
we would expect real estate-focused PE firms to achieve performance
outcomes comparable to those of PE specialists.

6.2. The role of industry-specific operating expertise

To assess the role of industry-specific operating expertise in deter-
mining investment performance outcomes for PE firms in the hotel
sector, we first interviewed a range of PE specialists. Specifically, we
asked those specialists about their own assessment of what sets them
apart from PE generalists. The CEO of a specialist hotel PE investment
firm, himself an industry veteran, put it as follows:

‘‘There’s only a handful of PE investors specializing in
hospitality real estate. I think those that I would characterize
as being the very best have an operating background, and
understand what it’s like to actually be in a hotel. How
do you think about revenue and stacking different kinds of
customers? It’s a 365 days a year business, 24 hours a day,
and every customer is unique and different. [...] So the best
leaders I have found as investors have been those that have
as a part of their background an experience of being in the
field and being an operator’’.

The same CEO also thought that this focus on hotel operations
rather than capital allocation was the main differentiating factor be-
tween PE specialists and generalists in the hotel industry:

‘‘There’s a real difference between the generalists and
those that are specialists. We call this the difference between
being an operator and an allocator. [...] We’ll focus on
bringing our tools, our resources as an operator, and being
able to reconstitute a revenue stream, profitability matrix,
make them more efficient, make them more profitable, and
then we’ll sell them to an institutional marketplace. And so I
think the difference between specialists and non-specialists is
really how we think about that execution, which is where we
have a natural competitive advantage’’.

Our interviewee did not think that specialists were allocating capital
to different markets (‘‘I don’t know that we view the world very differently
in terms of growth markets’’.), but added that the smaller scale of
specialists allowed them dedicate more resources to each investment
asset. He also added that his firm was more conservative in terms of
financial leverage than PE generalists:

‘‘We don’t use those kinds of structures to generate alpha.
For us it’s really about buying the best real estate on the
best street corner in a market that we want to be in, and
enhancing that physically, and then taking full advantage of
that operationally’’.

6.3. Interview evidence on specialist asset management strategies

specialist, the CFO of a hotel REIT, an industry veteran from a global
hotel operator, and an academic expert on hospitality investment. In
this section, we synthesize the responses from those interviews into
three sets of asset management strategies considered popular with PE
specialists in the hotel sector.

First, we find that the asset management strategies of PE specialists
focus on improving operating efficiency to maximize net income. Com-
mon adjustments to hotel operations include reductions in labor costs
through more efficient staffing and scheduling. Rationalizing labor in-
puts is often paired with modernizing hotel IT systems. Those strategies
are consistent with the findings reported in Section 5.1 that hotels expe-
rience significant declines in labor costs under specialist PE ownership,
combined with slight increases in IT expenses. As we show, those asset
management strategies on balance lead to higher departmental profits
and, ultimately, higher net income from operations.

Second, the asset management strategies of PE specialists reportedly
include paring back unprofitable activities and unnecessary expenses
incurred by previous owners. For instance, small owner-operators may
host unprofitable events simply because they are the locally-owned
hotel in town. As another example, ‘‘light-touch’’ housekeeping may
replace daily service. More generally, our interview partners report that
it can be unprofitable to incur the costs required to achieve perfect
guest experience scores on platforms such as Tripadvisor. To maximize
net income, hoteliers are often better served by aiming for slightly
lower levels of guest satisfaction. Those strategies are mirrored in the
evidence in Section 5.2 on the trade-offs between cost savings and
service quality for PE specialists.

Third, PE specialists may look beyond hotel operations for efficiency
improvements. Examples include replacing the incumbent management
company, introducing tax-efficient ‘‘OpCo/PropCo’’ structures (a form
of sale-and-lease-back), and managing property tax liabilities by chal-
lenging local tax assessments. In Section 5.1, we present evidence of
reductions in management fees; increases in rent expenses combined
with reductions in interest, amortization, and depreciation; and reduc-
tions in property taxes. Those findings are fully consistent with the asset
management strategies described to us by PE specialists in the hotel
sector.

The evidence presented in this section shows that: PE specialists per-
ceive their hotel-specific operating expertise to be the key factor setting
them apart from PE generalists; PE specialists’ lead partners are more
likely to have hotel-specific operating experience; PE specialists have a
larger impact on performance in hotels with more complex operations,
consistent with them possessing and leveraging superior hotel-specific
operating expertise; and key aspects of the asset management strategies
favored by PE specialists are reflected in our main empirical results.
In sum, the differences between PE generalists and specialists in asset
management strategies and resulting performance outcomes appear to
be driven by differences in industry-specific operating expertise.

7. Discussion

We address three issues about the wider implications of our results.
First, we discuss the external validity of our findings. Second, we
acknowledge an important limitation of our work and how it affects
the practical implications of our findings for the limited partners in PE
firms. Lastly, we pose a question for future research on the competition
between PE specialists and generalists based on a puzzling finding we
cannot explain conclusively. We address each of those issues in turn.

7.3. Question for future research

Why do PE generalists not acquire the same industry-specific oper-
ating expertise as specialists? We cannot answer this question conclu-
sively. Prior literature on specialization points to a trade-off between
access to a larger investment opportunity set and the ability to add
more value to each asset in a smaller investment portfolio (Hochberg
and Westerfield, 2010; Brown et al., 2023). It may be sub-optimal for
a generalist PE firm to hire professionals with deep industry-specific
operating expertise. Almost any individual industry likely represents
a small fraction of the overall investment opportunity set, and there
might be times when a generalist PE firm does not want to invest in
a given industry at all. Conversely, for individuals who spent their
career in, for instance, the hotel industry, and who want to transition
to investment management, the most intuitive option might be to start
up or join a PE firm focused on that industry. Thus, the market for
human capital in the PE sector may be segmented. Analyzing the links
between the industrial organization of the PE sector, the allocation of

talent in this sector, and the consequences for the competition between
PE specialists and generalists is beyond the scope of this study. We leave
that question for future research.

8. Conclusion

How can specialist PE firms compete with the inherent advantages
of their generalist rivals? We make progress towards answering this
question by analyzing a novel data set on thousands of investments
completed by PE specialists, generalists, and other, non-PE investors
in the U.S. hotel industry over the past two decades. We show that
sector-specialist PE firms achieve higher net income from operations
and realize higher capital gains than do generalist PE firms holding ex-
ante comparable assets. Those outcomes are predominantly driven by
PE specialists implementing larger cost savings in more areas of hotel
operations without compromising revenues. We use a combination
of cross-sectional heterogeneity tests and expert interviews to show
that a fundamental advantage allowing PE specialists to manage their
hotel investments more efficiently lies in the industry-specific operating
expertise of their investment professionals. Machine-learning analyses
on the résumés of investment professionals at specialist PE firms beyond
the hotel sector reveal that those individuals systematically possess
deeper industry-specific operating expertise than their peers at general-
ist firms. Our results suggest that specialist firms in the PE market can
compete with their generalist rivals by leveraging operating expertise
in a chosen market niche.
