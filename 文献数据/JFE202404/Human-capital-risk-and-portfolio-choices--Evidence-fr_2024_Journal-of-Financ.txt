1.  Introduction

Theory suggests that increasing idiosyncratic, uninsurable labor income risk may cause individuals to reduce the 
risk in their ﬁnancial assets. This relationship is confounded empirically by the tendency of risk tolerant people 
to choose riskier careers and hold riskier portfolios, leading to an upward-biased estimate of the eﬀect of earnings 
risk on risky assets holdings. We overcome this identiﬁcation problem by exploiting a discontinuity built into the 
Danish national university admissions system, which provides quasi-random assignment of similar applicants to 
programs with diﬀerent earnings volatility proﬁles. Our methodology allows us to measure the causal impact 
of enrolling in a high-volatility program, holding ﬁxed the average program earnings and human capital betas. 
We show that entering a program whose enrollees subsequently experience volatile earnings causes students to 
have more volatile earnings and, ceteris paribus, to hold fewer risky assets and be less likely to participate in the 
stock market. We calibrate our empirical results to a portfolio choice model with risky labor income that ﬁts our 
empirical ﬁndings well with modest participation costs, myopic behavior, and reasonable levels of risk aversion.

Human capital risk — speciﬁcally labor income risk — is arguably 
the most important risk people face. When income risk is independent 
of asset returns and cannot be traded away or diversiﬁed, consumers 
with standard preferences should optimally tilt their investment port-
folio away from risky assets (Kimball, 1993; Gollier and Pratt, 1996; 
Viceira, 2001; Cocco et al., 2005). As a response to this “background 
risk”, households with higher labor income risk should therefore be less 
likely to participate in the stock market, and conditional on participa-
tion should hold less risky assets.1 Quantifying the eﬀect of earnings 
risk on ﬁnancial choices has important implications for asset pricing 
(Bodie  et  al.,  1992;  Danthine  and  Donaldson,  2002;  Addoum  et  al., 
2019), the hedging of labor income through ﬁnancial markets (Massa 
and Simonov, 2006; Betermier et al., 2012), and for the link between 
stock market participation and the equity premium (Vissing-Jørgensen, 
2002).

However,  individuals  jointly  choose  their  careers  and  investment 
portfolios for reasons driven by common factors that are frequently un-

observable  to  researchers,  such  as  risk  preferences.  This  can  lead  to 
an omitted variable bias, causing an attenuation of the eﬀect of labor 
earnings risk on portfolio risk (Fagereng et al., 2018). Put simply, risk 
tolerant individuals tend to choose riskier careers and riskier portfolio 
holdings, so the observed correlation between earnings and portfolio 
risk will be larger than implied by the true causal eﬀect of income risk 
on portfolio choices. Furthermore, in order to credibly isolate the im-
pact of earnings risk, researchers must not only use variation orthogonal 
to unobserved preferences, but also control for other features of the 
earnings process (level, growth, and comovement with stock returns) 
that might impact stock holding.

To overcome this identiﬁcation problem, we exploit an institutional 
feature of the Danish university admissions system that creates quasi-
random variation in admission to university programs, and therefore in 
earnings risk proﬁles. Prospective university students rank up to eight 
programs in preferred order and compete for limited enrollment capac-
ity in selective programs based on a composite score that reﬂects their 
grades in high school. A central agency allocates placement in the dif-
ferent programs in preferred order to students with the highest scores,

We  ﬁnd  that  higher  earnings  volatility  caused  by  university  pro-

increase  in  cross-program  permanent  earnings  volatility  (i.e.,  an  in-

by  23%  (a  6.5  percentage-point  drop  from  0.28  to  0.215).  Further-
more, conditional on individuals participating in the stock market, a 
one-standard-deviation increase in cross-program permanent earnings 
volatility decreases the share of risky assets in the portfolio by 8% (a 2 
percentage-point drop from 0.25 to 0.23).

Our identiﬁcation strategy allows us to exploit the rich cross-section 
variation in programs’ earnings process and individual characteristics. 
We investigate how our estimates vary when we explore heterogene-
ity in individual characteristics. In particular, models predict that the 
impact of exogenous labor income risk on portfolio choices will vary 
with ﬁnancial wealth, human capital wealth, and age (Campbell and 
Viceira, 2002). We show that individuals with higher labor earnings 
have larger background risk eﬀects. Because the importance of ﬁnan-
cial wealth naturally increases relative to human capital wealth over 
the life cycle, extrapolating from these ﬁndings would predict smaller 
background risk eﬀects for older workers.

We  also  investigate  other  potential  mechanisms  that  could  be  at 
play. First, the program-level share of stock market participation could 
be driving our results, either through program peer eﬀects (e.g. Brunello 
et al., 2010; Feld and Zölitz, 2017; Booij et al., 2017) or knowledge 
about the stock market (e.g. Hong et al., 2004; Ouimet and Tate, 2020).

While earnings volatility – driven by variation in program of study 
– causes people to take less portfolio risk, the data show no raw asso-
ciation between earnings volatility and portfolio risk. This divergence 
between correlations and causal eﬀects can be explained by risk-based 
selection into programs of study. Students inclined to hold risky port-
folios tend to select into programs of study with volatile earnings, even 
though those programs cause them to take less portfolio risk. To illus-
trate this, we decompose our results in terms of average earnings and 
permanent earnings volatility from one of the least risky broad ﬁelds 
(education) to one of the riskiest ﬁelds (business). We show that the 
null association found in the data could be the result of an omitted vari-
able bias, highlighting the need for an identiﬁcation strategy to measure 
the causal eﬀect of earnings risk.

We use our IV estimates to derive target moments to which we cal-
ibrate a single-period model of portfolio choice with risky exogenous 
labor supply, following the setup in Campbell and Viceira (2002). Our 
model targets the average risky asset share in our sample and its IV-
derived gradient with respect to labor earnings volatility, as evaluated 
at  average  earnings.  By  comparing  our  empirical  estimates  with  the 
calibrated model results, our model can rationalize our empirical ﬁnd-
ings with modest participation costs (averaging 390DKK, $55), myopic 
behavior (consumers consider 1.1 years of future labor earnings), and 
reasonable levels of risk aversion (coeﬃcient of relative risk aversion of 
12).

We would expect background risk eﬀects stemming from risky la-
bor earnings to be more important for individuals with higher labor 
earnings  (holding  wealth  ﬁxed).  This  qualitative  prediction  is  borne 
out in our model calibration and empirical estimates, both of which 
show the gradient of the risky asset share gets steeper as the level of 
earnings increases. This variation with respect to earnings provides ad-
ditional untargeted moments that provide an out-of-sample test of our 
calibrated model. While point estimates from our IV regressions suggest 
a somewhat larger role than the model does for the level of earnings to 
increase background risk eﬀects, the model’s predictions are well within 
the conﬁdence intervals implied by our regressions.

1.1.  Contribution to the literature

Similar admission discontinuities to those we exploit here have been 
used in other contexts, most often to measure the eﬀect of program 
of  study  on  earnings.4 While  we  use  these  same  discontinuities,  our 
work diﬀers by studying how individuals adjust their portfolio choices 
in response to features of their program of study, notably its permanent 
earnings volatility.

Beginning with Guiso et al. (1996), a growing literature has docu-
mented the eﬀect of labor income risk on household investment portfo-
lios. Some evidence suggests that labor income risk typically has small

eﬀects in deterring stock market participation, or the fraction of risky 
assets held in investment portfolios (Fagereng et al., 2018).5 Bertaut 
and Haliassos (1997) study long-run precautionary motives in portfolio 
allocations and show that it is persistent risk that aﬀects rebalancing, 
rather than short-run risk. This idea is put forth more directly by An-
gerer and Lam (2009) who study the association between earnings risk 
and portfolio choices by explicitly separating permanent and transitory 
volatility.

However, unobservable risk preferences could correlate with both 
career and portfolio choices; the eﬀect of risk preferences on portfolio 
allocations was established some decades ago (Cass and Stiglitz, 1972; 
Cohn et al., 1975),6 and research suggests that risk preferences also af-
fect educational and career choices (e.g. Saks and Shore, 2005; Nielsen 
and Vissing-Jorgensen, 2006; Calvet et al., 2021). While the literature 
has recognized that the correlation between earnings risk and portfolio 
choices is likely to suﬀer from an omitted variable bias as a result, an 
issue remains because of the diﬃculty in ﬁnding sources of exogenous 
variation in earnings risk.

Fagereng et al. (2018) discuss how these selection issues, notably 
unobserved risk aversion, can bias estimates of the eﬀect of labor in-
come risk on portfolio choices. To overcome these issues, the authors 
exploit variation over time in ﬁrms’ proﬁtability risk. The authors ﬁnd 
that when the proﬁtability of a worker’s ﬁrm becomes more volatile, 
workers hold fewer risky assets. Their ﬁndings imply large background 
risk  eﬀects  even  though  large  changes  in  the  volatility  of  ﬁrm  prof-
itability have little impact on the volatility of labor income, since ﬁrms 
insulate workers from most of the change in ﬁrm proﬁtability risk.

Our paper shares with Fagereng et al. (2018) a common concern 
that unobserved risk aversion can bias estimates of the eﬀect of labor 
income risk on portfolio choices. Whereas their paper highlights the 
role of ﬁrms in insuring their workers against changes in proﬁtability 
risk, we ﬁnd that moving from a low-volatility to a high-volatility Uni-
versity program of study has a large and roughly one-for-one impact on 
an individual’s permanent earnings volatility. As a consequence, we ex-
plore an empirical setting with substantial variation in the labor income 
risk that individuals face and against which they are not insulated. This 
allows us to explore the impact of uninsurable changes in labor income 
risk over a wide range of risk values.

Betermier et al. (2012) analyze the portfolio holdings of households 
who switch industries between 1999 and 2002, in order to evaluate 
the eﬀect of earnings risk on portfolio choices while holding individual 
preferences constant. There is evidence that ﬁrms diﬀer in the earnings 
volatility of their employees (e.g. as correlated with technological in-
novation Kogan et al., 2020), and the paper tests the degree to which 
this association is causal. They ﬁnd eﬀects of switching across indus-
tries but no level eﬀects, suggesting that diﬀerences in cross-sectional 
preferences inﬂuence both earnings volatility and portfolio choices.7 Jo 
(2020) studies the eﬀect of state-level changes in employment protec-

Compared to previous studies, the rich cross-sectional variation in 
program earnings allows us to credibly study the causal eﬀect on ﬁnan-
cial decision-making of several features of the earnings process simul-
taneously and investigate heterogeneous eﬀects. This is an important 
feature of our empirical design because programs may diﬀer along other 
dimensions in addition to earning volatility, such as their level, growth, 
and human capital betas. Our results show that these other earnings 
process features do not mediate the causal impact of earnings volatility 
on portfolio choices. To our knowledge, this paper is unique in cleanly 
estimating the causal impact on portfolio choices of all of these earnings 
process features simultaneously.

Finally, our results suggest that quasi-random changes in university 
program of study pass through to portfolio choices because of exoge-
nous changes in earnings risk. Previous studies have identiﬁed that a 
welfare cost may exist due to the imperfect matching between prospec-
tive students’ risk preferences and the earnings risk associated with their 
program of study (Jensen and Shore, 2015; Barth et al., 2017). We con-
tribute to this literature by showing that students use education to sort 
into occupations that diﬀer in their earnings risk, based on their risk 
aversion. We also provide evidence that individuals adjust their ﬁnan-
cial behavior in response to the eﬀect of earnings volatility stemming 
from their program of study.

2.  Institutional setting

2.1.  Danish national university application

Our  study  design  is  based  on  the  institutional  details  of  univer-
sity admissions in Denmark. Prospective students apply to university 
through a central agency that allocates students to programs with lim-
ited enrollment capacity, which creates plausibly exogenous variation 
in the probability of acceptance into diﬀerent programs. As described 
in Andersen et al. (2020) and Heinesen (2018), university admission in 
Denmark is based on prospective students’ high school performance. A 
standardized score ranging from 0 to 13 in 0.1 increments is calculated 
based on students’ grades in their senior years of high school, taking 
into account the level and diﬃculty of the classes. Students can list up 
to eight programs (that is, university-major pairs such as International 
Finance  at  Copenhagen  Business  School)  in  order  of  preference.  Ad-
mission is granted to programs in preferred order to students with the 
highest scores until all seats are ﬁlled. For competitive programs, this 
generates an admission cutoﬀ, according to which students with a score 
above the cutoﬀ are admitted, while those with a score below it are not.
Admission to most programs depends only on the prospective stu-
dent’s high school score, with two exceptions. First, admissions can be 
made through two diﬀerent “Quota” systems. The Quota 1 admission 
system,  with  applications  in  June,  is  intended  for  students  applying 
directly  after  completion  of  high  school.  These  prospective  students 
are evaluated for admission into their chosen programs solely based 
on their composite high school score. The Quota 2 admission system, 
with applications in March, also evaluates prospective students based 
on their composite score, but allocates additional bonus points for extra-
curricular  activities.  This  system  is  usually  chosen  by  students  with 
relevant work experience for the programs to which they are applying. 
In our data we cannot observe these bonus points, thus the admission 
rule for Quota 2 partially depends on unobservable factors. In the sam-
ple we analyze, 54.4% of applications are made through the Quota 1 
admission system and the remainder through Quota 2.

Second, when applying to diﬀerent programs, students have the op-

cutoﬀ for their preferred program but are not accepted (typically, in 
such cases they are 0.1 to 0.2 points below the admission cutoﬀ). If any 
students drop out of the program in the ﬁrst few weeks of the semester, 
applicants on the standby list will be oﬀered places in the program. If 
no students drop out of the program, applicants on the standby list are 
guaranteed admission to the program in the following year. In our sam-
ple, about 40% of students choose this option when applying. Because 
these applications are conceptually diﬀerent and allow students to be 
admitted to programs despite having a lower score than the admission 
cutoﬀ, we rule them out in our analysis.

The institutional setting of university admission in Denmark is ideal 
for studying the eﬀect of education on individuals. Because the system 
credibly  leads  to  exogenous  variation  in  admission  to  diﬀerent  pro-
grams, it de facto randomizes students who are close to the admission 
cutoﬀs into programs with diﬀerent earnings processes. The fact that 
randomness dictates admission to diﬀerent programs for students close 
to the admission cutoﬀ allows us to control for the potentially endoge-
nous self-selection into university programs. If risk-averse students tend 
to enroll in programs for which earnings are less volatile, studying the 
relationship between earnings volatility and stock market participation 
could lead to erroneous conclusions regarding its eﬀect. Instead, as de-
scribed below, we focus on marginal students who are either slightly 
under or slightly above the cutoﬀ for their preferred program, such that 
a small change in the admission cutoﬀ would lead to admission to dif-
ferent programs.

admission cutoﬀ for their less-preferred safety program. This ensures 
that small changes in admission cutoﬀs due to annual supply and de-
mand will generate discontinuous variation in the enrollment program 
of these students. When the admission system is implemented strictly 
based on a student’s high school score, admission to either their reach

2.2.1.  Validity of the admission discontinuities

The Danish National University admission system closely relates to 
serial  dictatorship,  which  is  both  Pareto  eﬃcient  and  strategy-proof 
(Svensson,  1999),  subject  to  limits  on  the  number  of  programs  that 
can be ranked. This generates credible variation in admission to diﬀer-
ent university programs (Kirkeboen et al., 2016). However, a potential 
problem could arise if prospective students were able to guess the cut-
oﬀ of their preferred programs based on public information about past 
cutoﬀs and manipulate their rankings to increase their chances of en-
tering their preferred program. In this section, we present year-to-year 
variation in cutoﬀs, as well as formal density manipulation tests.

Panels (a) and (b) of Fig. A.1 show how cutoﬀs move from year 
to year, both unconditionally and based on the distance between the 
student’s current score and the previous year’s admission cutoﬀ.10 This 
ﬁgure shows that there is signiﬁcant year-to-year variation +/- 1 point 
around admission cutoﬀs, which also justiﬁes our choice of estimation 
bandwidth around the cutoﬀs. In our main results, we also control for 
the previous year’s admission cutoﬀs, so that the results are identiﬁed 
from changes in the admission cutoﬀ. Panel (c) presents the density 
of applications around the cutoﬀs. There is no evidence of bunching 
around the cutoﬀs, however we formally test this in the next section.

Table A.1 presents the p-values for density manipulation tests de-
veloped  by  Cattaneo  et  al.  (2019) under  diﬀerent  assumptions.  The 
estimation details are presented in the table’s notes. Only 1 of these 
12 cases (less than 10%), fails to reject manipulation of the assignment 
variable at the 5% signiﬁcance level. This provides evidence that the ad-

3.5.  Fuzzy regression discontinuity design

Directly estimating equations (5) and (6) may not reveal an unbi-

if cov(𝑦𝑖, 𝜇𝑖) ≠ 0 or cov(𝑦𝑖, 𝜖𝑖) ≠ 0. For example, if risk-averse individuals 
tend to select both ﬁelds of study in which earnings are less volatile and 
less risky investment portfolios, a classic omitted variable problem will 
plague the estimation of the causal eﬀect of earnings risk on ﬁnancial 
holdings, causing an attenuation bias. To overcome this identiﬁcation 
problem, our analysis uses the admission discontinuity presented in Sec-
tion 2.2 as a source of exogenous variation in admission to programs of 
study.

savings in bank deposits and the amount invested in stocks and bonds,

institutions. We categorize as investments in “stocks” any investments

From these data, we create a measure of stock market participation as a 
dummy variable indicating whether the individual holds stocks in any 
of the years studied, as well as the ratio of total stocks held to total ﬁ-

5.1.  Eﬀect of program earnings process on individual earnings process

As the ﬁrst step in our analysis, we demonstrate how the program-
level earnings process aﬀects the individual’s earnings process. Table 3
presents the baseline results, and d’Astous and Shore (2021) present a 
battery of robustness tests to validate these eﬀects.20 Panel A shows 
the  instrumental  variable  estimation.  For  each  attribute  of  the  earn-
ings process, we estimate equation (6) by two-stage least-squares using 
equation (7) as the ﬁrst stage. We instrument each component of the 
earnings process using the analogous instruments presented in equa-
tion (8).21 Panel B shows the results estimated with OLS regressions. In 
both panels, the dependent variables are individual-level outcomes of 
the earnings process (e.g., excess earnings growth and volatilities) and 
the main explanatory variables are the various attributes of the earnings 
process, measured at the program level.

The earnings results presented in Table 3 show how individual-level 
earnings process attributes depend on program-level earnings process 
attributes.  First,  we  demonstrate  that  excess  earnings  are  caused  by 
programs of study: an additional $1 in excess program-level earnings is 
estimated to increase individual earnings by $0.53. In contrast, earnings 
growth is largely driven by self-selection. This can be seen by compar-
ing the IV and OLS estimates; for excess earnings, both estimates are 
similar and statistically signiﬁcant, whereas for earnings growth, the 
OLS estimate of 0.84 is highly signiﬁcant, while the IV estimate is not. 

Although the OLS results for excess earnings growth suggest a strong 
eﬀect of the program studied, the IV counterpart is insigniﬁcant, sug-
gesting that most of the OLS eﬀect is driven by the self-selection of 
students in the program.

In terms of earnings volatility, Panel A shows that total volatility 
is partly due to the program of study (an estimated coeﬃcient of 0.52 
compared to 0.88 for its OLS counterpart). This result can be better 
understood by decomposing total volatility into transitory and perma-
nent components. Transitory volatility is only partly due to programs 
of study (an estimated coeﬃcient of 0.55 compared to 1.03 for its OLS 
counterpart), a result similar to that of total volatility. However, per-
manent volatility is highly signiﬁcant with a coeﬃcient of 1, suggesting 
that the permanent earnings volatility of the program of study is causal, 
and carried on to students about one-for-one.22 This justiﬁes the use of 
program-level variation in permanent and transitory earnings volatility 
to investigate the role of earnings risk in ﬁnancial choices.23

5.2.2.  Individual-level earnings volatility eﬀects

An alternative identiﬁcation strategy uses the same admission dis-
continuities, as well as reach and safety program attributes, to instru-
ment for individual-level earnings risk. Because of the limited time series 
of data available, individual-level earnings attributes are noisier, which 
could lead to less precise estimation results. In the right panel of Ta-
ble 4, we replace the program-level earnings process attributes used 
in our main results with their individual-level analogs, using the same 
reach and safety program instruments as used previously. The results 
are qualitatively similar to the results using program-level attributes. 
For  stock  market  participation,  permanent  earnings  volatility  is  still 
signiﬁcant at the 1% level, with a marginal eﬀect of -3.56. Its eﬀect 
on the share of risky assets is signiﬁcant at the 10% level, with a similar 
marginal eﬀect of -2.05. This lower statistical signiﬁcance and attenua-
tion bias (compared to program-level estimates) is to be expected when 
measuring noisy individual-level earnings processes, due to limited time 
series income data.

When using individual-level earnings estimates to predict portfolio 
choices, the ﬁrst-stage results presented in the right panel of Table A.4
show a Hansen over-identiﬁcation p-value of 0.80, which again rules 
out that our instruments might be correlated with the error term. The 
F-stats range from 2 to 9, which reject the null of weak instruments 
at the 95% conﬁdence interval, but also suggest that the program-level 
instruments used for our individual-level estimates are not as strong. 
We investigate each instrumented variable further by running tests of 
weak  IV  robust  inference  following  the  work  by  Olea  and  Pﬂueger 
(2013); Pﬂueger and Wang (2015), and Finlay et al. (2013) in Table A.5.

baseline program-level estimates as well as the individual-level ones.

5.2.3.  Quantifying the eﬀect of earnings volatility on individual ﬁnancial 
holdings

To further illustrate the implications of our ﬁndings, we show how 
our results can be used to explain the gap in stock market participa-
tion between business and education majors. In terms of ﬁeld-speciﬁc 
averages, Table A.2 shows that, compared to education, business has 
higher excess log earnings (0.091 vs -0.025), higher permanent volatil-
ity (0.018 vs 0.006) and a higher stock market participation rate (0.40 
vs 0.21). We make use of the marginal eﬀects of excess earnings and 
permanent earnings volatility to decompose the participation gap be-
tween business and education majors into the causal eﬀects of earnings 
and earnings risk, and residual selection. First, given that permanent 
earnings volatility is 0.018 − 0.006 = 0.012 higher in business than in 
education, this would lead to a rate of stock market participation that 
is 7.8 percentage points lower (i.e., (0.018 − 0.006) × −6.50) in business 
than it is in education, attributable to permanent earnings risk, ceteris 
paribus.

Second,  given  that  excess  earnings  are  0.091 − (−0.025) = 0.116
higher in business, this would lead to a rate of stock market participa-
tion that is 6 percentage points higher (i.e., 0.116 × 0.51 = 0.06) in busi-
ness than in education, attributable to excess earnings, ceteris paribus. 
Therefore, based only on the eﬀects of earnings and their volatility, the 
stock market participation rate should be 0.078 − 0.06 = 0.018 lower in 
business. However, Table A.2 shows that stock market participation is 
0.40 − 0.21 = 0.19 higher in business than in education. Out of this gap, 
−0.078 comes  from  diﬀerences  in  permanent  earnings  volatility  and 
0.06 comes from excess earnings. The excess gap between these two 
majors is therefore 0.208, and is due to a mix of self-selection and the 
eﬀect of the program of study. This illustrates the fact that using the 
association between earnings risk and stock market participation will 
miss large oﬀsetting eﬀects of the program of study. This is consistent 
with Panel B of Table 4 which shows no association between permanent 
earnings risk and ﬁnancial holdings.

Another way of scaling our results is in terms of the share of the 
variation  in  portfolio  holdings  that  can  be  explained  by  education-
induced permanent earnings volatility. First, note that Table 2 shows 
that  the  cross-sectional  standard  deviations  of  individuals’  and  pro-
grams’ stock shares are 0.17 and 0.033, respectively. This implies that 
(0.033∕0.17)2 ≃ 3.8% of the variation in an individual’s stock shares can 
be explained by their program of study. Table 2 shows that the cross-
sectional standard deviation of programs’ permanent volatility is 0.008, 
and Table 4 shows that the coeﬃcient on the causal impact of perma-
nent volatility on stock share is -2. Therefore, 1% of the variation in 
individuals’ stock shares (i.e., (22 × 0.0082)∕0.172) — and 24% of the 
variation in programs’ stock shares (i.e., (22 × 0.0082)∕0.0332) — can 
be explained by the causal eﬀect of the program of study’s permanent 
earnings risk on stock shares, respectively.

5.3.  Heterogeneity

Theoretical models with risky labor income, such as Campbell and 
Viceira (2002), suggest that responses to an increase in labor income 
risk  will  vary  based  on  the  ratio  of  ﬁnancial  wealth  to  total  wealth 
(ﬁnancial wealth and human capital wealth combined), risk aversion, 
and age. As the ratio of ﬁnancial to total wealth increases, the impact 
of increasing labor income risk on ﬁnancial risk-taking should become 
smaller. This implies an in-sample prediction borne out by the data that 
individuals with relatively more ﬁnancial wealth should have smaller 
background risk eﬀects. It also implies an out-of-sample prediction or 
extrapolation that older individuals – who are largely absent from our 
identiﬁcation sample and would have relatively more ﬁnancial wealth 
– will have relatively smaller background risk eﬀects.

In Table 5, we extend the baseline results of Table 4 by interacting 
our main program-level earnings attributes with excess earnings (𝑢), ﬁ-
nancial wealth, the ratio of ﬁnancial wealth to total wealth, and age. We 
present the sample average of these characteristics in the last line of the

columns present the results with these interactions. Columns (2) and

background  risk  eﬀects  are  larger  for  individuals  with  higher  excess 
earnings. The marginal eﬀect on the interaction of permanent volatil-

-8.13 for the risky share, conditional on participation. The eﬀects are 
signiﬁcant at the 5% and 1% level, respectively, and show that individ-
uals with higher levels of human capital reduce their exposure to risky 
assets more in response to an increase in permanent earnings volatil-

in calibrating the structural model and its out-of-sample predictions in

tively less important. Our noisy results are unsurprising since we have 
little age variation in our data to exploit.

Although our empirical setting presents limited age variation (most 
individuals apply to university around the same age), our sample oﬀers 
signiﬁcant diﬀerences in the relative importance of ﬁnancial and human 
capital wealth. In line with theoretical predictions, we observe larger 
background risk eﬀects for individuals with higher labor income and 
a lower share of ﬁnancial wealth. We infer from this that background 
risk eﬀects would likely be smaller for workers older than those in our 
sample. This is because, as people age, the relative signiﬁcance of their 
ﬁnancial wealth and the present value of their labor income naturally 
shift, with ﬁnancial wealth gaining importance over time. Furthermore, 
earnings volatility typically decreases as individuals enter middle age 
(Low et al., 2010), leading to reduced labor income risk and, conse-
quently, smaller background risk eﬀects for older workers compared to 
those in our sample.

5.4.  Human capital betas

When earnings comove with stock market returns, human capital 
betas can be created to measure the relationship (e.g., Guvenen et al., 
2017; Addoum et al., 2019). If individuals consider the comovement of 
their earnings and stock market returns when making portfolio choices, 
they will tend to shy away from risky assets when their returns comove 
with their own earnings. We test for this relationship in our data, but 
ﬁnd no evidence that programs of study have a causal eﬀect on human 
capital betas. As such, the admission discontinuities we study do not 
provide convincing variation in program-level variation in human cap-
ital beta, and therefore we do not ﬁnd that they aﬀect portfolio choices 
in our sample.

We  nonetheless  present  our  results  in  Appendix  Table A.7.  We 
ﬁrst  calculate  annual  program-level  comovement  between  real  earn-
ings growth (Δ𝑢𝑖𝑡) and stock market returns and investigate its eﬀect 
on individuals’ own human capital beta. We use both the Danish OMX 
C20 and the S&P 500 indices in percentage and log returns, and mea-
sure the average correlation 8 to 12 years after application. In Panel I, 
we show that cross-program variation in human capital betas does not 
explain individual-level human capital betas.29 In Panel II, we show 
that instrument program-level human capital betas do not aﬀect stock 
market participation or the portfolio share of risky assets. Finally, in 
Section 5.5, we also investigate the impact of controlling for human 
capital beta when measuring the eﬀect of permanent earnings volatility 
and ﬁnd that it does not aﬀect our main ﬁndings.

5.5.2.  Alternative mechanisms

Program-level stock market participation. Next, we investigate 
potential alternative mechanisms that could explain our ﬁndings. We 
focus our presentation on the results using program-level measures in 
Table A.10,  and  Table A.11 presents  the  individual-level  equivalent. 
Our baseline ﬁndings of Table 4 are reproduced and bolded in columns 
(1) and (6). We ﬁrst explicitly consider the probability that the share 
of individuals participating in the stock market in the program of study 
could inﬂuence our results through peer eﬀects or knowledge of the 
stock market. To rule out this possibility, we instrument the share of 
individuals  in  the  student’s  program  that  invest  in  the  stock  market 
using  our  same  identiﬁcation  strategy  and  instruments.  Columns  (2) 
and (7) both show that, although the program-level share of individuals 
that participate in the stock market has an eﬀect on participation and 
the share of risky assets held, this eﬀect is not suﬃcient to signiﬁcantly 
change our results, which are both still signiﬁcant at the 1% level.

Program-level human capital betas. In Section 5.4, we show that 
the human capital beta is not strongly caused by programs of study, nor 
does it predict stock market participation or the share of risky assets in 
our estimation strategy. To investigate the robustness of our results to 
their inclusion, we therefore control for the non-instrumented version. 
In columns (3) and (8) of Tables A.10 and A.11 present econometric 
speciﬁcations that control for human capital beta, and we ﬁnd that our 
main ﬁndings are still unaﬀected by such inclusion. This provides novel 
evidence of the role of earnings risk in portfolio choices, even when 
holding ﬁx human capital betas.

Program-level ﬁnancial wealth. Next,  we consider the possibil-
ity that graduates from low-risk programs might accumulate ﬁnancial 
wealth faster than their peers in riskier programs, which could drive 
the rise in their equity share. To rule out this possibility, we instru-
ment ﬁnancial wealth of individuals in the student’s program using our 
same identiﬁcation strategy and instruments. Columns (4) and (9) of 
Table A.10 show that the inclusion of this variable does not alter our 
ﬁndings, so that the level of ﬁnancial wealth cannot be driving our ﬁnd-
ings.

Other program characteristics. Another potential mechanism that 
could  explain  our  ﬁndings  relates  to  unobserved  program  character-
istics.  This  would  be  a  potential  concern  in  our  analysis  if  program 
rankings by prospective students or other aspects of the reach and safety 
programs correlate with permanent earnings volatility to drive the re-
sults. In columns (5) and (10) of Table A.10 we therefore include ﬁxed 
eﬀects  for  each  prospective  student’s  reach  and  safety  programs,  ef-

fectively  controlling  for  all  constant  eﬀects  of  both  programs.30 The 
eﬀects are 12% to 15% smaller, but are both statistically signiﬁcant 
when testing at the 5% level. This provides evidence that other pro-
gram characteristics cannot explain our main ﬁndings. Together, these 
results therefore suggest that variation of permanent earnings volatility 
across programs of study is driving the main eﬀect documented here.31

5.5.3.  Alternative earnings process assumptions

Next, we investigate the robustness of our ﬁndings to assumptions 
about the earnings process. Our main results are based on excess earn-
ings measured as the residuals from equation (2), averaged 8 to 12 years 
after enrollment. In Table A.12 we show the results for program- and 
individual-level earnings measures, using raw and excess earnings, and 
alternatively averaging the attributes of the earnings process 8 to 15 
years after enrollment. Panels A to D, and Panels E to H show robust-
ness to these alternative assumptions as they relate to our ﬁndings in 
Table 4. Our main conclusions remain unchanged with these diﬀerent 
assumptions.

Our earnings attributes are estimated over the full sample period to 
maximize the precision of our estimates. However, one concern about 
this approach is that earnings attributes potentially contain informa-
tion on future earnings attributes of the program that workers couldn’t 
observe at the time they apply to University. As an additional test to 
address this concern, in Table A.13 we re-estimate our program-level 
variables using only the information available to individuals up to their 
own cohort-application year. We show that our results are qualitatively 
unchanged. Because we use fewer data points to calculate our program-
level variables in this context, the results are noisier than our baseline 
results.  Nonetheless,  our  interpretation  is  unaﬀected  by  this  change, 
suggesting limited concerns about using future information in our base-
line results.

To understand why our model calibration implies that consumers 
are highly myopic, consider that ﬁnancial wealth is negligible for most 
individuals in our sample compared to the present value of their life-
time human capital wealth. Since ﬁnancial wealth represents only a tiny 
fraction of total wealth for most consumers in the model, even a very 
small equity premium is enough to push non-myopic consumers with 
reasonable risk aversion to hold highly leveraged risky asset positions. 
Models without myopia would therefore predict counterfactually high 
risky asset shares and high stock market participation rates. However, 
individuals in our sample have only a 25% risky share on average. When 
consumers are myopic, this increases the relative importance of ﬁnan-
cial wealth as a share of total wealth. Myopia reduces the perceived 
value of human capital wealth, so it lowers the optimal risky asset share 
to observed levels (without resorting to extremely high levels of risk 
aversion that would lead the risky asset share to be counterfactually 
sensitive to changes in labor earnings risk).

Another option to lower the optimal risky share to observed levels 
would be make consumers pessimistic, for example by introducing a 
perceived disaster risk that lowers the expected excess return on risky 
assets to nearly zero (e.g. Fagereng et al., 2017). While this approach 
could be used to calibrate the model to the observed average risky as-
set share, it ﬁts the IV-estimated gradients of the risky asset share with 
respect to human capital risk less well.

predictions

In  Fig. 3,  we  compare  the  moments  predicted  by  our  calibrated 
model to our targeted and untargeted moments. The left panel presents 
the results for (a) the risky asset share and the right panel presents the 
results for (b) the stock market participation rate. In each ﬁgure, the 
dashed line shows the model’s prediction; prediction lines can be com-
pared with the solid line and conﬁdence band showing the 95th percent 
conﬁdence interval on the IV-derived estimates that the model seeks to 
fall within.

The middle row compares calibrated model predictions to the tar-
geted moments that are evaluated at sample average earnings. The mo-
ments predicted by our calibrated model are very close to the targets 
derived from the IV point estimates and are well within the conﬁdence 
bands.

The ﬁrst and third rows compare calibrated model predictions to 
untargeted moments evaluated, for low labor earnings (the 25th per-

labor  earnings  (the  75th  percentile  of  the  program-level  average  in-

the 95th percent conﬁdence bands derived from our IV estimates, sug-
gesting that the model ﬁts these untargeted moments as well as the 
targeted ones.

We would expect background risk eﬀects stemming from risky la-
bor earnings to be more important for individuals with higher labor 
earnings (holding wealth ﬁxed). As a result, we would predict the gra-
dient of the risky asset share (and stock market participation rate) to be 
steeper for those with higher labor earnings. This qualitative prediction 
is visible in Fig. 3, where the lines (both estimated in the data and pre-
dicted by the model) become steeper as they move from lower to higher 
labor earnings. The model not only matches this prediction of increased 
gradient with earning qualitatively, it matches quantitatively as well. 
However, the gradient becomes steeper with earnings at a somewhat 
higher rate in the IV estimates than in the model; point estimates from 
the IV suggest a somewhat larger role for the level of earnings in ef-
fecting background risk than the model does, though well within the 
conﬁdence interval.

For our targeted moments evaluated at average earnings, our em-
pirical  results  imply  drop  in  the  risky  asset  share  from  26.9%  when 
labor earnings risk is low (volatility at 0.005) down to 23.2% when la-
bor earnings risk is high (volatility at 0.025). We match this targeted 
slope of -185% ((23.2%-26.9%)/(0.025-0.005) very well with our cal-
ibrated slope of -185%. Our empirical results show that this slope is 
ﬂatter (-52%) when earnings are low and steeper (-318%) when earn-
ings are high; the risky asset share is more responsive to changes in 
labor  earnings  risk  for  those  with  more  labor  earnings.  Our  calibra-
tion matches this pattern qualitatively, but shows a smaller diﬀerence 
in slopes between the low-earning and high-earning cases (-145% and 
-221%, respectively). Low- and high-earning people in our sample dif-
fer more in their responsiveness to labor earnings risk than would be 
implied by our calibrated model. However, the standard errors in our 
empirical ﬁndings are large enough that we cannot reject the hypothesis 
that the untargeted calibrated slopes fall within our conﬁdence inter-
vals.

7.  Conclusion

In this paper, we study the eﬀect of earnings volatility on portfolio 
choices. Because career and investment choices are jointly determined, 
we use an identiﬁcation strategy to isolate exogenous variation in earn-
ings  risk.  We  exploit  an  institutional  feature  of  the  Danish  National 
University system which allows us to study a sample of students who 
are quasi-randomly assigned to diﬀerent programs of study that vary in 
the subsequent earnings proﬁles of their enrollees. d’Astous and Shore 
(2021) show  that  permanent  earnings  volatility  passes  through  from 
programs of study to individuals one-for-one. We use this source of vari-
ation in earnings volatility to study its eﬀect on ﬁnancial decisions.

We show that individuals with exogenously higher earnings volatil-
ity are less likely to hold risky ﬁnancial assets. This is consistent with 
the theory of background risk that an exogenous increase in idiosyn-
cratic, uninsurable labor income risk should lead individuals to reduce 
the risk in their ﬁnancial portfolios (Kimball, 1993). The eﬀects that 
we estimate are fairly large. A one-standard-deviation increase in per-
manent volatility due to the program of study decreases an individual’s 
stock market participation by 23% and, conditional on participation, 
decreases the share of risky assets held by 8%. However, oﬀsetting ef-
fects of excess earnings, self-selection and program of study obscure this 
relationship in raw correlations. We present evidence that this ﬁnding 
is robust to considering human capital betas.

Our causal estimates of the elasticities of portfolio choices with re-
spect to earnings risk provide additional moments that can be used to 
pin down the underlying parameters of a standard investment problem. 
This model is able to match untargeted moments exploiting heterogene-
ity in model estimates with respect to the level of earnings with modest 
participation costs, myopic behavior, and reasonable levels of risk aver-
sion.
