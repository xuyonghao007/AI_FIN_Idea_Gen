The real and financial effects of internal liquidity: Evidence from the Tax Cuts
and Jobs Act✩
James F. Albertus a,∗, Brent Glover a, Oliver Levine b
a Carnegie Mellon University, Tepper School of Business, 5000 Forbes Avenue, Pittsburgh, PA, 15213, USA

A R T I C L E I N F O

A B S T R A C T

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act unlocked as much as $1.7 trillion of U.S. multinationals’ foreign cash. We examine
the real and financial response to this liquidity shock and find that firms did not increase capital expenditures,
employment, R&D, or M&A, regardless of financial constraints. On the financial side, firms paid out only about
one-third of the new liquidity to shareholders and retained half as cash. This high retention was not associated
with poor governance. The high propensity to retain the liquidity shock as cash, even among well-governed
firms with limited financial constraints, is difficult to reconcile with existing theory.

On January 1, 2018, the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) came into
effect, changing several features of the corporate tax code. Among its
key provisions was unlocking ‘‘trapped cash’’ that U.S. multinationals
had accumulated in their foreign subsidiaries. Prior to the TCJA, the
U.S. corporate tax code incentivized multinationals to defer repatria-
tion of foreign income, leading to large balances of accumulated cash
and financial securities (Foley et al., 2007; Faulkender et al., 2019).
With the changes imposed by the TCJA, firms suddenly had access to
additional internal capital at zero marginal cost, funds that previously
had been extremely costly to use for U.S. investment or payouts. In

aggregate, the law increased liquidity of U.S. firms by providing access
to $1.7 trillion in foreign cash (Bureau of Economic Analysis, 2020).

In this paper, we evaluate how this sudden and significant increase
in  internal  liquidity,  brought  about  by  the  TCJA,  affected  real  and
financial decisions of U.S. multinationals. Although the tax law applied
to all U.S. corporations, firms had differential exposure to this policy
change  based  on  their  accumulated  stock  of  liquid  foreign  profits.
We  use  confidential  data  on  cash  and  marketable  security  holdings
at foreign subsidiaries of U.S. multinationals (hereafter, foreign cash)
collected by the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) to measure each

In addition, we explore how firms adjusted their payouts, cash holdings,
and leverage in response to the decreased cost of accessing their foreign
cash  holdings.  Our  results  provide  insight  into  how  liquidity  relates
to firm decisions and whether the TCJA was successful in stimulating
U.S. investment and hiring by unlocking firms’ foreign cash. On the
financial side, it reveals the degree to which firms actively adjust their
cash holdings, shareholder payouts, and debt positions in response to
internal liquidity shocks.

We find no evidence that firms responded to the liquidity shock
by  increasing  investment  in  the  U.S.:  the  increased  liquidity  led  to
no  change  in  capital  expenditure,  wage  expense,  or  R&D.  Similarly,
we find no effect on foreign real activity.1 Theory predicts that real
investment should increase if the firm is financially constrained or faces
agency conflicts. However, we do not find that the most constrained
firms responded differently than the least constrained. Similarly, the
response does not vary with the strength of governance. This evidence
is consistent with our sample of firms being neither materially finan-
cially constrained nor poorly governed. In addition, we do not find a
differential response for firms in industries more amenable to voluntary
profit shifting. Overall, our results indicate that a lack of liquidity did
not constrain the real decisions of firms, and unlocking trapped cash
likely  did  not  stimulate  investment  and  hiring  as  proponents  of  the
policy had predicted.2 Our findings also contrast with early evidence of
a positive relationship between trapped cash and investment following
the TCJA (Beyer et al., 2021; Olson, 2021; Markarian and Crawford,
2022). However, our study is unique in terms of identification strategy
and data sources.3

We next ask whether increased internal liquidity led to increased
acquisition  activity.  Similar  to  other  investment  decisions,  increased
liquidity could decrease the cost of capital (Myers, 1984) or exacerbate
agency conflicts (Jensen, 1986), with each channel predicting increased
M&A  activity.  Indeed,  the  merger  literature  has  demonstrated  that
both firm-level and macro liquidity are significant drivers of merger
activity (Harford, 1999, 2005). Specific to our setting, previous studies
have shown that trapped cash was associated with increased foreign
acquisitions prior to the TCJA, and that these acquisitions had lower
announcement  returns  (Hanlon  et  al., 2015; Edwards  et  al., 2016).
However, we find no evidence that firms responded to the liquidity
shock  by  adjusting  acquisition  activity  either  in  the  U.S.  or  abroad.
Further,  we  find  a  similar  non-response  for  firms  that  have  weak
governance. While the findings of Harford et al. (2017) and Albertus
et al. (2022) suggest an important role of agency conflicts in the firm’s
use of foreign cash, our results do not provide clear evidence of these
conflicts.

How  did  firms  adjust  their  cash  positions  and  shareholder  pay-
outs in response to the increase in internal liquidity? Traditional pre-
cautionary savings theory predicts that for a firm that is financially
unconstrained—whether  due  to  high  cash  holdings  or  low  cost  of
external  financing—the  marginal  value  of  liquidity  approaches  one:
shareholders  value  a  dollar  of  cash  the  same  inside  the  firm  or  in
their pockets (Riddick and Whited, 2009; Bolton et al., 2011). Add to
this any cost of holding cash (e.g., agency costs or cost of carry) and
shareholders prefer any additional liquidity to be paid out.

Indeed,  existing  empirical  evidence  suggests  that  unconstrained
firms  would  respond  to  liquidity  shocks  by  paying  it  out  to  share-
holders rather than retaining it (Dharmapala, 2024). Notably, Almeida
et al. (2004) finds that unconstrained firms do not retain cash inflows.
Similarly, Dharmapala  et  al. (2011)  finds  that  92%  of  repatriations
unlocked by the American Jobs Creation Act (AJCA) in 2005 were used
to make shareholder payouts rather than being retained as cash. From
this perspective, precautionary savings theory predicts that the cash
unlocked by the TCJA should be paid out if these firms are financially
unconstrained.

In contrast, we find that for each dollar of foreign cash that was
freed by the TCJA, only about 30 cents was paid out to shareholders,
primarily as share repurchases. This coincided with a 48 cent reduction
in consolidated cash holdings. In addition, we do not find a signifi-
cant response in firm leverage, suggesting that firms did not use this
increased liquidity to pay down debt. Together, our findings reveal a
high  propensity  of  seemingly  unconstrained  firms  to  retain  liquidity
shocks as cash. The low payout and high cash retention we document
is difficult to rationalize as a traditional precautionary savings motive
for cash holdings.

To understand this result, we next consider potential frictions that
may be driving the low payout and high retention of cash following the
tax reform. First, firms may have held cash in their foreign subsidiaries
for operational reasons, rather than due to a tax lockout effect. To test
this possibility, we measure the liquidity shock as only the portion of
foreign cash that is in excess of that required to support foreign opera-
tions. We find similar results using this alternative measure, suggesting
that the small response is not due to the inclusion of foreign cash used
for  ongoing  operations.  Second,  we  consider  whether  managers  are
retaining the freed cash for the private benefits of control. We find no
evidence that the financial response is due to agency conflicts: better
governed  firms  reduce  cash  and  increase  payouts  at  the  same  level
as poorly governed firms. Third, it is possible that adjusting the cash
position and payout levels takes longer than two years, the observation
window used in our benchmark sample. To test this, we extend our
sample by two years through 2021. We do not find that adjustments to
cash holdings, or elevated shareholder payouts, continue to occur more
than two years following the reform. Taken together, the low payout
and high cash retention in response to the liquidity increase does not
appear to result from cash held for operations, agency conflicts, or a
delay in adjustment.

Our evidence of high retention of new liquidity by financially un-
constrained firms stands in tension with traditional precautionary sav-
ings theory, which suggests that unconstrained firms should pay out the
new liquidity. However, our findings are consistent with what Graham
and Leary (2018) describe as firms choosing to only ‘‘loosely’’ adhere
to cash targets. As evidence of this loose adherence, Graham and Leary
(2018) document that about half of the time-series variation in cash
holdings is explained by contemporaneous cash flows and investment
expenditures, consistent with only a partial adjustment in cash holdings
in  response  to  liquidity  shocks.  This  parallels  evidence  of  minimal
active  adjustment  of  debt  in  response  to  shocks  to  market  leverage
documented in Welch (2004). In the case of leverage adjustment, the
muted response may be explained by fixed costs of adjustments (Fischer
et al., 1989). However, in the case of cash holdings, this is an unlikely
explanation, as paying out excess cash is not particularly costly.

One possible explanation that reconciles our findings with precau-
tionary savings theory is that cash may act as a type of collateral for
firm borrowing both before and after the reform. In addition to the
usefulness of cash in satisfying short-term liquidity needs, Gao et al.
(2021) show that firms may hold cash to lower the cost of borrowing by
reducing the likelihood of—and increasing recovery in—default. This
role for cash persists even after the cash was unlocked by the TCJA,
potentially making it suboptimal to fully pay out these holdings.

Another  possible  explanation  is  that  optimal  cash  holdings  may
be roughly indeterminate following the TCJA for firms in our sample
because the marginal costs (i.e., costs of carry and agency) and benefits
(i.e., precautionary savings) are both likely quite small for these firms.4
In other words, firms may have had strong tax incentives to accumulate
cash prior to reform due to the repatriation tax, but little compelling
reason to adjust cash downward following the reform. This weak in-
centive to adjust following reform could present as a loose cash target,
but nevertheless be consistent with precautionary savings theory.

Our results provide new insight into firms’ elevated cash holdings
over the last several decades (Bates et al., 2009).5 Several papers—
using data prior to the TCJA—have shown that tax incentives are an
important driver of firms’ cash holdings, and in particular foreign cash
holdings, since the beginning of the century (Foley et al., 2007; Gu,
2017; Graham  and  Leary, 2018; Faulkender  et  al., 2019; Darmouni
and  Mota, 2024).  We  find  that  firms  did  reduce  cash  holdings  in
response  to  the  removal  of  the  repatriation  tax,  consistent  with  tax
incentives  contributing  to  firms’  accumulation  of  cash.  At  the  same
time,  the  modest  magnitude  of  the  downward  adjustment  could  be
interpreted as evidence that factors beyond tax incentives are important
to understanding patterns in cash holdings over the last two decades.
From this perspective, our findings are consistent with Pinkowitz et al.
(2016), who challenge a prominent view in the literature by asserting
that repatriation taxes have only a limited ability to explain the high
cash holdings of U.S. firms.

We contribute to a large literature seeking to understand the effect
of liquidity on firm investment and financial policy (e.g., Fazzari et al.,
1988; Almeida  et  al., 2004).  The  setting  provided  by  the  TCJA  tax
reform provides several advantages that help to overcome the empirical
challenges inherent in answering these questions (Kaplan and Zingales,
1997; Erickson  and  Whited, 2000; Gomes, 2001; Alti, 2003).  First,
the  TCJA  liquidity  shock  is  firm-specific,  generating  cross-sectional
variation  in  the  treatment  intensity.  This  also  allows  for  the  inclu-
sion of firm fixed effects in our regression specification, substantially
controlling for unobserved firm heterogeneity. Second, our approach
controls for average industry-year variation in the outcome variables
resulting from other provisions of the TCJA—such as the reduction in
the U.S. corporate income tax rate—allowing us to isolate the effect of
liquidity changes. Third, the freeing of unrepatriated foreign earnings
was mandatory, and therefore there are no endogenous selection effects
driven by a firm’s decision to participate in taxable repatriation. This
contrasts with the AJCA, a previous voluntary repatriation program.

A potential concern with our estimation approach is that our treat-
ment variable—the quantity of foreign cash prior to the reform—may
be correlated with the change in investment opportunities associated
with the reform. Specifically, firms that had more foreign cash unlocked
by the TCJA (e.g., due to higher past foreign profitability) may have
also benefited more from a separate provision of the reform that elim-
inated U.S. taxes on foreign operations going forward.6 It is important
to note that this potential omitted variable bias would tend to overstate
the  effect,  of  which  we  find  none,  mitigating  this  concern ex  post.
Nevertheless, we consider this potential bias by constructing a firm-
specific proxy for the effect of the TCJA on investment opportunities.
In particular, we estimate the wedge between U.S. and foreign tax rates
for each firm’s foreign operations: a higher value indicates that a firm
may have a higher increase in post-TCJA investment opportunities than
a firm with a lower wedge. We find that our results are unchanged with
the inclusion of this control, providing evidence that our treatment vari-
able is not acting as a proxy for post-reform investment opportunities.7
This is consistent with Garcia-Bernardo et al. (2022), who find that
the TCJA did not significantly alter the geographical allocation of the
foreign profits of U.S. multinationals. Further, we provide a dynamic
analysis  showing  parallel  trends  in  investment  and  payouts  prior  to
treatment.

This paper relates to previous work that used a 2005 temporary
repatriation tax holiday to determine how repatriated foreign cash was
used by U.S. multinationals. The tax holiday, part of the AJCA, allowed
firms  to  voluntarily  repatriate  funds,  under  certain  restrictions,  at  a
reduced tax rate. Dharmapala et al. (2011) finds that each dollar of
increased  repatriation  was  associated  with  up  to  a  92  cent  increase
in shareholder payouts, and found no effect on real investment even
among constrained firms. In contrast, Faulkender and Petersen (2012)
finds  a  small  or  insignificant  increase  in  payouts  while  real  invest-
ment increased among constrained firms. One of the primary empirical
challenges of exploiting this 2005 program is that participation was
voluntary,  leading  to  an  endogenous  selection  effect.  These  studies
differ in their approach to addressing this selection problem, potentially
leading to their contrasting results.8 Our environment avoids this con-
cern altogether because participation was mandatory for all firms.9 Our
findings contrast with these previous studies in that firms responded to
the increased liquidity with only a modest increase in payouts and no
increase in investment. In addition, neither study explores the effect on
firms’ cash holdings, where we find that firms choose to only partially

1.  Data

To understand firms’ response to a liquidity shock provided by new
access to their foreign cash, we require high quality non-consolidated
data on firms’ domestic and foreign operations and financial positions.
Unfortunately, such data are not publicly available. For example, data
in  Compustat’s  geographic  segment  file  are  reported  inconsistently
across firms and are usually limited to information on sales. To address
the shortcoming of publicly available data, one of the authors applied
for and was granted access to the confidential income statements and
balance sheets of U.S. multinationals’ domestic and foreign operations,
maintained on secure government servers at the Bureau of Economic
Analysis.

We construct our dataset from BEA’s annual and benchmark surveys
of the Activities of U.S. Multinational Enterprises.11 The surveys collect
separate information on both the domestic and foreign operations of
U.S.  multinationals  and  are  conducted  pursuant  to  the  International
Investment and Trade in Services Survey Act (hereafter the Act). The
Act stipulates that the ‘‘use of an individual company’s data for tax,
investigative, or regulatory purposes is prohibited’’. Willful noncom-
pliance with the Act may result in imprisonment for up to one year.
For these reasons, in addition to their monitoring of corporate events
and a system of internal data integrity checks, BEA believes the surveys
accurately capture virtually complete data on the universe of U.S. direct
investment abroad.

We limit the sample to majority-owned affiliates, which are com-
monly  referred  to  as  ‘‘subsidiaries’’,  the  term  used  in  this  paper.  In
addition, we omit firms in the financial services (NAICS 5200–5299),
utility  (NAICS  2200-2299),  and  public  administration  (NAICS  9200-
9299) industries. We also require that firms have at least one subsidiary
in a country with a corporate income tax rate below the U.S. rate prior
to 2018. We merge these data with Compustat, SDC, and FactSet to in-
corporate information on stock repurchases, mergers and acquisitions,
and institutional equity ownership, respectively. We start the sample
in  2010  to  include  several  years  prior  to  the  TCJA  while  avoiding
the turbulence of the financial crisis. In the baseline analysis we end
the sample in 2019, which is the last year for which BEA’s data are
available. The baseline sample consists of 3146 firm-year observations,
corresponding to 489 unique firms. For certain analyses that rely on
consolidated  (Compustat)  outcomes,  we  extend  the  sample  through
2021.

2.  Empirical design

We study a provision of the TCJA that eliminated the repatriation
tax on U.S. multinational firms’ accumulated foreign profits. With this
change, firms suddenly had access to additional internal capital at zero
marginal cost, funds that previously had been extremely costly to use
for U.S. investment or payouts.12

12 Although access to the additional capital became costless on the margin
following the reform, the law did impose a sunk cost on firms. Specifically,
prior  to  the  TCJA,  firms  paid  U.S.  taxes  on  their  foreign  earnings  upon
repatriation. Repatriation taxes could be deferred indefinitely by keeping the
profits in their foreign subsidiaries and not using the funds for U.S. investment
or shareholder payouts. The TCJA imposed a one-time, mandatory ‘‘transition
tax’’ on all previously unrepatriated earnings, automatically freeing all foreign
cash. The legislation provided a tax holiday on this deferred tax liability in
that the transition tax rate on foreign financial assets, at 15.5% before foreign
tax credits, was significantly lower than the (voluntary) repatriation tax rate
prior to 2018. The legislation also removed repatriation taxes going forward.
See Dobridge et al. (2023) and Dharmapala (2024) for details on the provisions
of the TCJA.

The  TCJA,  as  legislation,  emerged  rapidly  and  was  passed  and
implemented in short order. The earliest draft of the text was intro-
duced  in  the  U.S.  House  of  Representatives  on  November  2,  2017.
The final bill was signed into law on December 22, 2017, and became
effective less than two months after it was introduced on January 1,
2018. Earlier in 2017 there was discussion of tax reform, but whether
it  would  happen,  when,  and  what  would  be  included  in  a  possible

As a result, in conjunction with the fixed effects and control variables
described below, we believe the TCJA generated a plausibly exogenous
shock to firms’ internal liquidity.

Critically, our baseline specification includes firm fixed effects (𝛼𝑖).
These  remove  unobserved  firm-level  heterogeneity  that  may  be  cor-
related  with  foreign  cash  accumulation  and  drive  investment  deci-
sions. In addition, industry-by-year fixed effects (𝛿𝑗 ,𝑡), where industry
is  categorized  by  four-digit  NAICS  codes,  help  control  for  potential
industry-specific responses to the implementation of the TCJA.

Since one of our primary goals is to identify the effect of liquid-
ity increases on real investment, we must ensure that our treatment
intensity measure does not act as a proxy for unobserved investment
opportunities. Firm and industry-by-year fixed effects control for both
(1) time-invariant cross-sectional differences in investment opportuni-
ties, and (2) industry-average changes in investment opportunities over
time, including those brought about by the TCJA. These controls largely
address several concerns, including that the TCJA included a tax rate
cut on U.S. income, potentially improving after-tax investment returns
for all firms. Still, there may be a firm-specific change in investment
opportunities brought about by the TCJA. If (and only if) this firm-
specific change is correlated with pre-TCJA foreign cash accumulation,
and is omitted from the specification, then this could bias our estimates.
It is worth noting that this omitted variable bias would generally
overstate the response of investment to the liquidity increase because
foreign cash is likely to be positively correlated with the firm-specific
increase in foreign investment opportunities. For example, a firm that
generates most of its income in a low-tax country would benefit more
from the removal of the repatriation tax provided by the TCJA.15

Accordingly, we re-estimate our main specifications omitting all con-
trols. We discuss the results, which are qualitatively and quantitatively
similar to the baseline, in Section 3.

advantages in our setting relative to alternative measures. One mea-

firm chooses to designate, for accounting purposes, as ‘‘permanently

subsidiaries, as cash or real assets. In contrast, our measure ensures
that we are capturing cash and financial assets, rather than illiquid real
investments, that truly enhance a firm’s liquidity position, regardless of
the firm’s chosen PRE accounting designation.19

3.  Results

In  this  section,  we  first  estimate  the  effect  of  the  TCJA-induced
increase in internal liquidity on firm’s real investment decisions, in-
cluding capital expenditures, employment, research and development
(R&D), and mergers and acquisitions (M&A). We consider whether fi-
nancial constraints or agency conflicts play a role in this response. Next,
we explore the response in the financial positions of firms following
the unlocking of their foreign cash, including changes in payouts, cash
holdings, and leverage.

Before proceeding with our main empirical tests, we first explore
whether our treatment variable—a firm’s foreign cash ratio—predicts
subsidiary-to-parent repatriations. Following the TCJA, the distinction
between foreign and domestic cash was effectively nullified for U.S.
tax purposes, meaning that a firm could pay dividends from its for-
eign  subsidiaries  to  its  U.S.  parent  (i.e.,  a  repatriation)  without  tax
consequences in the U.S. If firms were holding cash in their foreign
subsidiaries due to the tax lockout created by repatriation taxes prior to
2018, we might expect to see foreign cash holdings partially reallocated
to U.S. parents.

Table 2 shows the results of our baseline regression using Eq. (1)
with subsidiary-to-parent repatriations as the outcome variable. Col-
umn (1) shows that 13 cents of each dollar of foreign cash was repatri-
ated annually in the two years following the reform, meaning roughly
one quarter of the foreign cash was repatriated. The year-by-year esti-
mates, using Eq. (2), are reported in Column (2), indicating that consid-
erable repatriation occurs each year post-TCJA. The increase in repa-
triations following reform can also clearly be seen in the unconditional
dollar amount of repatriations shown in Fig. 1.20

The reallocation of cash from foreign subsidiaries to U.S. parents
provides suggestive evidence that taxes played a role in firms’ decision
to accumulate foreign cash. We next explore how the freed foreign cash
affected the real and financial decisions of firms.

3.1.  Real economic activity

Did removing the tax lockout on foreign cash stimulate investment
and employment by U.S. multinationals? We assess the effect of the
increased liquidity on firms’ real activity by estimating the difference-
in-differences specification in Eq. (1). Table 3 reports the results for
domestic (U.S.) investment and employment. We define investment as
capital expenditures and employment as wage expense, each scaled by
lagged assets.

Columns (1) and (3) report the results for the average treatment
effect in the two-year post period. Across both measures of U.S. real
activity, we find no significant response by firms to the increased access
to foreign cash. Contrary to the hopes of some TCJA proponents, these
results indicate that the reform likely did not have a discernible effect
on the real decisions of firms in our sample. The findings suggest that
these firms are not meaningfully constrained financially and thus the
increased liquidity did not materially decrease the cost of capital.

3.1.1.  Financial constraints, agency conflicts, and profit shifting

We find that firms do not respond to increased access to liquidity
by  increasing  capital  expenditures,  employment,  or  R&D.  However,
basic financial theory predicts that investment would increase if the
firm is financially constrained (Stein, 1997; Giroud and Mueller, 2015),
whereby an increase in internal capital would lower the cost of capital.
In  contrast,  investment  would  not  increase  for  unconstrained  firms.
We explore this possibility by testing whether the investment response
depends on financial constraints.

Financial  constraints  are  notoriously  difficult  to  measure  (Farre-
Mensa and Ljungqvist, 2016). With this caveat, we rely on the measure
of Hadlock  and  Pierce (2010)  based  on  firm  size  and  age.  We  aug-
ment the baseline regression in Eq. (1) to include the interaction with
financial constraints, specifically:

A positive estimate for 𝛽0 would indicate that more financially con-

Table 6 reports the results from estimating Eq. (3). For all outcome

In addition to financial constraints, misaligned incentives between
managers  and  shareholders  may  lead  to  an  increase  in  real  activity

in Eq. (3) using a proxy for the strength of corporate governance in the
place of financial constraints. We proxy for corporate governance using
the fraction of equity ownership of the top 5 institutional investors,
as  defined  by Ferreira  and  Matos (2008).  The  results  are  reported
in Table  7.  For  U.S.  investment,  U.S.  employment,  and  foreign  em-

conflicts  are  not  an  important  driver  of  real  activity  in  response  to
increased access to cash. For foreign investment, an outcome with no
clear prediction, the interaction term is statistically significant at the
10% level, but is economically small.22

3.1.2.  Mergers and acquisitions

We next consider another form of real economic activity: acquisi-
tions. For reasons similar to those for other real outcomes, increased
access  to  liquidity  may  lead  to  increased  acquisition  activity.  This
could include decreased cost of capital, as well as exacerbated agency
conflicts such as empire building or free cash flow problems (Jensen,
1986; Harford, 1999). Relatedly, Hanlon et al. (2015) finds that trapped
foreign cash led to an increase in foreign acquisitions made with cash,
and that these acquisitions had lower announcement returns.

We test whether increased access to cash led to an increase in acqui-
sition activity by estimating our baseline Eq. (1) with the dollar volume
of acquisitions, scaled by lagged assets, as the outcome variable. The
results are reported in Table 9. Similar to Hanlon et al. (2015), we
focus on acquisitions made with cash. Consistent with other forms of
real activity, we find no effect of increased access to liquidity on firm
M&A activity. This is true for both U.S. acquisitions, which are most
likely to be affected by the reform, as well as foreign acquisitions. These
results contrast with other work that indicates both acquisitions and
announcement returns are increasing in foreign cash (see Atwood et al.,
2020 and Amberger and Robinson, 2022, respectively). In contrast to
existing work, this study uses foreign cash data directly observed by
BEA, potentially contributing to the different findings.

In addition to a reduced cost of capital, agency conflicts—such as
empire building motives—may lead managers to engage in increased
acquisition  activity.  We  consider  the  role  of  agency  conflicts  by  in-
cluding an interaction with our measure of the strength of corporate
governance, the equity ownership of the top-5 institutional investors.
The results are reported in Table 10. For both U.S. and foreign acquisi-
tion activity, we find no evidence that managers facing less institutional
oversight  responded  to  the  liquidity  shock  by  increasing  acquisition
activity.

We also consider whether the foreign cash freed by the TCJA was
used to pay down debt and reduce leverage. Prior to the TCJA, firms
may have used their trapped foreign cash to support borrowing in the
U.S.—financing  that  could  be  used  for  U.S.  payouts  and  investment
without restriction—as a way to circumvent the repatriation tax and
indirectly pay out foreign profits to U.S. shareholders (De Simone and
Lester, 2018). A post-reform decline in debt could reflect an unwinding
of those pre-TCJA tax-arbitrage positions. However, Columns (9) and
(10) of Table 11 show no significant change in book leverage following
the TCJA. This result suggests that firms did not use their freed foreign
cash to adjust their debt positions.27

We  next  consider  three  factors  that  may  affect  a  firm’s  financial
response to the freeing of its foreign cash. First, we consider whether
foreign cash was being held for operational reasons rather than purely
for tax reasons. We test this possibility by measuring the TCJA-induced
liquidity increase as the portion of foreign cash that is in excess of that
required  to  support  foreign  operations.  Excess  foreign  cash  is  deter-
mined in four steps. First, the ratio of cash to net property, plant, and
equipment is calculated for domestic only Compustat firms (i.e., non-
multinationals) in 2017. Second, the median of this ratio within 2-digit
NAICS industry bins is matched to each foreign subsidiary and multi-
plied by the subsidiary’s net property, plant, and equipment, to obtain
a predicted cash value. Third, this predicted cash value is subtracted
from the subsidiary’s cash holdings. If the difference is negative it is set
to zero. Fourth, these excess cash values are aggregated across a firm’s
foreign subsidiaries and scaled by consolidated assets. We denote this
variable 𝐸 𝑥𝐹 𝑜𝑟𝐶 𝑎𝑠ℎ.

We  measure  the  response  in  consolidated  cash  holdings,  repur-
chases, and payouts, by estimating Eq. (2) using the firms’ 2017 level
of excess foreign cash in place of total foreign cash. Table 12 reports
the results. For all three outcome variables, the coefficient estimates
using excess foreign cash are very similar to our baseline results. This
suggests  that  the  muted  response  in  payouts  and  consolidated  cash
holdings was unlikely due to the need to keep the freed cash in the
foreign subsidiaries for operational reasons.

Second, managers may receive private benefits from the firm’s cash
holdings  and  therefore  choose  to  retain  more  cash  than  is  optimal
from  the  perspective  of  shareholders.  Indeed, Harford  et  al. (2017)
finds  that  the  value  of  a  firm’s  foreign  cash  holdings  is  discounted
by  its  shareholders,  and Kim  et  al. (2023)  finds  that  shareholders
responded positively to a policy that pressured Korean firms to disgorge
excess cash. To test whether agency conflicts are associated with higher
retention of freed cash, we re-estimate the financial response with the
inclusion of an interaction with a proxy for corporate governance. As
institutional owners are likely able to exert pressure on management to
pay out excess cash, we again use the top-5 institutional ownership of
firms as a proxy for strength of governance. Table 13 reports the results
of the estimation. In all cases, there is no differential effect attributable
to institutional presence, providing no evidence that retention of cash
is due to agency conflicts.

Third, we assess whether the low payout of cash is due to a slow

Given  these  results,  a  question  remains:  why  was  less  than  one-
third of each dollar of new liquidity used to make repurchases, with
half of it retained as cash, once it was unlocked by the TCJA? Indeed,
conventional wisdom and existing empirical evidence suggest that un-
constrained firms would likely respond to the liquidity shock by paying
out the excess liquidity to shareholders rather than retaining it as cash
(Almeida et al., 2004, Dharmapala et al., 2011; Dharmapala, 2024).

In contrast, our results suggest that firms do not quickly adjust cash
ratios in response to positive liquidity shocks, similar to what Graham
and Leary (2018) describes as ‘‘loose’’ adherence to cash targets. In
other  words,  nominal  cash  holdings  appear  to  be  somewhat  sticky,
analogous  to  the  nominal  stickiness  of  debt  documented  by Welch
(2004). One possibility is that this loose cash targeting is the result of
fixed costs of adjustment. However, given that paying out excess cash
is unlikely to be particularly costly, an alternative explanation is that
firms may be roughly indifferent between paying out and retaining the
increased liquidity. Specifically, prior to reform, the marginal benefits
of retaining cash were high due to repatriation taxes, leading to foreign
cash accumulation. After reform, only the ‘‘traditional’’ cash holdings
trade-off remains, that is, costs of carry and agency versus precaution-
ary savings motives (e.g., Nikolov and Whited, 2014). For our sample
of firms that appear to be well-governed and financially unconstrained,
these traditional marginal costs and benefits are each likely quite small,
leading to optimal cash holdings that may be roughly indeterminate.
Simply put, firms had a strong tax incentive to accumulate cash prior
to reform, but little compelling reason to adjust cash downward once
the repatriation tax was removed by the TCJA.

It is worth noting that firms did not reduce leverage in conjunction
with their high retention of cash after the reform. Gao et al. (2021)
show that it may be optimal for firms to hold cash alongside their debt
positions because it reduces the likelihood of default and acts as a type
of collateral, thus lowering the cost of borrowing. Their model extends

the traditional precautionary savings model, and may help to explain
why firms did not pay out more of their freed foreign cash.

It  is  also  possible  that  the  optimal  level  of  internal  liquidity—
represented  by  U.S.  cash  before  the  reform  and  consolidated  cash
after—increased as a result of the TCJA. Specifically, the reduction in
the U.S. corporate tax rate, which affected all firms in our sample, likely
reduced the tax cost of holding cash inside the firm.28 Reducing the
marginal cost of holding cash would, all else equal, increase the optimal
level of cash holdings, which is consistent with our results. This implies
that the marginal benefits of cash need not be large for firms to choose
to retain freed cash.

4.  Conclusion

The  Tax  Cuts  and  Jobs  Act  unlocked  up  to  $1.7  trillion  in  cash
trapped in U.S. multinational firms’ foreign operations. We construct
a direct measure of firms’ exposure to this policy change using detailed
data from the Bureau of Economic Analysis and implement a difference-
in-differences research design to explore its impact on their real and
financial policies in the U.S. and abroad. We find no significant as-
sociation between the policy change and firms’ capital expenditures,
employment, research and development, or mergers and acquisitions
at either their domestic or foreign operations. These findings do not
vary with the degree of firms’ financial constraints. In terms of financial
outcomes, the policy shock is not associated with firms’ dividend or
leverage decisions, but has a moderate positive association with stock
repurchases. Firms with more trapped liquidity pre-TCJA also reduce
their consolidated cash holdings post-TCJA by 48 cents for each dollar
of  foreign  cash  unlocked.  Firms  thus  retain  about  half  of  the  freed
cash.  Strength  of  corporate  governance  does  not  appear  to  mediate
firms’ financial responses to the policy change. Our findings suggest
that increased access to capital likely did not have a significant effect
on  firms’  real  decisions,  and  demonstrates  only  a  loose  targeting  of
cash ratios. This is consistent with small marginal costs and benefits
of holding cash for these firms, as well as a limited incentive for firms
to pay out excess cash accumulated overseas.
