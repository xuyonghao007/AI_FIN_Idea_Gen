1.  Introduction

We study the impact of disclosure and inattention on the decision to retain fee-based financial advice using a
two-tiered natural regulatory experiment. Increased salience in fee disclosure raises the drop rate for advice,
implying improved attention — particularly for relatively sophisticated investors. However, a novel auto-drop
requirement for inattentive investors generates far more drops, implying limited attention despite salient
disclosure — particularly for the unsophisticated. Contrary to studies of commission-based advice, we find
that investors benefit from fee-based advice. Benefits are higher for less sophisticated investors, who tend to
be detrimentally auto-dropped. Drops triggered by salient disclosure tend to be beneficial.

Attention to the cost of financial services is a key consideration in
household finance, as pointed out in a commentary on their general
magnitude  (Bogle, 2008),  and  a  presidential  address  on  the  specific
case of delegated portfolio management (French, 2008). The case of
personal financial advice is similarly relevant. Advice plays a central
role in the investment decisions of tens of millions of households, at
an estimated cost of around $100 billion per year globally, or about
0.7%  of  advised  assets.1 Ceteris  paribus,  this  cost  translates  into  a
20%  erosion  in  cumulative  savings  over  a  notional  thirty-two  year
investment horizon—a figure similar to the estimated capitalized cost
of active management in French (2008).2 In short, the cost of financial
advice surely warrants investors’ attention.

Yet,  households  can  be  prone  to  inattention,  inertia,  and  poor
decisions  (Madrian  and  Shea, 2001).  These  tendencies  are  likely  to
be strong in the case of financial advice, because assessing costs and
benefits of advice is complex and opaque (perhaps intentionally so, as
in Carlin, 2009). Thus, the extent to which investors actively assess the
advice decision is an important empirical question, as is the derivative
question of whether regulators can improve deficiencies in investors’
attention. Our study provides novel insights into these matters.

Our analysis centers on a natural regulatory experiment uniquely
suited to the topic — the 2013 adoption of the Australian law known
as the Future of Financial Advice (FOFA). This law instituted three new
requirements. First, mutual funds are prohibited from paying advisors
who place investors into their funds, an arrangement referred to as con-
flicted remuneration by FOFA and revenue sharing in the U.S. Second,

We  analyze  the  portfolio  holdings  of  265,000  clients  of  a  large
Australian financial planning firm who choose between fee-based ad-
vice or self-direction. Our data spans a ten-year window around FOFA
inception, but it does not facilitate a comparative analysis of the ban
on commission-based pay since the firm’s advice fee is directly charged
to  investors.  However,  our  analysis  does  provide  novel  evidence  of
positive benefits from fee-based advice, contrary to much of the lit-
erature  on  commission-based  advice  (e.g., Bergstresser  et  al., 2008;
Christoffersen et al., 2013; Del Guercio and Reuter, 2014). Our focus
is on the two elements of FOFA that directly relate to attention — the
FDS and auto-drop. In combination, these features are particularly well
suited to analyzing whether investors periodically assess the costs and
benefits of continuing advice (i.e., ‘the advice decision’). The FDS is a
simple and direct ‘plain English’ document that states the dollars paid
for advice; specific services provided; and nothing else (see Appendix A
for an industry association template). This makes it a sharp instrument
for  assessing  salience  in  fee  disclosure.  Auto-drop  is  a  remarkably
proactive mechanism that terminates an ongoing contract unless the
investor explicitly approves the fee disclosure. These two features act
in  tandem  to  provide  a  direct  measure  of  inattention  to  salient  fee
disclosure.  To  our  knowledge,  this  two-tiered  natural  experiment  in
regulation is unique.

We begin with an analysis of the diligence with which investors
attend to the advice decision, and the impact of the FDS and opt-in
nudges on that decision (see Thaler and Sunstein, 2009, for a general
treatise on nudges). We find that investors maintain a modest degree
of  diligence  in  attending  to  the  advice  decision.  The  baseline  (pre
FDS)  advice  drop  rate  is  about  0.75%  per  month.  The  FDS  triggers
an increase in the drop rate, cumulating to a statistically significant
4%–5%  abnormal  drops  relative  to  baseline  after  six  months.  From
this, we conclude that salient fee disclosure improves attention to the
advice decision, relative to the opaque disclosure of the prior regulatory
regime.

The opt-in requirement (which we often abbreviate as opt-in) has
a much more significant 13%–16% marginal impact on the cumulative
advice drop rate (beyond baseline and FDS). This effect is discrete and
largely aligned with the 60-day deadline for opting in, consistent with
auto-drop.  Moreover,  the  impact  is  permanent:  only  one  percent  of
auto-drops subsequently reverse back into advice.

Perhaps more importantly, because the opt-in notice arrives con-
current with an FDS, the 13%–16% rate of auto-drop provides a novel
and  direct  measure  of  inattention  to  salient  disclosure.4 This  makes
our  setting  unique:  typical  studies  of  disclosure  can  only  measure
affirmative  responses.  We  conclude  that  salience  in  disclosure  helps
nudge investors to attend to their advice decision, but the effectiveness
of salience is limited.

We  also  consider  the  differential  impact  of  FOFA  nudges  based
on  investor  characteristics  relating  to  investment  sophistication,  in-
ertia,  overconfidence,  and  other  traits  thought  to  affect  investment
decisions.5 We find clear evidence that the FDS has more impact for
relatively sophisticated investors (e.g. wealthier, older, and retirement-
focused investors) who are likely predisposed to attention. Conversely,

relatively  unsophisticated  investors  (young,  less  wealthy)  are  more

An important question remains: Does the impact of FOFA nudges on
investors’ advice decision actually help investors? The notable disparity
in impact for auto-drop vs. FDS leads to the disconcerting conclusion
that  FOFA  may  push  investors  out  of  potentially  valuable  financial
advice, rather than induce voluntarily opt-out via salient fee disclosure.
Two follow-on questions can help assess whether this amounts to over-
nudging by FOFA. First, are those who value advice the ones most likely
pushed out? And second, do the benefits that auto-dropped investors
gained under advice persist after auto-drop?

If one presumes that advice is harmful, as might be inferred from
the evidence on commission-based advice, then any nudge out of advice
is  presumably  helpful.  But  there  seems  little  empirical  basis  for  a
presumption of harm in the case of fee-based advice, where conflict of
interest is less obvious. Thus, we assess the benefits of fee-based advice
both generally, and in relation to specific investor characteristics. We
then investigate whether FOFA differentially nudges out investors who
receive fewer benefits.

We find that compared to self-directed portfolios, advised portfo-
lios have higher returns, lower fees, better diversification, and better
alignment of equity exposure with age; implying investors benefit from
fee-based advice. We then relate these benefits to the same investor
characteristics used to assess differential impact of FOFA nudges on
investors’ advice decision. Even after accounting for service and ad-
visory fees, our analysis reveals that fee-based advice significantly en-
hances net-of-service-fee returns for younger investors, and those with
substantial assets, extensive investment histories, and employment.

Given these critical differences among investors, we create an in-
strument for advice value using these investor characteristics. We then
interact that instrument with each of the two FOFA nudges. We do this
to assess whether each nudge generates a higher advice drop rate for
investors who receive less benefit from advice. The FDS successfully
discriminates investors — those receiving less benefit are more likely to
drop following an FDS. This holds using both predicted benefits based
on investor characteristics (i.e., ex ante) and realized benefits (i.e., ex
post). However, auto-drop fails the discrimination test — we observe
higher auto-drop among investors with higher predicted benefit. Also,
there is no statistically reliable distinction using realized benefits.

We conclude that the FDS – which requires attention – tends to steer
relatively sophisticated investors towards the right decision on advice.
Auto-drop – which acts on inattention – tends to steer relatively unso-
phisticated investors towards what appears to be the wrong decision on
advice.

Our conclusion on auto-drop comes with two caveats. First, advice
benefits do not reliably reverse in the twelve months following auto-
drop, suggesting investors are not worse off. Second, the service fee
for advice is dollar-based in our dataset. This creates a bias against
smaller investors (Thiel, 2022), who pay a relatively high percentage
fee. Nevertheless, auto-dropped investors on average receive positive
net-of-service-fee benefits.

Our  paper  fills  several  gaps  in  the  rapidly  growing  household
finance and consumer protection literature. Previous work documents
that funds distributed via advisors underperform directly sold funds,
both  before  and  after  fees  (Bergstresser  et  al., 2008; Christoffersen
et  al., 2013; Del  Guercio  and  Reuter, 2014).  Studies  of  advised  in-
vestors’ portfolios generally find better diversification but higher fees
and lower returns. Some are based on risk-adjusted benchmarks (Foerster
et al., 2017; Linnainmaa et al., 2021); others compare self-directed and
advised investors (Kramer, 2012; Bhattacharya et al., 2012; Hackethal
et al., 2012; Gaudecker, 2015; Calcagno and Monticone, 2015; Hoechle
et al., 2017; Chalmers and Reuter, 2020). These studies do not consider
fee-based advice. Our study expands this literature to the increasingly
common regime of fee-based advice. Moreover, we provide the first
direct estimates of how advice value relates to investor characteristics,
which is critical to considering optimal choice and regulatory nudges.

Theoretical models attribute the value of advice to informational
and  psychological  sources  (Inderst  and  Ottaviani, 2012b; Gennaioli
et al., 2015). And they argue that a commission-based remuneration
structure, coupled with an inability to evaluate advice quality, can lead
to undesirable outcomes for advised investors (Inderst and Ottaviani,
2012a,c; Stoughton  et  al., 2011).  However, Chang  and  Szydlowski
(2020) propose that banning conflicted fees may not improve investor
welfare. Our evidence suggests – but does not directly establish – that
fee-based advice could benefit consumers.

Improving investor sophistication is a crucial dimension of investor
protection (Stoughton et al., 2011). Our study supports this view. We
provide large-sample evidence that echoes (Bhattacharya et al., 2012),
who conclude that investors often lack awareness of a need for advice.
Our findings illuminate the challenges faced by less sophisticated in-
vestors who benefit more from advice, but are less able to evaluate
those benefits. Nevertheless, the efficacy of financial literacy programs
is subject to debate. A major concern is their untargeted nature, and
the  long  gap  between  education  and  decision-making  (Lusardi  and
Mitchell, 2014; Fernandes et al., 2014; Kaiser et al., 2022).

By  contrast,  decision-relevant  nudges  via  disclosure  (Hirshleifer
and  Teoh, 2003; Loewenstein  et  al., 2014);  default  actions  such  as
automatic enrollment in retirement plans (Madrian and Shea, 2001);
and low-cost default investment products (Chalmers and Reuter, 2020)
offer  more  timely  and  direct  solutions.  Nudges  have  likewise  been
shown to be effective for increasing saving (Karlan et al., 2016) and
avoiding bank fees (Stango and Zinman, 2014, 2016). Yet, our evidence
highlights  how  the  optimal  default  action  can  be  unclear,  and  that
targeting investors via automatic nudges can be an errant regulatory
strategy.

describes the institutional context and the data. Section 3 presents our

2.1.  The Australian asset management market

As of December 2016 (the end of our sample), the Australian asset
management market ranked sixth in the world with USD 2.1 trillion
in assets under management, comparable to the U.S. per capita.6 The
market is supported by a mandatory national retirement saving scheme
known as superannuation, which had a compound annual growth rate
of 9% in assets under management from 2000 to 2016, outperforming
the U.S. growth rate of 5.5%. Relative to GDP, Australian pension assets
ranked seventh in the world (1.31 × GDP) compared to the U.S. fifth
rank (1.69 × GDP).7

The Superannuation Guarantee (Administration) Act of 1992 man-
dates that employers contribute 9–9.5% (revised over time) of salary
into a superannuation account for each employee during the sample
period.  The  self-employed  can  also  contribute,  and  employees  have
the  option  to  make  additional  contributions.  Retirement  savings  are

2.2.  Data source

We  use  anonymous  investor-level  data  from  a  large  Australian
financial  planning  firm  on  a  confidential  basis.  The  firm  offers  all
investors  access  to  a  mutual-fund  investment  platform  for  a  fee.  In-
vestors can choose (and switch anytime) between self-directed portfolio
construction and maintenance, or retaining a financial advisor for an
additional  monthly  fee.  We  observe  the  total  service  fees  (charged
as a dollar amount) deducted from investor accounts. This averages
0.56% of assets under advice per year for self-directed investors and
0.82% for advised investors. Effectively, the incremental fee for advice
is 0.26% per year. Advisors do not receive commissions; the advice fee
is independent of asset allocation, turnover, or fund selection.

All investors receive rudimentary financial advice at account initia-
tion8 and have access to the same investment platform, which includes
approximately 900 mutual funds from providers considered reliable by
the firm’s research team. Investors paying for advice also receive (1)
a comprehensive formal annual review of their risk profile, portfolio
performance,  and  financial  plan,  (2)  quarterly  (at  least)  telephone
contact to address these issues, and (3) ongoing availability for ad hoc
counsel and advice.

The dataset includes over 14 million monthly records of 265,593
investors’ mutual-fund holdings from 2006 (the first year of advice ser-
vice) through 2016. Investor characteristics include birth year, gender,
employment status, and account anniversary dates (the FDS date and
opt-in notice date). Survivorship bias is not a concern, as investors who
left the platform remain in the dataset. We apply bounds on specific
variables to exclude apparent errors (e.g., age must be between 18 and
115).

We match investor holdings to the Morningstar Direct database to
gather fund returns, expense ratios, asset allocations, and other fund
characteristics.  The  fund  code  identifies  retirement  (tax-advantaged
superannuation  and  pension)  and  non-retirement  (fully  taxable)  in-
vestment funds. While our primary analysis pools fund types, we also
condition on fund type in the regression analyses.

Table 1 provides a summary of the data. From Panel A, the number
of  investors  increased  from  approximately  57,000  in  2006  to  about
150,000 by the 2013 FOFA initiation date (we tabulate biennially for
brevity).  The  fraction  of  advised  investors  in  our  dataset  increased
dramatically (as might be expected from a new service), reaching 20%
of all investors by the end of the sample. The growth in superannuation
contributed to the rapid expansion of the financial planning industry in
Australia.9 The increase occurred primarily before the implementation
of  FOFA,  after  which  it  plateaued  and  experienced  a  slight  decline
(likely due to compliance and advisor-education costs, according to the
firm). Investors who held at least one retirement fund constitute 79%
of the sample.

2.3.  Investor characteristics

The literature suggests that, in part, investor characteristics deter-
mine the use of financial advisors. Lack of financial knowledge (Lusardi
and Mitchell, 2014) or financial confidence (Gennaioli et al., 2015) are
two reasons often cited. Chalmers and Reuter (2020) study a popu-
lation of university pension plan members and find that the demand
for advice from a broker decreases with investor knowledge (income,
age,  education)  and  confidence  (being  male).  Similarly, Fong  et  al.
(2020)  use  Finnish  population  share  registry  data  to  document  that
higher-income (sophisticated) investors are more likely to use discount
brokers  and  that  older  men  (particularly  those  over  45  years  old)
prefer full-service brokers. However, the evidence in broader contexts
is  mixed. Bhattacharya  et  al. (2012)  and Calcagno  and  Monticone
(2015) examine data from European banks and find that financially
illiterate  investors  either  decline  free  financial  advice  or  disregard
advice generally viewed as beneficial.

Panel B of Table 1 summarizes investor characteristics in our sam-
ple.  All  variable  definitions  are  provided  in Appendix  B.  In  subse-
quent regression analysis, we standardize all investor characteristics to
facilitate comparison of their significance.

Portfolio size is a proxy for wealth and sophistication (Calvet et al.,
2007, 2009).  The  mean  portfolio  size  for  advised  investors  is  about
$300,000, more than three times the mean for self-directed investors.10
This finding suggests that investor sophistication positively predicts the
purchase of advice. Generally, the portfolio size of investors with at
least one retirement fund is smaller than those without a retirement
fund ($77.3k vs. $125.5k).

The  average  investor  age  in  our  sample  is  55  years,  similar  to
Kramer (2012) and Calcagno and Monticone (2015), but slightly higher
than Linnainmaa et al. (2021) (51.2) and Bhattacharya et al. (2012)
(49–52.9).  The  average  age  in  studies  of  company  or  university  re-
tirement plans is much younger, around 40 (Madrian and Shea, 2001;
Chalmers and Reuter, 2020). In our sample, age is similar for investors
with or without retirement funds.

We expect investors with a longer investment history to have higher
inertia,  i.e.,  less  likely  to  change  advice  status  (Madrian  and  Shea,
2001). The average investor in our sample has 5.3 years of investment
history – 3.3 years for advised investors and 5.5 years for self-directed

Gender representation is nearly at parity, consistent with U.S. and

such as returns, asset allocation, and diversification, are considered in

3.  The advice decision and impact of FOFA nudges

We begin Section 3 by relating the decision to initiate, add, or drop
advice to investor characteristics to determine how investor sophisti-
cation, inertia, and overconfidence help shape demand for fee-based
advice.  We  then  study  the  impact  of  FOFA  nudges  on  the  advice
decision using these baseline dependencies as controls.

3.1.  Cross-sectional model for the advice decision

We  assess  the  cross-sectional  determinants  of  demand  for  advice
using a probability model estimated over each of three decisions. The
first is the initial advice decision that the literature focuses on (e.g.,
Bhattacharya et al., 2012; Chalmers and Reuter, 2020). In our case, that
means the choice to be advised or self-directed at account inception.
We  also  consider  subsequent  switches  to  or  from  advice,  which  the
investor can do at any time. We use a linear probability model in all
specifications to more easily assess the marginal effect and economic
significance.12 As  this  is  a  cross-sectional  regression,  data  is  at  the
investor  level  with  independent  variables  set  to  the  last  available
values.

4.1.  Portfolio quality metrics

We assess the value of advice using two perspectives on portfolio
quality:  returns  and  portfolio  characteristics.  In  assessing  portfolio
characteristics, such as equity allocation and diversification, we follow
the literature (e.g., Bhattacharya et al., 2012; Kramer, 2012; Foerster
et al., 2017; Li et al., 2014). However, most of our focus is on returns
as that is the first-order consideration in investing.

Financial advisors may affect fund returns through fund selectivity
and/or asset allocation. Fund selectivity can add value once a basic
allocation decision is set, and this (i.e., portfolio alpha) is the norm in
performance assessment. However, the asset allocation decision is also
potentially relevant—indeed, it may be the larger determinant of the
overall investment outcome (Brinson et al., 1986, 1991; Ibbotson and
Kaplan, 2000; Calvet et al., 2007). Hence, our analysis considers both
abnormal returns and the factor component of returns (see Appendix B
for return factors considered).

We  compute  value-weighted  average  abnormal  returns  using
monthly  fund  holdings,  both  net  and  gross  of  fund  fees.16 We  also
consider fund fees directly since they are an aspect of fund selection.
Total service fees (denoted ServFees, which includes the advice fee for
those advised) are excluded from both net and gross abnormal returns.
By assessing abnormal returns net of fund fees, we evaluate the gross
benefits of advice. Our study also examines returns after service fees to
determine if the varied benefits of advice surpass the associated costs.
Table  5 summarizes  our  measures.  Consistent  with  much  of  the
literature  on  mutual  fund  performance,  the  average  net  and  gross
monthly abnormal return is negative.17 Fund fees average 1.42% per
year.  As  a  first  indication  of  value  from  advice,  returns  are  higher
(more precisely, less negative) for advised investors. This is primarily
due to investments in funds with lower fees. Fund fees are also lower
for  investors  who  hold  a  retirement  fund;  and  post-FOFA.  In  both
cases, lower fund fees translate into better risk-adjusted returns. Total

To  the  extent  advisors  seek  to  improve  selectivity,  we  expect  a
greater focus on active management. We proxy this with the value-
weighted idiosyncratic risk of funds held, denoted Active and computed
from  36-month  rolling  regressions  on  fund  returns.  The  mean  for
advised investors is 14.85% versus 10.35% for self-directed investors.
Investors without a retirement fund hold more active funds (14.18%
vs. 9.95%).

Since mutual funds are already diversified portfolios, we use the
number  of  asset  classes  in  the  investor’s  portfolio  (using  fund-level
allocations from Morningstar) to proxy for diversification. The mean
is 6.5; higher for advised (7.13 v. 6.43) and retirement (6.62 v. 5.75)

While increasing equity exposure from zero is likely beneficial (Madrian
and Shea, 2001; Gennaioli et al., 2015), Chalmers and Reuter (2020)
emphasize that investors may start with some equity, such as through
a target date fund. Thus, further increasing equity might do more harm
than good. A more robust way to measure the benefits from advice
would be tailored equity exposure, e.g., less exposure with age. In our
sample, advised investors are less exposed to equity (43.4% vs. 48.9%),
suggesting a need for a conditional analysis.

We follow the literature in computing home bias as the portfolio
weight on domestic (Australian) bonds and equity. Australia accounts
for  less  than  4%  of  global  equity  capital,  but  our  sample’s  mean
portfolio weight on Australian assets is 45.5%. While high, this home
bias  is  less  than  the  60%  weight  reported  by Foerster  et  al. (2017)
based on Canadian data. Bhattacharya et al. (2012) also report that
German investors held 52% of their portfolio in German assets, 4% less

4.3.  Investor characteristics and portfolio quality

The  evidence  in Table  6 on  the  control  variables  discussed  in
Section 2.3 indicates  that  log  Assets,  log  Age,  and 1Retire proxy  for
investment sophistication. In particular, wealthier investors have signif-
icantly higher net abnormal returns (coefficient 0.008), including net
of total service fees (coefficient 0.011). This is due entirely to lower
fund fees (coefficient −0.010). Wealthier investors do not earn higher
gross abnormal returns, despite holding funds with more idiosyncratic
risk.  They  maintain  higher  equity  allocations  (1.742)  and  are  more
diversified across asset classes (0.236), but they have greater home bias.
These results are generally consistent with Calvet et al. (2007).

Log  Age  is  also  positively  related  to  net  abnormal  returns  (coef-
ficient  0.020),  due  to  both  lower  fund  fees  and  higher  gross  fund
returns. Age predicts less home bias and less equity exposure, consistent
with Kramer (2012) and Madrian and Shea (2001). The indicator 1Retire
identifies lower fund fees and active share, better diversification and
lower home bias, but not significantly higher abnormal returns. While
the inertia proxies log DaysInv and log DaysBank are both associated
with  lower  net  abnormal  returns,  they  yield  opposing  inferences  in
several columns of Table 6. Since they are similar in magnitude, we
focus on their sum. Consistent with Madrian and Shea (2001), we find
that inertia lessens portfolio quality (lower returns, higher fees, less
diversification, and higher home bias). Likewise, consistent with the
overconfidence literature (Barber and Odean, 2001; Barber et al., 2005,
2009), males hold funds with lower abnormal returns, greater active
share, higher fees, less diversification, and greater equity exposure and
home bias. 1Employed generally aligns with 1Male, suggesting both proxy
for overconfidence.

4.4.  Baseline inferences on value from advice

The coefficient on 1Adv in Table 6 indicates that fee-based advice
yields a statistically reliable 4.4 bps per month (53 bps annual) increase
in  net  abnormal  returns.  This  result  derives  from  both  lower  fund
fees (1.8 bps per month) and higher gross abnormal returns (2.5 bps
monthly, marginally significant). A relevant question is whether this
marginal  return  associated  with  advice  covers  its  marginal  cost.  A
novel feature of fee-based advice (and our data) is that we can directly

assess this question using the differential service fee for advice. From
Table 6, the net abnormal return of advised investors after service fees
is higher (coefficient 0.19), but the relation is not statistically reliable.
Thus, investors reliably cover costs, but a net benefit is less clear. We
get sharper inferences on advice benefits later (Sections 5.1 and 5.2),
conditioning on FOFA nudges as an investor-selection mechanism.

Our conclusion on returns aligns with Bhattacharya et al. (2012),
who observe that investor portfolio performance would have improved
had they followed the advice provided to them. However, our evidence
contrasts with studies based on stock or mutual fund trading records
under commission-based advice, which typically find that advice yields
lower returns (Hackethal et al., 2012; Hoechle et al., 2017; Chalmers
and Reuter, 2020). Further support for an informational role of fee-
based advice is seen with the positive relation (0.468) to the active
share  (idiosyncratic  risk)  in  funds  held. Kramer (2012)  finds  lower
idiosyncratic risk in advised portfolios, while Bhattacharya et al. (2012)
find no significant difference.

Fee-based advice also improves portfolio quality on other dimen-
sions. There is a 76 bps higher allocation to equity with advice. As with
active share, the literature reports mixed results here. Kramer (2012)
finds lower equity allocations with advice, whereas Bhattacharya et al.
(2012), Foerster et al. (2017), and Chalmers and Reuter (2020) report
higher equity allocations.19 Diversification across asset classes improves
with advice (0.098), consistent with the literature. However, home bias
also increases, contrary to Kramer (2012) and Hoechle et al. (2017).
Interestingly, Bhattacharya et al. (2012) find that even when advice
would reduce home bias, investors often fail to follow that advice.

the dot product 𝐴 ⋅ 𝐵 where 𝐴 is the vector of estimated coefficients on

5.1.1.  Do FDS nudges discriminate on advice value?

From Panel A of Table 8, the coefficient on gross AdviceValue is
negative for baseline drops (−0.002, reliable at 1%), thus investors with
less AdviceValue more likely drop advice independent of FOFA nudges.
From  Panel  B,  this  relation  is  even  stronger  with  net  AdviceValue.
We  conclude  that  a  subset  of  investors  are  naturally  attentive  to  a
cost/benefit  assessment  of  advice,  and  that  attention  improves  their
advice decisions.

The FDS nudge nearly triples the effectiveness of investors’ baseline
cost-benefit assessment of advice. From Panel A of Table 8, the inter-
active coefficient on gross AdviceValue is −0.009, as is the coefficient
on net AdviceValue in Panel B. Thus, investors who benefit more from
advice are significantly less likely to drop following an FDS, both gross
or net of advice fees. This incremental effect of the FDS is about twice
baseline  values  (−0.002  for  gross  AdviceValue  and −0.005  for  net
AdviceValue).

5.1.2.  Does the opt-in nudge properly discriminate advice value?

The  evidence  in Table  8 on  auto-drop  is  not  favorable.  In  Panel
A,  the  coefficient  on  gross  AdviceValue,  when  interacted  with  the
indicator  for  an  opt-in  event,  is  a  statistically  reliable  0.090.  This
implies more drops in the 12 months following an opt-in notice for
investors  who  receive  greater  benefits  from  advice.  This  outcome  is
contrary to the presumed regulatory objective of selectively terminating
advice for investors receiving less benefit. In Panel B, the coefficient
on net AdviceValue is much smaller, but still significantly positive, at
0.007. The difference arises because investors dropping after the opt-
in notice (mostly auto-drops) tend to pay high percentage advice fees,
averaging about 92% of gross AdviceValue.

An important observation here is the apparent role of fixed costs
in providing advice. Assuming the service provider sets (dollar) advice
fees to (partially) recover fixed costs, the percentage advice fee can be
quite high for less wealthy investors (who tend to be auto-dropped).
Thus,  even  though  less  wealthy  investors  benefit  more  from  advice
gross of fees (see Section 4.5), advice is less cost–effective for them
with dollar-based pricing.

6.  Conclusion

Australia’s natural experiment in the regulation of financial advice –
FOFA – yields novel and important insights on the role of salience in fee

disclosure and on the impact of an unusually proactive auto-drop for
regulating the continuation of advice services. We find strong evidence
that salience is an important feature of disclosure. However, we find
even stronger evidence of substantial residual inattention, irrespective
of salient disclosure. In particular, auto-drop – the regulatory response
to a failure to opt-in to advice continuance — occurs at seven times
the normal drop rate for advice. Auto-drop provides a direct measure
of inattention to concurrent disclosure.

We analyze the value of fee-based advice and find that it benefits
investors  on  several  dimensions.  Benefits  are  stronger  for  relatively
unsophisticated investors and those susceptible to inertia and overcon-
fidence. Investors’ decisions regarding advice are somewhat success-
ful  in  discriminating  advice  benefits,  but  FOFA’s  disclosure  require-
ment  strongly  improves  discrimination  of  advice  benefits.  However,
auto-drop does not successfully discriminate investors, as auto-dropped
investors were receiving above-average value from advice.

This  leads  to  our  central  conclusion.  To  be  effective,  regulation
of  costly  financial  services  must  engage  the  investor.  Brute  force
(e.g.,  auto-drop)  has  high  impact  but  –  at  least  in  this  case  –  it  is
ineffective. Furthermore, we find that even highly salient fee disclosure
fails to engage many investors. This underscores the primary challenge
for regulators: finding ways to foster the investor engagement needed
to enhance decision-making in financial advice.

Appendix B.  Variable definitions

• 1ADV – Indicator for a client-month where the client is advised.
• 1Employed – Indicator for employment status at beginning of month.
• 1Male - Indicator for male.
• 1Retire - Indicator for an account that hold retirement-class mutual funds.
• AbnRet – (monthly %) The investor’s portfolio weighted average abnormal return net of fund fees (excluding advice fee). Computed as the
sum of the intercept from a time-series regression of fund returns (all months) on the U.S. market, size, value, momentum, and bond factors
(data from Ken French website) plus a comparable set of five Australian factors (data from Adrian Lee of Fong et al., 2014), plus the residual
return for corresponding months. In the case of ‘Past AbnRet’ residuals are trailing twelve months. In the case of ‘AbnRet’, residuals are
twelve months forward looking. AbnRetGross is AbnRet plus fund fees added back (AbnRet is net of fund fees).

• AbnRet-ServFees – (monthly %) AbnRet minus ServFees, ServFees is the total service fees that the investors pay to the advisory firm for
maintaining the investment portfolios and receiving the advice. This includes the adviser service fee and the platform service fee. ServFees
is calculated as the dollar fee divided by the assets. ServFees is also winsorized at 1% level.

• ActiveShr – (monthly in units of percent squared; i.e., 1 = 0.0001) For each fund held by the investor, square the fund’s monthly AbnRet

and average. Then compute the investor’s portfolio-weighted average.

• Assets – (AUD in 1000s) Beginning of month investor funds under administration, not including bank deposits, aggregated across account

types, e.g., investment and super-annuation (pension).

• Diversification – (abbreviated Diverse) Measures the number of unique asset classes in an investor’s portfolio, using MorningStar’s partition

of each fund held.

• DaysInv – Observation date less date of appearance on the investment platform.
• DaysBank – Observation date less date of appearance at bank (e.g., checking account).
• Equity – (monthly in percent) Portfolio-weighted average equity percent for each fund held, from MorningStar.
• Factor return – (monthly observations at the investor-portfolio level in units of percent). [See also abnormal return.] Subtract the abnormal
return for a given fund-month from the fund return to get the factor return for that fund-month. This is the 10-factor coefficient vector times
the 10-factor return realization vector. Average across the funds held by the investor using portfolio weights. In the case of ‘FactorRet’ the
value is twelve months forward looking. In the case of ‘Past FactorRet’ the value is trailing twelve months.

• Fee disclosure statement (FDS) – After July 1, 2013, investors paying for advice receive an annual Fee Disclosure Statement (FDS) on their
service anniversary date. The FDS reports dollar amount of advice service fee paid and the services have been provided (e.g. annual portfolio
review; quarterly phone meetings; etc.) during the last 12 months. See Appendix A.

• Fund fees – (monthly in percent) Portfolio weighted average fund fee taken from Morningstar. (Fees are annual in the summary Table 5.)
• Home bias – (monthly in percent) Sum the domestic (Australian) equity percent and the domestic fixed-income percent for each fund held

(from MorningStar). Compute portfolio-weighted average.

• Investor, Advised – Refers to a client of the bank’s investment service who pays a monthly dollar-based advice fee and is assigned a financial
advisor who is available for ad hoc query; who confers with the investor at least quarterly; and who annually performs a comprehensive
portfolio review.

• Investor, Self-directed Refers to a client of the bank’s investment service who does not pay an additional advice fee (or receive ongoing

advice).

• Opt-in notice – After July 1, 2013, investors paying for advice receive a biennial Opt-in notice on their service anniversary date. This
requests that the customer notify the bank of an intent to continue the advice relationship. If after sixty days the investor has not responded
affirmatively, the ongoing advisory relationship (and the associated fee) terminates at the end of month.
