We study how information disclosure concerns shape the choice of limited partners (LPs) by venture capitalists 
(VCs). Late-2002 court rulings prevented public LPs from providing confidentiality to investment managers. 
The best-performing VCs, but not other managers, responded by excluding public LPs from their new funds. 
Lost  access  reduced  public  LP  returns  by  $1.6  billion  relative  to  $14  billion  of  their  VC  commitments. 
Legislation reducing disclosure, contracts limiting information access, and added fund-of-funds intermediaries 
helped restore access. These changes focused on protecting portfolio company information, highlighting the 
importance of proprietary information for VC investing and the potential costs of transparency.

1. Introduction

Companies accessing public markets trade off the benefits of raising 
capital with the costs of making the information publicly available 
(Bhattacharya and Ritter, 1983; Doidge et al., 2017). Venture capi-
tal (VC) funds face a similar trade-off when accepting capital from 
state pension plans and public university endowments. These public 
investors constitute one of the largest pools of institutional money, but 
that capital comes with a disclosure cost, as these investors are subject 

to disclosure requirements under the state-level Freedom of Informa-
tion Acts (FOIAs). While the allocation of public investors to VC has 
dramatically increased over the last two decades, little is known about 
the impact of disclosure requirements on this asset class. Understanding 
the consequences of these laws both informs the ongoing policy debate 
about disclosure regulation and sheds light on the unique features of 
the VC industry.2

Although  VC  funds  are  opaque  to  outsiders,  there  is  extensive 
information sharing between VCs and the limited partners (LPs) that 
invest into VC funds. A major driver of this is LPs’ need to evaluate 
VC skill. More so than most asset managers, like, for example, those 
managing mutual funds (Fama and French, 2010) and buyout (BO) 
funds (Kaplan and Schoar, 2005), some VC fund managers appear to be

that,  information  helps  VCs  build  trust  and  allows  LPs  to  manage 
the illiquidity and uncertainty inherent in private market investments 
(Nadauld et al., 2019).

This  information  sharing  might  start  with  a  pitch  outlining  the 
VC  fund’s  team,  the  fund’s  investment  thesis,  the  fund’s  structure, 
the performance of the firm’s past funds, and the status of any prior 
investments. An LP’s subsequent due diligence and investment decision 
would generate further information flow, including a limited partner-
ship agreement (LPA) detailing the fund terms. Such terms include the 
fund’s fees, the fund’s governance structure, and the LP’s information 
rights, with major LPs being able to negotiate these terms either on 
behalf of all LPs or for only themselves through a so-called side let-
ter. After initial capital calls, the fund would provide its LPs, on an 
ongoing basis, formal financial documents that outline the fund’s asset 
values, cash flows, and performance metrics. In addition to fund-level 
data, these reports specify the performance of companies in the fund’s 
portfolio. VCs consider all this information confidential.

In this paper, we study how disclosure concerns impact the match-
ing  between  VCs  and  their  LPs.  Specifically,  we  exploit  exogenous 
variation in LPs’ ability to provide confidentiality. This variation is a 
result of court rulings in late 2002, which built on state-level FOIAs 
to force public LPs to disclose the performance of their private equity 
(PE) investments (including VC funds) to the public. Originally enacted 
over the course of the 20th century, state-level FOIAs, also known 
as sunshine laws, force government bodies to disclose some types of 
information in response to requests from third parties. These laws aim 
to balance the costs and benefits of transparency by exempting trade 
secrets and other sensitive information from FOIA requests. However, 
the scope of these exemptions is often unclear and is determined in part 
by court cases. Following the dot-com crash and the Enron scandal, 
journalists and public employees used these acts to initiate lawsuits 
seeking access to the information that fund managers provide to their 
public investors. High-profile initial court rulings in late 2002 forced 
the  disclosure  of  the  fund-level  return  data  and  left  the  secrecy  of 
portfolio company information and other sensitive data as an open 
question. These rulings meant VC funds with public LPs could be forced 
to make more information public.

The fact that VCs voluntarily disclosed essentially none of these 
data  to  the  public  suggests  there  are  costs  to  disclosure.  However, 
it is ex-ante unclear how agents (VCs) would react to increased in-
formation disclosure requirements imposed on their principals (public 
LPs).  One  hypothesis  is  that  VCs  with  certain  focus  areas,  such  as 
those targeting certain industries or early-stage startups, would value 
confidentiality  more  and  react  more  strongly.  Alternatively,  poorly 
performing VCs might show the largest reaction, as they could lose 
the most from publicizing their (subpar) returns. Along those lines, 
consistent  with  the  evidence  on  misallocation  from  Hochberg  and 
Rauh (2012), transparency might discipline public LPs out of poorly 
performing investments. A third scenario is that the VC-LP matches are 
sticky and would not change after the FOIA shock, due to, for example, 
an informational hold-up preventing VCs from easily replacing their 
existing LP base (Hochberg et al., 2014).

To test these hypotheses, we use Preqin data on LP commitments 
to  VC  funds.  We  split  U.S.-based  LPs  into  public  and  private  LPs, 
with public LPs being subject to FOIAs and private LPs serving as a 
natural control group. Our public LPs are mostly state pension plans 
and public university endowments, while private LPs include private 
pension plans, foundations, and private endowments. We consider a va-
riety of VC characteristics, including fund size; the fund’s target stage, 
geography, industry; and VC firm quality. We measure VC firm quality 
by ranking VC firms based on the number of IPOs VCs had in their 
portfolios (with board seats) between 1995 and 2001 in VentureSource 
data (similar to Nahata (2008)). We classify the top 20 firms as ‘‘top 
VCs’’, which collectively accounted for 9% of funds and 22% of raised 
capital over that period.

We first focus on how the fraction of commitments by public LPs 
changed  after  the  court  decisions,  which  we  refer  to  as  the  FOIA 
shock. Following the FOIA shock, the best-performing VCs dramatically 
reduced their commitments from public LPs relative to a control group 
of  nontop  VCs:  the  fraction  of  public  investments  dropped  by  15 
percentage points, about one-half of the pre-shock values. Other VC 
characteristics, such as stage, industry focus, and target geography, 
are not associated with a differential change in public LP share. The 
dynamics show pre-shock parallel trends and an immediate, differen-
tial  response,  supporting  our  causal  interpretation.  While  anecdotal 
accounts on the response of VC firms make it clear that many VCs 
disliked the FOIA rulings, we show that, on average, only top VCs 
reacted by excluding public LPs from their funds.

Next, we turn to the LP-level analysis to study changes in the ag-
gregate portfolio composition of investors’ private market allocations. 
We confirm that following the FOIA shock public LPs reduced their 
allocation to top VC funds and reallocated to funds of funds (FoFs) 
with established top VC relationships: intermediaries that might allow 
them to regain exposure to the best-performing VCs. This reallocation is 
consistent with the idea that, by creating an additional layer of report-
ing where the private intermediary does not pass along the portfolio 
company information received from its GPs to its LPs, FoFs help public 
investors maintain their exposure to best-performing VCs. This broad 
pattern is consistent with case studies and anecdotal evidence from 
public endowments.

Using  data  from  MSCI-Burgiss,  we  quantify  the  impact  of  these 
reallocations on public LPs’ returns in the 2003–2006 period — im-
mediately  following  the  FOIA  shock.  We  estimate  the  effect  of  the 
FOIA shock by comparing public LPs’ actual returns with the returns 
they would have received by following a simple reinvestment strategy. 
In  light  of  the  FoF  reallocations  discussed  above,  we  consider  the 
returns to public LPs’ combined commitments in VC and FoF, which 
accounted for $14 billion and $12 billion, respectively. Compared to 
the naive reinvestment counterfactual, public LPs’ exposure to the best-
performing VC firms was halved, reducing their returns by $4.3 billion. 
The reallocations within nontop VCs and usage of FoFs to regain access 
to top VCs helped public LPs offset a large part of this performance gap, 
bringing the forgone returns to $1.6 billion overall (6% on $26 billion 
of VC and FoF investments combined).

Our results show that only the best-performing VCs reduced their 
commitments from public LPs. Given that forced disclosure could ad-
versely  affect  all  VCs  and  the  companies  they  back,  it  is  perhaps 
puzzling  why  only  top  VCs  reduced  their  public  commitments.  We 
attempt to differentiate between two potential drivers of this response. 
First, top VCs could have a greater ability to replace LPs by virtue 
of having excess LP demand (Sensoy et al., 2014) – we call this a 
differential supply of capital channel. Second, top VCs could care more 
about privacy than other VCs – for example, top VCs could have better-
quality proprietary information on portfolio companies or a stronger 
desire to conceal fee discounts given to some investors – we label this 
a differential demand for privacy channel. Importantly, VCs having an 
underlying demand for privacy drives the effect in both cases; what 
distinguishes these two channels is whether the difference between top 
VCs and nontop VCs is driven by top VCs having a stronger demand for 
privacy than nontop VCs or by top VCs having a greater ability to act 
on that desire than nontop VCs.

We find stronger evidence supporting the supply of capital channel. 
First, the results of our tests of VC characteristics speak against the 
demand-side  arguments:  while  both  early-stage  funds  and  funds  in 
particular industries might have higher demands for privacy, neither 
stage nor industry is associated with VCs’ response to the disclosure 
shock. Second, a demand for privacy driven by the desire to conceal 
the fee-based discrimination among LPs is unlikely. On the contrary, 
as Begenau and Siriwardane (2024) document, funds in high demand 
are less likely to use a tiered fee structure, suggesting that top VCs 
tend not to segregate their LPs using different fee schedules. Third,

we reestimate our main results based on a sample of BO firms, which 
are,  in  general,  more  scalable  than  VC  firms  (Metrick  and  Yasuda, 
2010). The ability to expand fund sizes means that BO firms do not 
face the LP rationing dynamic that characterizes top VCs. Similar to 
top VC funds, the best-performing BO funds could also arguably possess 
more valuable proprietary information than their nontop counterparts. 
However, top BO funds show little to no reaction to the disclosure 
laws, casting further doubt on the demand-side argument. Finally, we 
see a similar reduction in public commitments when we look at other 
proxies of VC firms that had scaled closer to their optimal capacity, such 
as above-standard high carried interest, presence of auxiliary funds, 
and fund sequence number. Although we cannot rule out a differential 
demand for privacy, the presented evidence is more consistent with the 
supply of capital being the reason why only top VCs appear to actively 
respond to the FOIA shock.

Our causal mechanism is supported by our tests on top VC alloca-
tions, direct quotes from VCs and LPs, and the apparent reallocation 
of public LPs into FoFs with top VC exposure. However, there may 
be a lingering concern that the remnants of the dot-com bubble burst 
contaminate our results. We address these concerns in two ways. First, 
we show that there was no decrease in public LPs’ allocation to top VCs 
in the period after the dot-com burst but before the FOIA shock. Second, 
our results persist at the fund level, commitment level, and LP level, in 
addition to passing a number of other robustness checks. Varying the 
level of analysis allows us to control for a host of parameters, including 
LP identity, VC firm identity, and time-variant LP state characteristics.
Given that VCs appear extremely sensitive to disclosure, it is natural 
to ask exactly what type of information they seek to protect. We shed 
light on this question by analyzing LPAs of funds raised by the same 
VC firms immediately before and after the FOIA shock . We document 
the emergence of several strategies GPs use to protect information from 
FOIA disclosure, including limiting the information released to public 
LPs and providing information to them only through temporary online 
portals.  VCs  overwhelmingly  added  strong  protections  for  portfolio 
company information. While return information was protected by some 
VCs, other VCs explicitly permitted disclosure if required by law. The 
contrast between these two types of sensitive information suggests that 
both VCs and LPs agreed that portfolio company information should 
remain private.

Both publicly and privately sourced datasets in venture capital echo 
this wedge between portfolio company and fund financial information: 
while the data on portfolio companies are generally incomplete, we 
know substantially more about fund-level performance (Kaplan and 
Lerner, 2016). In addition, the datasets relying on public information 
may miss the return information of leading VC firms if top VCs are 
more likely to protect their return information. As the small size of 
our sample of LPAs limits our ability to study this question directly, 
we turn to Preqin data to test the extent to which VCs limited the 
disclosure of this information to their LPs. We test for limited disclosure 
by analyzing missing return information in Preqin, leveraging the fact 
that Preqin generally collects data via FOIA requests sent to public LPs. 
We find that after the lawsuits, the performance information of top 
VC firms was more likely to be missing in Preqin. This reporting gap 
also holds for top VCs who took money from public LPs, suggesting 
that some public LPs agreed to the contractual innovation and limited 
the scope of the information they receive from VCs. An analysis of top 
VC performance in MSCI-Burgiss and Preqin confirms that the latter 
significantly understates top VC returns due to missing data.

Our final set of analyses looks at post-FOIA-shock amendments to 
state FOIAs that reduced public LPs’ disclosure requirements. These 
amendments exempted certain LPs and certain types of information 
from FOIA requests, which we use to explore the importance of differ-
ent kinds of information. First, we document that most of the amend-
ments focused on exempting portfolio company information, suggesting 
that portfolio company information was a priority for industry partic-
ipants, to the extent that they shaped this regulation. A number of

amendments provided protection of fund return information in addi-
tion to exempting the portfolio company data. Second, we observe a 
rebound in public LPs’ top VC investments following legislative protec-
tion of portfolio company information, while amendments protecting 
fund  return  information  are  associated  with  a  significantly  weaker 
restoration of prior relationships with top VC firms. Though the FOIA 
amendments are endogenous in their nature, these tests provide sugges-
tive evidence that exemption of portfolio company information played 
an important role in the return of public LPs to top VC firms.

Our paper adds to a growing literature studying institutional in-
vestors and their relationships with VC firms. First and foremost, we 
add  to  the  relatively  small  literature  that  focuses  on  how  general 
partners (GPs) in the PE industry assess their relationships with LPs. 
These papers have principally focused on LPs’ exposure to liquidity 
shocks.  Lerner  and  Schoar  (2004)  find  that  GPs  screen  for  ‘‘deep-
pocket’’  investors  by  imposing  restrictions  on  LP  stake  sales  in  in-
vestment  contracts.  Maurin  et  al.  (2023)  model  GP  preferences  for 
illiquidity-tolerant LPs and show how they affect GPs’ behaviors. We 
contribute to this strand of research by studying the important but 
unexplored aspect of LPs’ ability to provide confidentiality. Our results 
demonstrate that the regulatory environment surrounding institutional 
investors is a relevant dimension influencing the choice of LPs by VC 
partnerships, going beyond the notion of access to top funds due to 
earlier relationships.

Second, we add to the more extensive literature on LP decision-
making  and  performance  in  the  context  of  the  PE  industry.  Lerner 
et al. (2007) provide the first large-scale evidence on the investment 
choices made by LPs; Sensoy et al. (2014) and Cavagnaro et al. (2019) 
explore the performance of LPs further; and Brown et al. (2021) study 
whether LPs can time their exposure to PE. Giommetti and Sørensen 
(2021), Gredil et al. (2021), Korteweg et al. (2022), and Gourier et al. 
(2024) study how LPs approach allocation in PE. Another strand of 
the literature focuses on the particular aspects of investments in PE 
made by public pensions, with recent contributions from Hochberg and 
Rauh (2012), Andonov et al. (2018), Mittal (2022), and Begenau and 
Siriwardane (2024). We complement this literature by demonstrating 
the relevance of the regulatory framework surrounding LPs, with FOIAs 
being  an  important  dimension  of  segmentation  among  institutional 
investors. This segmentation might have implications for understanding 
the performance of public LPs, as they potentially face a reduced set of 
investment opportunities in VC relative to their private counterparts 
and receive less data from GPs to inform their investment decisions.

Finally, our paper contributes to the literature on the costs and 
benefits of disclosure requirements in PE. Brown et al. (2008) propose a 
framework for the cost–benefit analysis of regulatory disclosure based 
on the introduction and quick repeal of the SEC-mandated disclosure 
for hedge funds. Hermalin and Weisbach (2012) argue that there could 
be a point beyond which additional disclosure can exacerbate agency 
problems and decrease firm value. By studying disclosure requirements 
imposed on principals (the LPs), we show that while the requirements 
improved  information  disclosure  by  LPs  to  the  public,  information 
production by agents (the GPs) for LPs has dropped.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, 
we discuss the institutional background and the data. In Section 3, we 
present tests on how VCs’ sources of capital changed after the FOIA 
shock. In Section 4, we study implications for public LPs’ portfolio 
allocations and returns. In Section 5, we analyze the effects of the 
FOIA shock on contracts and laws. In Section 6, we conduct additional 
analysis and robustness tests, which further support our key findings. 
In Section 7, we conclude.

2.1. Institutional background

The Enron and WorldCom scandals and the bursting of the dot-com 
bubble created a call for increased transparency in the early 2000s. This 
led not just to the Sarbanes-Oxley Act but also to generally greater 
scrutiny of financial information and investments. Public institutions 
were a natural target for disclosure advocates due to state-level open 
records  laws,  known  as  FOIAs,  which  had  been  enacted  over  the 
course of the 20th century. These laws set high standards of disclosure 
for  public  institutions  by  allowing  anyone  to  request  disclosure  of 
previously  unreleased  information.  FOIAs  were  used  by  journalists, 
watchdogs,  and  researchers,  but  also  by  companies  and  specialized 
business  intelligence  firms  such  as  the  FOIA  Group.  As  these  laws 
were broad, they were necessarily vague and subject to questions of 
interpretation. Although FOIAs offered exemptions for trade secrets, it 
was unclear what that meant in the context of VC.

In late 2002, the potential for these laws to be used to force the 
disclosure of information on public LPs’ PE investments crystallized. On 
September 18, 2002, the University of Texas Investment Management 
Company (UTIMCO) responded to the choice between disclosure and 
litigation by making the fund-level return information available to the 
public.3 This unexpected change to the university’s disclosure policy 
triggered  a  chain  of  landmark  lawsuits  in  late  2002,  most  notably 
against the California State Teachers’ Retirement System (CalSTRS), 
the California Public Employees’ Retirement System (CalPERS), and the 
University of California.

These lawsuits were typically brought by journalists under FOIA 
and sought the disclosure of not just fund-level returns but also fund 
terms and even portfolio company information. The above-mentioned 
public pensions and public endowments ended up disclosing fund-level 
return data in late 2002 through early 2003 (Hurdle, 2005). However, 
uncertainty remained as to whether the ultimate scope of disclosure 
would expand to other data sent by VCs to their LPs such as portfolio 
company information.

For example, a lawsuit against the nation’s largest public pension 
plan, CalPERS, was initiated by the San Jose Mercury News on Oc-
tober 25, 2002, after the pension plan refused to disclose fund-level 
performance data and descriptions of the portfolio companies.4 In a 
preliminary ruling in November 2002, the court determined that in-
dividual fund performance does not represent trade secrets, and hence 
is subject to disclosure under California’s FOIA. At the same time, the 
court left open the question of the secrecy of portfolio company data. 
In December 2002, CalPERS settled the case with the petitioner by 
agreeing to release the fund-level performance data. As part of the 
settlement, the portfolio company data remained private.5

VC firms responded to this surprise increase in disclosure risk in 
different  ways.  Some  strongly  and  publicly  opposed  disclosure.  For 
example, Sequoia Capital not only spoke out publicly, it also expelled 
the University of Michigan and the University of California from its 
future funds and asked them to divest from its existing funds. Other 
VC firms took advantage of the situation. For example, the relatively 
young firm MPM Capital LP did not exclude CalPERS from its funds, 
instead saying that the firm was not concerned about return data being 
released and would keep a close eye on the development of the situation 
regarding potential portfolio company disclosures. Dennis Henner, a 
partner at the firm, explained,

An important feature of these lawsuits was uncertainty regarding 
the scope of the required information disclosure. The plaintiffs sought 
comprehensive  disclosure  covering  not  just  historical  fund  returns, 
but also fund terms, identities of other investors in the funds, and 
portfolio company information including corporate strategies, equity 
stakes, valuations, and financial statements. The prevailing view at the 
time was that disclosure of these data could lead to significant harm to 
startups, and by extension, their VCs.

The mass release of sensitive portfolio company data ultimately 
never  took  place,  but  the  potential  for  it  was  a  recurring  threat. 
For example, in 2004 the Texas Attorney General ruled that individ-
ual portfolio company data should be made public, before reversing 
that decision.8 In 2005, the Ohio Bureau of Workers’ Compensation 
similarly announced that it would release portfolio company perfor-
mance data, before reversing course under pressure from GPs.9 Perhaps 
the  closest  case  occurred  in  2006,  when  the  Ohio  Department  of 
Development disclosed information about the portfolio companies of 
JumpStart, an impact-focused venture firm it had contributed funds to. 
This disclosure led to damaging reporting on portfolio companies; for 
example, a local newspaper quoted JumpStart as saying ‘‘Neither the 
idea nor the market was strong enough to warrant further investment’’ 
in its internal reports on portfolio company CoupAd.10 Although it is 
challenging to assert causality, VentureSource data show patterns con-
sistent with JumpStart suffering a reputational hit: while the firm had 
board seats in 42% of its portfolio companies and the average number 
of round participants in its deals was 2.1 before the disclosure, these 
numbers dropped to 3% and 1.4, respectively, after the information was 
made public.

Several factors caused the threat of additional disclosure to lessen 
over time. First, the disquiet of the excluded public LP managers led 
state legislators to adjust their FOIAs to exclude certain information 
regarding PE investments of public entities, in particular the data on 
the underlying portfolio companies. For example, the state of Michigan 
amended its FOIA in 2004 to allow the University of Michigan to keep 
its records private. We explore these amendments in Section 5.3. Sec-
ond, an absence of court- or legislation-mandated disclosure of portfolio 
company information gradually reduced the perceived risk of portfolio 
company information being subject to FOIA requests. Third, VCs and 
public LPs coordinated to create a limited reporting approach, in which 
LPs agreed to receive only selective information from VC firms, as we 
discuss in Section 5.1. This approach often entails carving out a class 
of LPs with limited information rights (named ‘‘FOIA Partner’’) within 
the LPAs. While not every LP has accepted this approach, it has allowed 
some to maintain their access to top VCs. According to Buyouts Insider 
(2018), such restrictive clauses are now prevalent and present in every 
second LPA of VC funds.

2.2. Data

Commitments data: Preqin data (accessed via WRDS) form the 
basis of our primary analysis. We construct a sample of LP–GP re-
lationships  based  on  dated  capital  commitments.  Our  data  include 
both the commitments themselves and a variety of LP, GP, and fund 
characteristics. Preqin has been extensively used by both academics (for 
example, Lerner et al. (2007) and Cavagnaro et al. (2019)) and practi-
tioners focusing on the capital allocation decisions and performance of 
investors in PE.

The  FOIA  shock  applied  only  to  U.S.  public  investors,  and  we 
limit our main sample to U.S.-based LPs to avoid potential geographic 
confounds. Foreign LPs account for 16% of total commitments; they 
are scattered across countries with different macroeconomic conditions; 
and their commitments might depend on LP-specific factors like cur-
rency exchange rates, prices for export commodities like oil or metals, 
and government policies (Bernstein et al., 2013).

The distinction between U.S.-based private and public LPs is cen-
tral to our analysis. State pensions constitute the majority of public 
LPs.  Since  Preqin  does  not  distinguish  between  private  and  public 
endowments, we manually classify endowments as public or private, 
and then add the former category to the group of public investors. 
We further group certain state-operated sovereign wealth funds (for 
example, the Alaska Permanent Fund) and insurance companies (for 
example, the Ohio Bureau of Workers’ Compensation) as a separate 
category of other public LPs. We define private LPs as any non-public 
LPs, including, for example, private pension plans, foundations, FoF, 
and  private  endowments.  This  group  of  investors  is  not  subject  to 
FOIAs.

We  focus  on  U.S.-based  VC  funds,  dropping  non-U.S.  funds  and 
secondary, BO, co-investment, sidecar, and turnaround funds. We con-
sider funds raised between 1999 and 2006 in our baseline regressions, 
covering about four years before and after the initial court rulings. In 
the analysis of potential spillover effects and capital reallocations, we 
extend our sample to include BO funds and FoFs. Our final sample 
underlying the main analysis consists of 927 LPs, which, altogether, 
made 6710 capital commitments to 845 VC funds managed by 511 VC 
firms.

Performance data: We rank VC firms using VentureSource, which 
provides comprehensive coverage of VC portfolio companies, exits, and 
board memberships over that period. Following Nahata (2008), we 
use portfolio companies’ IPOs to identify top VCs. We rank VC firms 
based on VentureSource by counting the number of IPOs between 1995 
and 2001 in which the VC firm participated as lead investor (with 
board seats). We label the top 20 VC firms in that ranking (listed in 
Appendix  A) as top VCs.11 The list naturally includes many of the most 
prominent VC firms at the time. We refer to the funds raised by top VCs 
as top VC funds, which is an ex-ante classification based on VC firm 
characteristics rather than an ex-post sorting based on realized fund 
performance. In Section 5, we confirm the relevance of this ranking by 
showing that top VCs have historically delivered higher returns.

In addition to capital commitments, Preqin reports the performance 
of VC funds. The primary source of these performance data is FOIA 
requests, which are directly related to the events studied in this pa-
per (Preqin, 2023). We exploit this particular feature of this data source 
by exploring the absence of return information for VC funds in Preqin. 
Such an analysis offers important insights regarding the reaction of 
agents (VC firms) to the new disclosure requirements imposed on prin-
cipals (public investors). As we show, the missing nature of return data 
on top VCs in Preqin limits our ability to reliably estimate the economic 
costs incurred by public LPs. Because of that, we use MSCI-Burgiss data

had higher sequence numbers (for example, Sequoia XI), and they were 
more likely to focus on early-stage investments and investments in the

a lower percentage of public LPs in top VCs firms reporting the data.

3.2. Dynamics of the effect across investor types

Fig.  1 presents the dynamics of the effect shown in Table  2 and 
supports the idea that the FOIA shock caused a decrease in public LPs’ 
access to top VCs. The figure presents the breakdown by public LPs, 
FoF LPs, and non-FoF private LPs for both top (Panel A) and nontop 
(Panel B) VC funds. Motivated by the prior literature, we consider FoFs 
separately from other private LPs (Harris et al., 2018; Nadauld et al., 
2019). The fractions of commitments sum up to 100% for each type of 
VC firm (horizontally, across the plots) in each year.

Panel A indicates that public LP commitments to top VCs are stable 
before the FOIA shock but show an immediate and significant drop 
after the shock. Non-FoF private LP commitments remain stable around 
the event, while FoFs experience an increase in commitments to top 
VCs. These figures support a causal interpretation — commitments from 
public LPs and both classes of private LPs to top VCs appear to have 
been on a similar trend before the FOIA shock, but following the event, 
top VCs decrease their reliance on public LPs.

In Panel B, we show that nontop VCs exhibit little reaction to the 
FOIA shock, with no major reallocation away from public LPs. This 

suggests that the effects in Panel A were specific to top VC funds, 
again supporting our causal assertions. The observation that top VCs 
increased their reliance on FoF LPs (with no offsetting reallocation 
of FoFs from nontop VCs) raises the intriguing question of where the 
public LP commitments went and where the FoFs’ capital came from. 
In Section 4.1, we provide evidence for the possibility that the public 
LPs reallocated to FoFs to regain access to top VCs. This evidence is 
supported by the anecdotal accounts of industry participants and case 
studies from public university endowments.

3.3. Supply of capital versus demand for privacy

There are several reasons why VCs might be especially privacy-
conscious.  First,  secrecy  might  be  a  competitive  advantage  for  the 
startups that the VCs themselves invest in. Past research on firm disclo-
sure (Botosan and Stanford, 2005; Berger and Hann, 2007; Dedman and 
Lennox, 2009; Bernard, 2016) highlights the costs of providing exces-
sive information to incumbent competitors, customers, and suppliers. 
The high uncertainty environments in which startups operate might 
make such disclosures especially costly for them. Second, the VC funds 
might face costs of disclosure, beyond damage to the startups they back. 
VCs’ positions could illustrate their proprietary views on markets and 
segments, as shown by Agarwal et al. (2013) for hedge fund managers’ 
confidential holdings. The staged financing model used in VC makes 
these concerns even more acute, as disclosing the pricing of a round 
or an exit could reduce a VC firm’s bargaining power in subsequent 
fundraising or liquidity events. Third, disclosure might impact a VC’s 
relationships with other LPs. The VC industry has both unusually high 
fees (Litvak, 2009; Metrick and Yasuda, 2011) and unusually volatile 
returns, a potential point of friction with union members or student 
stakeholders of pension funds and endowments.

In  Section  5  we  attempt  to  tease  out  the  information  VCs  are 
protecting,  but  for  now  we  will  focus  on  our  finding  that  there  is 
not only a general demand of VCs for privacy, but also an apparent 
differential response from top VCs. There are multiple possible reasons 
why top VCs could be more sensitive to the disclosure requirements. 
We group these mechanisms into two broad categories: supply-side 
mechanisms based on top VCs facing lower costs of turning away LPs, 
and demand-side mechanisms based on top VCs having an even higher 
demand for privacy than other funds. While an underlying demand for 
privacy drives the effect in both cases, the two channels are distinct 
based on whether the difference between top VCs and other VCs is 
driven by different levels of demand for privacy or different abilities 
to act on those desires.

A supply of capital channel relies on top VC funds having excess 
demand from investors. The idea that top VCs are scaled lower than the 
willingness of investors to commit their capital is in line with Metrick 
and Yasuda (2010), who show that VC funds are much less scalable 
than BO funds. In the case of the best-performing VC funds, some but 
not all of this value is extracted via higher fees (Hochberg et al., 2014). 
By virtue of having excess demand from LPs, top VCs have a greater 
ability to modify their LP base in response to disclosure concerns or 
other selection criteria. A top VC can drop public LPs and replace them 
with more or larger commitments from private LPs that were eager 
to invest but rationed out of the funds. In contrast, lower-ranked VC 
firms are more reliant on their existing investor base and would have 
to reduce their fund size if they excluded their many public LPs from 
the pool of their investors. This channel makes prominent VC firms 
less dependent on any particular LPs and consequently more likely to 
forgo commitments from public LPs during the period of uncertainty 
following the FOIA shock.

A demand for privacy channel involves top VCs having a differentially 
higher demand for privacy. A natural story is that high-performing 
VC funds may add more value and have more valuable proprietary 
information — if top VCs are better, their trade secrets will naturally 
be more valuable. Although it is challenging to rule out this story com-
pletely, the earlier results comparing VC funds’ industry specialization 
and their stage focus partially speak against it. Despite presumably 
having different needs for confidentiality, neither early-stage VCs nor 
those focused on certain industries show larger reactions to disclosure. 
As an additional test of these two channels, we look at the reactions 
of BO firms, which were impacted by the same disclosure shock. Top 
BO firms outperformed their nontop counterparts in the same manner 
as top VCs and so, similar to top VCs, arguably possess more valuable 
proprietary data than less successful BO firms. Given these similarities, 
top BO firms might exhibit increased demand for privacy relative to 
nontop BO firms. Yet, BO firms are more scalable and grow faster than 
VC firms (Metrick and Yasuda, 2010), which reduces the importance 
of the supply-side channels for this industry in general. Therefore, the 
analysis of top versus nontop BO firms in Table IA.3 offers a test of 
demand channels without substantial interference from the supply-side 
alternatives. Our results indicate that top BO firms show no particular 
reaction to these disclosure laws. The overall evidence is more consis-
tent with a role of supply of capital channel rather than with top firms 
simply putting a higher value on confidentiality.

Beyond potentially adding more value, top VCs differ from other 
VCs in terms of fees and returns. It is possible that these differences 
in  fund  terms  and  performance  might  create  a  higher  demand  for 
privacy that is not linked to the nature of their portfolio companies. 
Top VCs notably charge higher fees (Robinson and Sensoy, 2013), and 
the negative publicity around fee data and discrimination among LPs 
could harm their reputation and fundraising prospects. However, this 
seems unlikely for two reasons. First, Begenau and Siriwardane (2024) 
find that fee discounts are less common among funds in high demand, 
suggesting that top VCs are less likely to discriminate based on price. 
Second, our analysis in Section 5 suggests that VCs viewed portfolio 
company information as being more important. Third, it is unclear how 

9 

R. Abuzov et al.

costs of disclosing returns could be high enough to justify the dramatic 
shift of the LP base away from public investors. If anything, one would 
expect the opposite result, as top VCs persistently deliver higher returns 
(Harris et al., 2023), and it is unclear why disclosing good returns 
would be worse than disclosing poor returns.

Ideally, we would also test the scaling hypothesis directly by looking 
at factors associated with the ability to scale. This is challenging for 
several reasons. First, where a fund sits relative to its optimal size is 
endogenous to LP commitments, and that optimal size is itself endoge-
nous to hiring choices. Second, data on intuitive proxies of scaling (fees, 
subscription ratio) are usually missing for top funds. Third, our top VC 
measure is naturally correlated with several natural measures of ability 
to scale, consistent with the intuition that outperforming funds have 
excess demand. In line with the last point, we provide suggestive tests 
in Table IA.4.

In Panel A, we look at several proxies for an inability to scale. 
First, we look at VC firms that charge carried interest above the 20% 
standard.14 These firms are presumably struggling to scale, as they have 
decided to charge higher performance fees rather than increase fund 
size. Second, we look at older VC firms, which are presumably more 
likely to have been able to scale to their preferred size. Third, we look at 
firms with non-core funds. Funds that have a later-stage (for example, 
Accel Growth) or foreign fund (for example, Accel Israel) in addition to 
their core fund sequence are similarly presumably struggling to scale. 
All of these groups see larger reductions in public LP relationships, 
consistent with a scalability channel. These results are in line with the 
findings of Robinson and Sensoy (2013), who document that carried 
interest is positively related to fund size and fund sequence number 
for VC firms. In Panel B, we look specifically at the non-core funds 
themselves. Unfortunately, the small number of non-core funds during 
this era means we lack power to find economically meaningful effects.
Overall, while we cannot rule out that top VCs exhibit an increased 
demand for privacy relative to other VC firms, our results are more 
consistent with a supply of capital story, where top VCs are the only 
funds  that  have  excess  investor  demand  and  so  only  top  VCs  can 
reallocate away from public LPs at a relatively low cost.

4. Changes in public LPs’ allocations and performance

We now turn our analysis from the VC fund-level to the LP-level, 
measuring how public LPs’ propensity to invest in top VCs changed and 
relating that back to the investment returns of public LPs.

4.1. Changes in allocations

We study how public LPs responded to being excluded from top VCs 
by looking at their commitments to private market funds before and 
after the FOIA shock. As before, we use private LPs as a control group 
because they were not subject to FOIA requirements. In Table  4, we 
consider the change in the portion of LP-year-level commitments going 
to different classes of managers. We consider the broad categories of 
VC, BO, and FoF, which we split further based on their characteristics. 
We split VC and BO funds into top and nontop managers, following 
the previously discussed classification. Given the intriguing increase in 
FoF allocations to top VCs shown in Fig.  1, we split FoFs by focusing 
on their preexisting relationships with top VCs: we label top 20 fund

4.2. Counterfactual allocations

We measure the direct financial effect of the FOIA shock on public 
LPs by comparing their actual investments to a counterfactual portfolio. 
Our baseline counterfactual assumes that, in the absence of the FOIA 
shock, public LPs would commit the same amount to all the funds raised 
by managers with whom they had invested previously. This reinvest-
ment counterfactual is based on the high incidence of reinvestments 
within established GP–LP relationships, as documented by Lerner et al. 
(2007).

In this analysis, we focus on the funds closed in the period from 
2003 to 2006, the years immediately following the shock. We weight 
the actual portfolio based on LPs’ dollar commitments and the counter-
factual portfolio based on the dollar value of the past commitments. We 
also construct count-weighted portfolios based on the number of actual 
or hypothetical commitments from public LPs. We consider only FoF 
and VC commitments, as they appear to be the margins of adjustment, 
and including the large number of BO commitments would add noise 
to the estimations. After dropping $58 billion (975) BO commitments, 
we start our analysis with the aggregate portfolio of $14 billion (691) 
VC and $12 billion (236) FoF reported commitments.

Table  5 uses return data from MSCI-Burgiss to compare the per-
formance  of  the  reinvestment  counterfactual  to  the  performance  of 
the public LPs’ actual commitments, with Table IA.5 providing addi-
tional details on portfolio construction based on a specific example. 
We measure performance based on TVPI, the most common VC per-
formance  measure,  which  we  calculate  using  the  latest  cash  flows 
and NAV information. The commitment-size weighted (commitment-
count weighted) reinvestment counterfactual delivers a 1.70× (1.90×) 

TVPI, about 0.06× (0.14×) higher than the 1.64× (1.76×) TVPI from 
public LPs’ actual commitments. Across the $14 billion reported VC 
commitments and $12 billion FoF commitments by public LPs over the 
2003–2006 period, these TVPI differences mean that public LPs’ actual 
investments performed $1.6 billion ($3.5 billion) worse than if they 
have naively reallocated to the managers they had previously invested 
in.

We decompose this return difference by measuring the performance 
impact of switching classes of GPs from the reinvestment assumption 
to  the  actual  commitments.  First,  we  compare  the  performance  of 
the reinvestment counterfactual to the performance of a portfolio that 
models top VC commitments based on public LPs’ actual commitments 
and other commitments based on reinvestment. Replacing the reinvest-
ment counterfactual VC commitments with the actual public LP top VC 
commitments (and leaving other commitments the same) reduces the 
portion of top VC commitments by more than half and cuts the portfolio 
TVPI by 0.16× (0.31×). This dramatic drop is driven by the especially 
strong performance of top VCs during this period: across all of their 
2003–2006 funds, top VCs delivered a TVPI of 3.4× versus 1.5× for 
other VCs, broadly consistent with Harris et al. (2023). The widening 
performance gap between top and nontop VCs in the high-uncertainty 
period following the FOIA shock is consistent with nontop VC firms 
incurring meaningful costs from the FOIA shock.

Kaplan and Schoar (2005) point out, this persistence itself is puzzling, 
as  in  most  financial  markets  GPs  would  raise  fees  (e.g.,  Berk  and 
Green (2004)) rather than give their LPs rents. One explanation in 
the literature is the model proposed by Hochberg et al. (2014), where 
existing LPs (but not new potential LPs) know the skill of their VCs and 
that allows them hold up fundraising and extract information rents. In 
that model, the key friction is that incumbent LPs leaving a fund convey 
negative information. Another model is Marquez et al. (2015), where 
GPs choose to leave money on the table for investors to counterbalance 
a risk of not raising a fund or raising a small fund, both of which would 
be more costly for VCs with higher skill. In either model, it is not clear 
that ejecting public LPs would have a high cost for fund managers in 
conditions of credible signaling. In Marquez et al. (2015), investors are 
indistinguishable and competitive. In Hochberg et al. (2014), publicly 
ejecting LPs for a credible reason would presumably not send that 
manner of negative signal. In fact, Sequoia’s public scolding of the 
University of California would be entirely consistent with eliminating 
doubt  as  to  whether  its  departure  was  based  on  an  assessment  of 
Sequioa’s quality or an attempt to gain bargaining power. Relatedly, 
we do not observe a significant drop in the fund sizes of top VC firms 
relative to their nontop counterparts following the event. In practice, 
we know that relationships in this market are sticky, and there was 
likely  a  significant  cost  to  ejecting  certain  LPs  from  the  fund.  The 
willingness of top VCs to reshuffle their investors confirms that VCs 
do indeed put high value on privacy.

5. Contractual and regulatory innovation

The  disclosure  shock  led  to  compensating  changes  in  both  the 
contracts between LPs and VCs and the FOIA laws themselves. We 
first  document  changes  in  LPAs  around  the  FOIA  shock,  highlight-
ing  what  information  that  VCs  and  LPs  agreed  to  protect  and  the 
consequences  of  that  information  protection  for  public  information 
availability. We then examine the FOIA amendments passed in several 
states, documenting the information they targeted and the effects of 
these confidentiality-enhancing legal interventions.

5.1. Evolution of contracts between VCs and LPs

The rights and responsibilities of VCs and their LPs are spelled out 
in lengthy LPAs. Consistent with the general secrecy of the industry, 
these documents themselves are confidential and not generally avail-
able. However, we collect a sample of these investment contracts from 
anonymous data providers. We consider two samples of LPAs: a broad 
sample of 39 LPAs from 1999–2006 and a matched sample of 14 LPAs 
created by taking the latest pre-FOIA LPA and the earliest post-FOIA 
LPA for the seven VC firms.

Fig.  3 summarizes the changes in these contracts around the FOIA 
shock, with points farther to the right denoting higher fraction of LPAs 
offering  a  particular  protection.  We  see  a  significant  strengthening 
of confidentiality requirements in both samples. Most pre-FOIA shock 
LPAs did not have a confidentiality clause at all, while every firm in 
both samples had such a clause following the FOIA shock. These clauses 
gave VCs broad control over information disclosure, often allowing VCs 
to anonymize and redact data or provide information access only tem-
porarily via a password protected online portal. None of the pre-FOIA

While  portfolio  company  information  was  uniformly  protected, 
most VCs explicitly allowed fund-level return information disclosure. 
Some  VCs  did  prohibit  any  return  information  disclosure,  but  the 
general pattern is much weaker than that seen with the protection 
of  portfolio  company  information.  The  investment  contracts  are,  of 
course, the result of negotiations between VCs and their LPs and a by-
product of the needs and bargaining power of both sides. However, the 
fact that portfolio company data was more protected than fund-level 
performance information suggests that the VCs saw the confidentiality 
of portfolio company information as of the highest importance.

The limitations to the data transmission affected every second in-
vestment contract in different forms (Buyouts Insider, 2018) and left 
many  public  LPs  with  less  information.  On  the  upside,  these  new 
clauses could have allowed public LPs to preserve or regain relation-
ships  with  VC  firms.  On  the  downside,  however,  terms  restricting 
information  access  by  public  LPs  make  both  VC  and  aggregate  al-
location decisions more challenging and raise legal and compliance 
concerns. Higher information asymmetry faced by public LPs might also 
undermine their performance in the secondary market for LP stakes 
(Nadauld et al., 2019) as well as co-investments with fund managers 
(Fang et al., 2015; Braun et al., 2020).

Missing  returns  datapoints  in  fund-level  performance  reports  of 
public LPs are a testament to added restrictions on information sharing 
and disclosure. For example, the 2017 Report on Alternative Investment 
Returns by the Regents of the University of California reports that they 
were ‘‘unable to provide fund level information [for Kleiner Perkins and 
Sequoia Capital] because The Regents of the University of California 
does not receive this information from the General Partner’’ (see Section 
IA.4 of Online Appendix). Though the top VCs in our sample were 
more likely to add terms protecting against disclosure, unfortunately, 
our  small  sample  of  investment  contracts  prevents  us  from  making 
generalizations.  To  show  broader  evidence  on  the  impact  of  these 
contractual terms and test whether there was a differential impact on 
top VCs, we turn to missing return information in Preqin.

5.2. Evidence from missing returns

We shed light on the prevalence of data-protecting LPAs by studying 
an outcome of these restrictions: missing return data in Preqin. While 
parts  of  the  data  in  Preqin  are  collected  via  voluntary  disclosures 
by  GPs  and  private  LPs,  routine  FOIA  requests  sent  to  public  LPs 
are the dominant source of return data in the database. Because of 
this, Preqin systematically collects return data using FOIA requests, 
and, under current FOIA laws, public investors must generally report 
performance information in response to these requests. This means that 
the returns of a fund with public commitments can be missing from 
Preqin when the public LP is not able to send data to Preqin as the 
LP does not possess the requested data due to limited reporting. At the 
same time, the return data of funds without public LPs can be present 
due to sporadic reporting by private investors or direct (and potentially 
selective) reporting by funds themselves.

As our first step, we analyze the absence of return information in 
Preqin to ascertain whether the lawsuits led to significant changes in in-
formation transmission between public LPs and VC funds. Specifically, 
we define return information as missing if a particular VC fund does 
not have a single return data point in Preqin. As the presence of return 
information is a characteristic of a fund, we perform this analysis at 
the VC fund-level. We extend our sample period and consider ten years 
before and after the lawsuits to address the slow-moving and long-term 
nature of the evolution of investment contracts in response to the FOIA 
shock. As we are most interested in capturing cases when GPs did not 
send information to their public LPs, we additionally interact our key 
variable of interest with the presence of at least one public LP in the 
fund.

only one particular – and arguably one of the most stringent – restric-

analysis of LPAs, it is likely that more VC firms, both top and nontop, 
adopted limitations on the reporting of portfolio company data to their 
public LPs.

We have documented significant financial and information costs to 
public LPs as a result of their stronger reporting regime. These costs 
were driven by the issues disclosure creates for VC funds and, we argue, 
for the portfolio companies in which those VCs invest. This type of 
disclosure costs complement findings on mutual funds (Agarwal et al., 
2015), hedge funds (Shi, 2017), and innovative public firms (Breuer 
et al., 2022). To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to report 
the deleterious impact of a VC firm’s investors disclosing information.
Although public LPs, their VCs, and those VCs’ portfolio companies 
face  direct  costs  of  disclosure,  the  general  equilibrium  effects  are 
unclear. While some VCs excluded public LPs and amended contracts 
to reduce information sharing and data disclosure, there is now more 
publicly available information on both VC firms and LP investments. 
As  documented  for  public  company  (Badertscher  et  al.,  2013)  and 
fund manager (Agarwal et al., 2015) disclosure, VC disclosure could 
have spillovers for industry players in different directions. VC firms

could learn the performance of their competitors and form preferences 
regarding the choice of syndication partners. Startups could target spe-
cific VCs based on their prior performance, fund sizes, or LP base. LPs 
might be better able to benchmark performance across funds and ben-
efit from more liquid secondary markets (as we discuss in Section 6.1). 
Although these general equilibrium effects are compelling to explore, 
they  are  often  challenging  to  test  within  our  empirical  framework. 
For example, while the case of Jumpstart (discussed in Section 2.1) is 
consistent with VC firms carefully choosing their investment partners, 
testing the broader effects of disclosure risks on syndication patterns 
requires establishing the links between startups and VC funds (rather 
than VC firms). In Table IA.6, we conduct tests on the propensity of 
top VCs syndicating with nontop VCs at the VC firm level and do not 
detect meaningful changes. The lack of evidence might be due to the 
measurement error and the associated attenuation bias, as investment 
rounds we observe in the post-treatment period are often done by the 
funds raised prior to the FOIA shock. In addition, the spread of FOIA 
amendments and changes in LPAs may have weakened the effect, even 
though their passage and effectiveness were unclear ex-ante at the time 
of the initial court rulings.

5.3. Regulatory innovation

Several states amended their FOIAs following the FOIA shock. These 
amendments  clarified  and  restricted  the  scope  of  public  investors’ 
required disclosure. Unlike the initial lawsuits, the amendments did 
not come as a shock to the VC industry participants. These law changes 
were passed with the explicit goal of helping public LPs regain access to 
VC funds, presumably based on lobbying and consultation from VC and 
instate public LPs. For example, the California senate bill exempting VC 
returns from FOIA requests reads ‘‘Following recent litigation seeking 
to  require  public  pension  funds  and  retirement  systems  and  public 
endowments or foundations to disclose certain information about al-
ternative investments, the funds risk being excluded from participation 
in certain alternative investments’’. Although this endogeneity makes 
causal analysis challenging, it opens another avenue of exploration: 
to the extent VCs and LPs drove these amendments, the nature of the 
amendments reveals what the VC industry saw as important.

We exploit the differences in information protected by these amend-
ments to shed light on the causes of disclosure avoidance. Fig.  4 shows 
the 14 states that passed FOIA amendments between 2002 and 2012 
and summarizes the nature of those amendments. Section IA.3 of Online 
Appendix  reproduces  their  text.  Consistent  with  portfolio  company 
information being the key point of contention, all the amendments pro-
tect portfolio company information. About a third of states additionally 
protected fund-level return information and stated that LPs need only 
disclose asset-class level returns. These protections sometimes applied 
to all public LPs in a state, and sometimes exempted only certain LPs 
(e.g., the University of Washington’s endowment). We test the overall

6. Additional analysis and robustness tests

This  section  contains  three  sets  of  additional  analysis.  We  first 
look at the evolution of the secondary market for LP stakes. Next, we 
document changes in fund composition and size and relate those to 
VC quality. Finally, we show that our results are robust to considering 
reinvestment decisions, adding foreign LPs, different definitions of top 
VC,  and  considering  only  VCs  with  pre-existing  relationships  with 
public investors.

6.1. The secondary market for LP stakes

The FOIA shock could impact the secondary market for LP stakes 
in several ways. The increased public disclosure of fund information 
could increase liquidity and transfers by improving matching and fund 
evaluation (e.g., Bhide (1993)), but the reduction in VCs’ information 
sharing  with  public  LPs  could  increase  information  asymmetry  and 
harm liquidity. VCs that prevented public LPs from buying fund stakes 
would reduce the number of potential buyers and reduce liquidity, but 
if VCs were able to force public LPs to sell existing fund stakes, we 
might see more sales after the initial court rulings. All of these channels 
would be mediated by VCs’ bargaining power.

transactions in LP stakes, we use Pitchbook data which contains some 
limited information on secondary commitments made by LPs (we ob-
serve buyers only). Figure IA.2 plots the frequency of secondary trans-

6.2. Fund size and investor demand

Our main results indicate that top VC firms reduced the fraction 
of  commitments  from  public  LPs  following  the  lawsuits.  As  public 
LPs accounted for 24% of total commitments in an average top VC 
fund prior to the shock (Panel B of Table  1), this reallocation might 
interfere with VCs’ fundraising process and ultimately result in smaller 
fund sizes. We test this possibility using fund-level tests. While the 
estimations in Table IA.7 point to a decrease in the size of funds raised 
by top VCs relative to a control group of lower-ranked VCs after the 
disclosure, this change is not statistically significant. This observation 
is consistent with the view that top VCs can more easily substitute 
their public LP base with more or larger commitments from private LPs 
and therefore mitigate the adverse impact of reallocations. Smaller and 
less successful VC firms might experience great difficulties in replacing 
their LPs, and they are in general more dependent on their existing LP 
base. Relatedly, Chen and Ewens (2021) document that the regulatory 
limitations on banks’ ability to commit capital to VC funds led to a 
decline in fund sizes of exposed local VC firms and hurt their future 
fundraising activity.

Since  the  drop  in  the  fraction  of  commitments  from  public  LPs 
might be driven by an increase in the number of investments from 
private LPs, we additionally estimate regressions for the number of 
LPs. The estimates in columns 2 to 4 of Table IA.7 suggest that the 
reallocations are mostly driven by the drop in the number of public LPs. 
The change in the number of private LPs is positive but not particularly 
pronounced. While the data limitations do not allow us to explore the 
changes in commitment sizes directly, the absence of changes in fund 
sizes of top VC firms could potentially stem from private LPs making 
larger commitments to this category of VCs.

Table IA.8 additionally examines fund size and investor demand for 
funds raised by top VC and BO firms. In Panel A, we show that top 
VCs raise larger funds and have higher subscription ratios, consistent 
with these firms being higher quality and capacity constrained. Panel 
B indicates that funds raised by top BO firms are also larger, but they 
are not more likely to be oversubscribed — consistent with only top 
VCs having excess demand. These tests are based on an extended time 
period as we lack the data on subscription ratios for the majority of 
funds.

6.3. Robustness checks

Foreign LPs as an additional control group. As Table IA.9 shows, our 
results are mostly unchanged if we add foreign LPs to our control group. 
Our main coefficient of interest on the fund-level fraction of public 
commitments moves only slightly — from 0.147 to 0.142. Columns 3 
to 6 also show that foreign LPs do not explain the absence of significant 
change in fund sizes of top VC firms: following the FOIA shock, top VCs 
increased their reliance on U.S.-based private LPs rather than foreign 
LPs. Table IA.10 reveals similar patterns in terms of summary statistics. 
We exclude foreign LPs from our main analysis because of the worries 
about potential LP geography confounds, which we can address within 
the sample of U.S.-based LPs by inclusion of granular LP state-by-year 
fixed effects.

Reinvestments. In this test, we model reinvestments by considering 
both the new commitments and the terminations of connections, de-
fined as the absence of a commitment from a previously investing LP 
in a given VC firm’s newly raised fund. We follow the prior literature on 
the skill of LPs by focusing on their reinvestment decisions in PE (Lerner 
et al., 2007; Sensoy et al., 2014; Cavagnaro et al., 2019). We perform 
this analysis separately for public and private LPs due to the concern 
that the disaggregated commitment-level tests could be especially sen-
sitive to the sporadic nature of known investment links for private LPs. 
Table IA.11 confirms that following the lawsuits, top VCs are less likely 
to accept reinvestments from public LPs. Reinvestments by private LPs 

Journal of Financial Economics 169 (2025) 104063 

show a smaller overall adjustment, though the propensity to reinvest 
grows during the first year following the disclosure shock.

Varying definitions of top VC. In Table IA.12, we separately consider 
the top 10, 20, 30, and 40 VC firms. The baseline results stay strong as 
we limit our sample of affected firms to the top 10, with the coefficients 
shrinking as we use broader definitions of top VCs. This is consistent 
with lower ranked VCs being less able to react to the LP disclosure 
requirements.

Restricting sample to VCs with prior public LP relationships. Table IA.13 
shows that our key results persist after eliminating VCs with no prior 
links to public LPs, as top VCs shift from their existing connections with 
FOIA-exposed plans to private LPs. This observation not only mitigates 
the concern about inherently different types of LPs, but also reinforces 
the main argument that performance disclosure by public LPs is the key 
driver of the changes.

7. Conclusion

We study the impact of disclosure on capital allocation decisions in 
the context of the VC industry. We show that a strengthening of FOIA-
based disclosure requirements for public investors led many top VCs 
to exclude these investors from their funds. At the same time, top VCs 
increase the fraction of commitments from their existing private LPs 
and also FoFs.

The reallocations significantly impaired the performance of public 
LPs’ VC investments, highlighting the value of confidentiality in VC 
investing  and  the  trade-off  between  performance  and  transparency. 
Specifically,  lost  access  to  top  VCs  reduced  public  investor  returns 
by $4.3 billion compared to the naive reinvestment counterfactual. 
Reallocations among nontop VCs and using FoFs to regain access to 
top VCs helped public LPs offset most of the performance loss, reducing 
losses to $1.6 billion.

It is natural to ask what type of information top VCs sought to 
protect by excluding public plans from their investor base. To inform 
our  analysis,  we  explore  the  evolution  of  investment  contracts  and 
changes in FOIA amendments following the FOIA shock. Overall, we 
see a strong and consistent pattern: the language of LPAs was over-
whelmingly changed to protect portfolio company information rather 
than to protect fund return information. Similarly, state amendments 
focused on exempting portfolio company data from the scope of FOIAs, 
with some states also protecting fund-level information. Using the state-
level heterogeneity in law content, we provide suggestive evidence that 
exempting portfolio company information from disclosure was more 
effective in restoring relationships between top VCs and public plans.
A long-term and pervasive consequence of these disclosure require-
ments is a reduction in the amount of information VCs share with their 
public LPs. We demonstrate this channel by looking at the completeness 
of return information in Preqin. Top VC funds, including funds with 
public LPs among their investors, are less likely to have their return 
data  available  after  the  regulation.  While  the  direct  impact  of  the 
lawsuits is limited to a fraction of each LP’s portfolio invested in top 
VCs, the systematic lack of information on fund performance and other 
information on fund operations represents an additional information 
cost, as public LPs have less information to use to compare perfor-
mance across funds when choosing new fund managers and making 
reinvestment decisions. As the industry participants themselves point 
out, FOIAs present a barrier to giving investors the information they 
need (Pennsylvania’s Public Pension Management and Asset Investment 
Review Commission, 2018).

Beyond  the  specific  case  of  public  LPs’  disclosure  policies,  our 
results underscore the cost of legal uncertainty. Further, they show how 
endogenous changes in information can counteract policy in a multi-
layer principal–agent framework. Forcing principals (plan managers) to 
disclose information created limited transparency as it led their agents 
(VC funds) to share less information with them.
