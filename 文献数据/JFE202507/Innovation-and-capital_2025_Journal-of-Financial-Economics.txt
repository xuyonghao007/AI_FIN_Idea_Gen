1. Introduction

Using a regime change in the commercialization of university innovation in 1980 that strongly increased uni-
versity  incentives  to  patent  and  license  discoveries,  we  document  that  an  increase  in  the  supply  of  commer-
cializable innovation attracts venture capital investment to the region. The Bayh-Dole Act shifted ownership of 
intellectual  property  stemming  from  federally-funded  research  from  the  federal  government  to  universities, 
spurring technology transfer into the local area. Because universities have different technological strengths, each 
local area surrounding a university experienced an increase after 1980 in commercializable innovation relevant 
to particular sets of industries which differed widely across university counties. Comparing industries within a 
county that were more versus less related to the local university’s innovative strengths, we show that venture 
capital dollars after 1980 flowed systematically towards geographic areas and industries affected most by the 
sudden influx of commercializable innovation from universities. These results persist even when controlling for 
ex ante geographic and industry distributions of corporate patenting and prior venture financing. The findings 
support the notion that increased supply of commercializable innovation serves to draw private capital invest-
ment to a region.

Venture capital is a key source of financing for the commercialization 
of new innovation. The geographic distribution of venture capital and 
other  forms  of  investment  for  innovation-driven  entrepreneurship, 
however, is highly concentrated. In 2019, Silicon Valley firms received 
39  %  of  all  U.S.  venture  capital  (VC)  allocations;  the  top  three  cities 
received 60 % of all VC funding deployed; and the top five received 69 % 
(PwC  MoneyTree,  2019).  Lack  of  access  to  capital  to  commercialize 
innovation  is  a  frequently  cited  reason  for  some  regions’  less 
well-functioning innovation ecosystems, and it has been one of the most 
frequently  targeted  areas  for  policy  intervention  (Lerner,  2009).  To 
encourage  ecosystem  formation,  policy  makers  have  often  focused—-
with mixed success (Lerner, 2009)—on interventions designed to pro-
vide  seed  capital  in  a  region  or  to  attract  venture  capital  funds  to  a 

region, often in the form of tax breaks for early-stage investment (Denes 
et al., 2023) or the formation of local government-backed funds. In this 
paper,  we  explore  an  alternative  possible  focus  for  policy  initiatives, 
namely,  interventions  designed  to  increase  the  supply  of  commercia-
lizable innovation—which provide the investment opportunities that a 
capital intervention on its own does not supply and which in turn may 
draw private investment to the region. We show that VC is drawn to and 
deployed in response to positive shocks to commercializable innovative 
activity in a region: increases in the supply of commercializable inno-
vation draw capital that serves as a critical component in the creation of 
successful entrepreneurial clusters.

innovation and private capital investment, however, is challenging. To 
shed light on how supply of commercializable innovation may attract 
investment capital, we take advantage of a shock to the production and

universities:  the  Bayh-Dole  Act  of  1980.  U.S.  research  universities 
depend heavily on federal funding for their research (Babina et al., 2023; 
Jacob and Lefgren, 2011). Inventions and associated intellectual prop-
erty resulting from such research were historically assigned to the fed-
eral  government,  and  most  federally-funded  research  was  never 
patented or commercialized. The Bayh-Dole Act allowed universities to 
hold property rights to innovations developed at their institutions using 
federal research funding and provided strong incentives for universities 
to engage in patenting and licensing activity. As a result, it led to the 
development of significant infrastructure for technology transfer, which 
made  university  innovation  become  significantly  more  accessible  to 
industry  (Henderson  et  al.,  1998;  Sampat  et  al.,  2003).  Importantly, 
because  universities  differ  in  research  strengths,  the  Bayh-Dole  Act 
provides  a  shock  to  the  spread  of  commercializable  innovation  that 
varies  not  only  across  geographic  areas,  but  also—depending  on  the 
pre-Act research strengths of the nearby universities—across technolo-
gies  within  a  geographic  area.  Our  empirical  approach  uses  this 
within-area  variation  in  the  industries  related  to  university  research 
strengths to measure the causal impact of the Bayh-Dole Act’s shock to 
the accessibility of commercializable innovation on venture capital in-
vestment, holding other geographic factors constant.

To build intuition for the strategy, consider the different pre-1980 
innovative strengths of two strong research universities: the University 
of Texas at Austin (UT) and Johns Hopkins University (JHU). When the 
Bayh-Dole Act was passed, UT had a top electrical and computer engi-
neering department, but was less strong in biosciences. In contrast, JHU 
specialized in biosciences research but not in computer engineering. We 
can thus identify the specific industries in each geographic area that are 
most likely to benefit from the Bayh-Dole Act’s shock to the accessibility 
of commercializable university innovation. In the Austin, TX area, we 
would  expect  the  effects  of  the  Bayh-Dole  Act  to  be  larger  in  local 
electronic  and  computer-related  industries  than  in  pharmaceutical  or 
bioscience-related  industries,  while  in  Baltimore,  MD,  the  area  sur-
rounding JHU, we would expect the reverse. The nature of this shock 
allows  us  to  identify  the  effects  of  commercializable  innovation  on 
capital  flows to regions, as we can hold a geographic area fixed, and 
identify  effects  off  of  cross-industry  differences  in  investment  capital 
flows, based on the intensity of field-specific patenting from the nearby 
university.  Our  analysis  further  tightens  identification  by  saturating 
regressions with fixed effects, including controls for nationwide changes 
in the flow of investment dollars to particular industries, such that our 
estimates cannot simply reflect sectoral trends.

We focus on VC investment in the region, as it is a primary source of 
private capital investment in innovation-driven ventures and represents 
one of the key elements of the financing ecosystem for such ventures. 
Importantly, the VC activity we measure is venture investment in the 
local  ecosystem  generally,  not  merely  in  university  innovation.  The 
Bayh-Dole Act unleashed a wave of commercializable university inno-
vation  into  the  local  economy,  which  stimulated  increased  corporate 
innovation in related industries, and drew VC investment in university 
counties into new ventures, both directly and indirectly connected to the 
local university.

Our empirical approach further takes advantage of a concurrent in-
crease  in  fund  flows  to  venture  capital  firms  brought  about  by  three 
policy  changes.  The  first  was  the  1979  clarification  to  the  Employee 
Retirement  Income  Security  Act  (ERISA)  Prudent  Man  Rule,  which 
allowed pension funds to invest in higher-risk assets. Quickly following 
it were the 1980 Small Business Act, which redefined VC fund managers 
as business development companies, and the 1980 ERISA Safe Harbor 
regulation,  which  sanctioned  limited  partnerships—the  dominant 
organizational  form  in  the  VC  industry.  These  regulatory  changes 
resulted in large increases in committed capital to VC firms in the early 
1980s—capital  which  then  needed  to  be  deployed  across  investment 
opportunities  in  the  U.S.  Our  findings  indicate  that  these  funds  were 
disproportionately  deployed  in  university  areas  and  in  the  industries 

most related to their innovative output, even when controlling for the 
pre-existing distributions of VC investment and corporate patenting. In 
fact,  the  locations  with  the  greatest  VC  growth  from  1980  to  1990, 
shown in Fig. 1 (Panel (d)), so closely resemble the locations of pre-1980 
university patenting (Panel (b)) that the attraction of ex-post VC to ex- 
ante university patenting is apparent, visually, even before examining 
the full cross-industry variation in each location.

We show empirically that the Bayh-Dole Act-induced shock to the 
supply  of commercializable innovation in the  vicinity  of top research 
universities leads to the increased flow of VC funds to university regions 
over non-university regions, and, specifically, to the industries in each 
university region that are most closely related to the local universities’ 
ex-ante technological strengths. We find an increase of $109,000 in VC 
funds after the Bayh-Dole Act per county-industry, per standard devia-
tion increase in our university “innovation index” measure, or $45,000 
per  county-industry  per  citation  to  a  university  patent.  This  effect 
amounts  to  approximately  $3.9  million  additional  VC  investment  per 
county after the Bayh-Dole Act, while the average university county saw 
a total increase of $23.4 million in VC funding over the 20 years sur-
rounding  both  the  Bayh-Dole  Act  and  the  regulatory  changes  that 
increased the supply of VC funding. Simply put, the Bayh-Dole Act shock 
to the supply of commercializable university innovation appears to ac-
count for more than 16 % of the increase in VC funding to these loca-
tions.  The  effect  on  VC  flows  is  magnified  in  university  areas  that 
received  the most  federal research funding before the  Bayh-Dole Act, 
further supporting the role of the policy change in releasing patented 
innovative  output  that  in  turn  draws  private  capital  investment,  and 
underscores  the  importance  of  federal  research  funding to  innovative 
hubs.1

While  we  focus  on  measuring  the  effect  of  a  plausibly  exogenous 
shock to commercializable innovation from universities on VC invest-
ment in a region, we also provide evidence of the Bayh-Dole Act’s effects 
on  broader  measures  of  interest  to  policy  makers,  such  as  corporate 
innovation and high growth entrepreneurial activity writ large. We find 
that,  in  addition  to  predicting  VC  disbursements,  ex-ante  (pre-1980) 
university  patenting  activity  (county-industry)  also  predicts  the  areas 
and  industries  of  post-Bayh-Dole  Act  corporate  patenting,  even  con-
trolling for prior corporate innovation activity. This finding is consistent 
with  the  findings  documented  in  Gross  and  Sampat  (2023) that  gov-
ernment research funding has extremely persistent effects on the clus-
tering  of  corporate  innovation.  Our  findings  are  consistent  with  VC 
investment playing a role in this process, and they support the notion 
that venture capitalists are able to find and fund the best new investment 
opportunities  as  they  emerge  from  university  ecosystems.  Using  data 
from Guzman and Stern (2020) on business registrants and their even-
tual  successful  exit  events,  we  further  show  (i)  that  ex-ante  commer-
cializable university innovation is a strong predictor of subsequent high 
growth entrepreneurial entry across areas; and (ii) that controlling for 
contemporary  VC  investment  nearly  eliminates  this  effect—in  other 
words, VCs identify precisely the high growth entrepreneurs launched 
by cutting edge university ideas. This new evidence suggests a mecha-
nism  through  which  commercializable  university  innovation  attracts 
VC:  the  entry of  high  growth entrepreneurs  into  university  areas  and 
industries related to those universities’ research strengths.

Second,  we  provide  policy-relevant  evidence  suggesting  that  the 
encouragement of commercializable innovation activity can draw cap-

nizing  the  strong  link  between  entrepreneurial activity  and  economic 
growth (Davis et al., 1998; Davis and Haltiwanger, 1992; Decker et al., 
2014; Fairlie et al., 2019; Guzman and Stern, 2020; Haltiwanger et al., 
2013; Jovanovic and MacDonald, 1994), policy makers often seek ways 
to  stimulate  and  support  entrepreneurial  activity  in  their  local  areas 
(Lerner,  2009).  Entrepreneurs  are  often  considered  to  be  drivers  of 
urban growth in general, and of innovation-driven growth in particular 
(Agrawal et al., 2010; Glaeser et al., 2015, 2010; Glaeser and Kerr, 2009; 
Hausman, 2020; Shane, 2004). Our findings suggest that one additional 
tool  in  the  policy  maker’s  toolbox  is  to  engage  in  policies  and  in-

allowing commercialization to be supported by private capital that then 
flows into the market in its wake. Such interventions designed to pro-
mote  the  supply  of  commercializable  innovation  may  be  more  cost 
effective than creating a government-backed fund or offering subsidies 
to VCs to enter a region.

Third, our paper contributes to a large literature on the sources of 
agglomeration  economies  that  generate  clusters  of  industrial  activity 
and  healthy  entrepreneurial  ecosystems.  Knowledge  spillovers,  input- 
output relationships (supply chains), and labor pooling have long been 
thought to drive the co-location of firms (Ellison et al., 2010; Greenstone 

et al., 2010; Marshall, 1890). Substantial theoretical and empirical ev-
idence supports the notion that innovation and entrepreneurship emerge 
in large part from the mixing of ideas in localities (Agrawal et al., 2008; 
Duranton and Puga, 2001; Glaeser et al., 1992) and that use of inno-
vation is disproportionately local (Jaffe et al., 1993; Kerr, 2010). Our 
paper illustrates the importance of input-output relationships in facili-
tating innovative clusters around universities, as VC is a critical input for 
many entrepreneurs and is allocated disproportionately locally, likely 
due to the ease of monitoring and advising proximate companies (Chen 
et al., 2010; Lerner, 1995).

Finally, our findings contribute to the growing literature on the in-

growth.  Universities  may  generate  positive  spillovers  for  their  local

2014;  Dittmar  and  Meisenzahl,  2022;  Moretti,  2004),  university

and  Meisenzahl,  2022;  Hausman,  2022).  Our  paper  demonstrates  an

2. Government-funded research and the Bayh-Dole Act of 1980

Since the Second World War, the U.S. federal government has been 
the country’s largest source of funding for research and development. 
Most university-based research is funded through grants. Grants provide 
money, equipment, or both to eligible researchers to carry out approved

projects  or  activities.  The  grantee  is  responsible  for  conducting  the 
project activities, reporting on progress, and preparing the results for 
publication.  Federal  research  funding  has  a  large  impact  on  the  pro-
ductivity of academic researchers (Jacob and Lefgren, 2011) and on the 
direction  of their  research  (Myers,  2020). A substantial  literature  has 
explored  the  effects  of  such  government  funding  for  research  on  the 
innovation  economy  (see  e.g.  Gruber  and  Johnson,  2019).  Federal 
funding has been found to spur the development of private-sector pat-
ents (Azoulay et al., 2019; Cockburn and Henderson, 1998; Jaffe, 1989), 
and cuts to federal funding have been shown shift university research 
funding  from  federal  to  private  sources,  leading  to  less  accessible 
innovation outputs that are often appropriated by the private funders 
(Babina et al., 2023).

Importantly,  most  of  the  technologies  developed  at  universities, 
whether funded by the government or by private funders, are embryonic 
at best, with the human capital of the academic inventors tied closely to 
the value of the invention as the inventor’s cooperation remains crucial 
to  its  commercialization.  Academic  researchers  are  more  focused  on 
novel, basic research that pushes forward the frontier of knowledge, and 
they  are  likely  to  shift  their  attention  to  competing  research  streams 
after initial invention, creating a contracting problem for commercial-
izing  academic innovation (Aghion et  al., 2008). Jensen and Thursby 
(2001) emphasize the embryonic state of the technologies licensed by 
universities. Commercialization of such research is in large part under-
taken  by  outside  entities,  who  invest  in  the  translational and  applied 
research necessary to derisk the technology and bring it to market in 
commercializable form, and who license the inventions for this purpose, 
typically in the form of patents or patent portfolios.2 These outside en-
tities include large public and private companies and startups, both VC- 
and  non-VC-backed.  Federally-funded  university  research  also  affects 
economic  activity  through  local knowledge  spillovers,  educated labor 
supply, and attraction of population to the local area (Andrews, 2023).
Until  the  1980s,  many  American  universities  were  reluctant  to 
engage  directly  in  commercialization  of  research.  Typically,  this 
avoidance  was  justified  by  arguments  that  patenting  and  licensing  of 
technology removed knowledge from the public domain. Many univer-
sities  feared  that  commercialization  compromised  the  academy’s 
commitment to open science, or that profit motives might undermine 
the purity of the scientific endeavor. Sampat (2006), for example, de-
scribes Columbia University’s reasoning that patenting activity “is not 
deemed  within  the  sphere  of  the  University’s  scholarly  objectives.” 
Many  top  research  universities  went  so  far  as  to  explicitly  forbid  the 
patenting of biomedical research (Mowery and Sampat, 2001).3

The legal regime further disincentivized commercialization of uni-
versity  research.  With  the  WWII  explosion  of  federal  funding  for 
research in universities, research organizations, and private companies, 
debates over the ownership of inventions resulting from such funding 
were fierce. Initially, most federal funding contracts stipulated that the 
government  would  retain  title  to  any  patents  that  resulted  from  its 
funding,  under  the  presumption  that  the  discoveries  from  publicly- 
funded  research  should  belong  to  the  public.  Each  federal  agency 

determined its own patent policy, with little uniformity across agencies 
(Gross and Sampat, 2024). Typically, rights to royalties or other income

The debate leading up to the passage of the Bayh-Dole Act focused 
primarily on the benefits of public knowledge and “open science” versus 
the benefits of incentives to innovate and commercialize research. One 
view  held  that  federally  financed  innovations  should  be  kept  in  the 
public  domain  to  maximize  potential  spillovers.  Countering  it  were 
those worried that private enterprise would not fully invest in discovery 
and commercialization without stronger intellectual property protection 
that  allowed  them  to  benefit  from  the  innovation  developed  in  the 
course of contract R&D, and that incentives were needed to encourage 
the marketing of federally-funded innovations to corporate developers 
who might choose to commercialize them. Concerns regarding the U.S.’s 
ability to maintain economic competitiveness added further pressure to 
provide incentives for innovation.

In  December  1980,  Congress  passed  the  Bayh-Dole  Act.  The  Act 
standardized  patent  policy  across  granting  agencies  and  reversed  the 
presumption  of  federal  title  to  inventions  developed  under  federally 
funded research. The Bayh-Dole Act gave universities the right to patent, 
own rights to, and keep royalty revenues from innovations developed 
using federal research funding. As quid pro quo, the Act also required 
universities  to  actively  promote  the  inventions’  commercialization, 
grant  the  federal  government  a  non-exclusive  license,  and  share  any 
royalties  with  the  inventor.  In  1984,  Congress  further  passed  the 
Trademark Clarification Act, which removed restrictions on the types of 
inventions universities could own, and on the transfer of property rights 
to other parties. Taken together, these laws significantly strengthened 
the  incentives  of  universities  and  faculty  to  produce,  patent,  and 
commercialize innovation.6

The Bayh-Dole Act required universities to establish mechanisms for 
disclosure of new inventions funded by federal research funds, report 
inventions to the sponsoring agency, promote the commercialization of 
technologies resulting from federally funded research, and share income 
from licensing of such inventions with the academic inventors. Univer-
sities  and  faculty  appear  to  have  responded  to  these  new  incentives, 
opening Technology Transfer Offices (TTOs, also sometimes referred to 
as Technology Licensing Office, or TLOs) at increasing rates in the 1980s

with collecting invention disclosures from university researchers, filing 
for  patent  protection,  forming  industry  partnerships,  and  licensing 
technologies to outside entities. Along with the creation of technology 
transfer offices, universities created new policies around the disclosure 
of  intellectual  property.  For  example,  in  1981,  in  response  to  the 
Bayh-Dole Act, Columbia University mandated the disclosure of all po-
tential inventions created within university research facilities (Mowery 
et al., 2015). TTOs experimented with different types of licensing op-
tions for their intellectual property. Areas of experimentation included 
contracts with varying levels of exclusivity and royalty structures, and 
licensing to spin-out startups in exchange for equity stakes in the new 
venture (Feldman et al., 2002). As controversies and disputes arose, such 
as  those  related  to  Harvard’s  exclusive  licensing  of  “OncoMouse”  in-

worked  to  understand how  to  adjudicate  between  commercial  oppor-
tunities and the norms of open science (Murray, 2010). As universities 
increasingly pursued licensing opportunities, many were able to enter 
into royalty-generating contracts, and some were able to generate sub-
stantial revenue, fostering the idea that technology licensing could serve 
as a significant source of funds to support a university’s mission (Colyvas 
et  al.,  2002;  Sampat,  2006).  In  addition,  commercializing  university 
inventions became a source of prestige.

Patenting from universities rose correspondingly (Henderson et al., 
1998), with the sharpest increase beginning in the late 1980s, as uni-
versity infrastructure adjusted to handle faculty disclosures, patent ap-
plications,  and  licensing  on  a  large  scale  (Thursby  et  al.,  2001). 
University  patenting  grew  substantially  over  time,  from  294  patents 
granted to universities in 1976 to over 2000 1990 (Fig. 2). Patenting also 
became more pervasive across universities; while only 132 universities 
had patented prior to the passage of the Bayh-Dole Act, 232 universities 
had  been  granted  at  least  one  patent  by  1995  and  340  by  2006.8
Although it is difficult to say whether faculty responded by producing 
more innovation after the Bayh-Dole Act, Lach and Schankerman (2008)
provide evidence suggesting that faculty responded to stronger royalty 
incentives by producing higher quality inventions.9

Overall, the Bayh-Dole Act, with its effects on both university culture 
and incentives, resulted in a significant shift in the relationship between 
universities and industry. While prior to the Act, large scale technology 
and idea transfer from universities was nearly impossible to achieve, the 
Act  strengthened  property  rights,  standardized  these  rights  across 
granting agencies, and provided significant economic incentives to both 
researchers and university administrations to engage in patenting and 
licensing activity.

3. Data and sample

3.1. Patent data

We  use  patent  data  for  several  purposes,  including  to  measure  (i) 
universities’  pre-Bayh-Dole  Act  innovative  strengths,  (ii)  corporate 
innovation  outcomes,  and  (iii)  the  pre-Bayh-Dole  Act  distribution  of 
corporate innovative activity across industries and areas as a counter-
factual target for VC flows. The National Bureau of Economic Research 
Patent Data Project provides a compiled version of publicly  available 
data from the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) on 
utility patents granted between 1976 and 2006 (Hall et al., 2001).10 The 
data  contain  year  of  patent  application  and  grant,  assignee,  assignee 
location, patent technology class, and forward and backward citations. 
Assignees (patent owners) may be individuals, U.S. or foreign corpora-
tions, U.S. or foreign governments, hospitals, or universities. We use the 
subset of patents assigned to universities and university-affiliated hos-
pitals  to  generate  our  sample  of  innovating  universities,  identify  the 
research fields in which each university is highly innovative, and con-
nect  these  research  fields  to  the  industries  that  may  use  a  particular 
research field’s innovations.11 Additionally, we use data on the distri-
bution of pre-1980 corporate patents to help distinguish the effects of 
the Bayh-Dole Act from that of the 1979 ERISA Prudent Man Rule, which 
may  have  increased  VC  funding  to  areas  with  strong  corporate 
innovation.

Because universities often chose not to patent inventions pre-Bayh- 
Dole  Act,  particularly  those  that  resulted  from  federally  funded 
research, we augment the NBER patent data in the pre-period using data 
from Gross and Sampat (2023) that consists of all patents that are based 
on federally-funded research, regardless of to whom they are assigned. 
Within this sample, we identify patents that have at least one inventor 
with a university affiliation, as described in Appendix A. This results in 
an additional 277 patents attributable to a university in the pre-period, 
which are added to the sample from the NBER patent data.

3.7. VC index

To estimate a plausibly causal effect of VC in certain analyses, we 
would  ideally  like  to  measure  the  presence  of  venture  capital  across 
geographic  areas  and  industries  in  a  manner  that  is  exogenous  to 
corporate innovation. Measuring pre-ERISA (pre-1979) VC allocations is 
a first step, since ERISA greatly increased the scale of funds available to 
VC firms for investment and thus provides useful variation over time. 
But  of  course,  even  before  ERISA,  VC  investments  were  presumably 
made where opportunities for useful innovation were greatest –  likely 
where corporate innovation was strong. Because corporate innovation

own  would  be  endogenous  to  ex-post  corporate  innovation.  We  thus 
follow Kortum and Lerner (2000) in scaling ex-ante VC in each county 
and industry by corporate R&D investments in the same locations. By 
using investment inputs rather than patenting outputs, we aim to control

3.8. Corporate patenting

Importantly,  VC  backed  startups  are  not  the  only  vehicle  for 
commercialization  of  university  research.  University  inventions  are 
licensed by established corporations as well. Using the designator in the 
NBER patent data that indicates a corporate assignee, we compute an 
overall measure of citation-weighted corporate patenting and raw count 
corporate patenting at the county-industry-year level.

3.9. High-growth entrepreneurship measures

To measure high growth entrepreneurship activity by county, we use 
data  provided  in  2020  by  the  Startup  Cartography  Project  (SCP), 
described in detail in Guzman and Stern (2020). These data are cate-
gorized by the business registration year of new firms and offer infor-
mation on these registrants’  eventual business outcomes. We use four 
variables calculated by the data providers: Entrants with Eventual Growth 
Events, Quality-Adjusted Quantity of Entrants, Entrant Quality Index, and 
Realized-to-Expected Eventual Growth Events.

3.10. Final sample

The sample that results from combining these various data sources 
contains  observations  at  the  county-industry-year  level,  where  in-
dustries are measured at the 3-digit SIC code level and the sample years 
are 1970–1990. Because there are often multiple universities and hos-
pitals  in  a  given  county  or  in  nearby  counties,  their  associated  treat-
ments (federal funding and the Innovation Index) are summed across 
universities  within  treated county-industries.  Thus, for  example, Mid-
dlesex  County,  MA,  is  treated  by  both  Harvard  and  MIT,  and  a 
biomedical industry in that county would be treated by the biomedical 
innovation index that is the sum of those from each school. We assume 
the Bayh-Dole Act treats only university counties (as defined in Section 
3.4) and only in the years after 1980.

4.2. Geographic decay

Our main results assume the university “treats”  only the county in 
which  it  is  contained.  Next,  we  explore  the  geographic  decay  of 
knowledge  spillovers  and  the  corresponding  local  nature  of  effects. 
Because  knowledge  spillovers  are  disproportionately  local  in  nature 
(Jaffe et al., 1993), we would expect our results to decay with distance, 
and we have already shown clear geographic decay in the county level 
analysis of Table 2. We now re-run our better-identified, county-industry 
analyses with two alternative datasets: one in which we code counties 
adjacent to university counties as treated and one in which we count as 
treated all counties within 75 miles of a sample research university. The 
estimates are as expected: the effect of the Bayh-Dole Act is smaller as we 
get further from the university (Appendix Table B6). The fact that these 
estimates decay with distance further supports the importance of local 
knowledge  spillovers—as  university-related  corporate  innovation  and 
entrepreneurship  increase  more  in  close  proximity  to  the  uni-
versity—and  of  localized  input-output  relationships,  as  VC  flows 
disproportionately to firms in the university’s immediate vicinity. In the 
absence  of  disproportionately  local  knowledge  spillovers,  university 
innovation would not draw VC funding to the local area—innovation 
could spread equally quickly to any region, however far away, and VC 
investment  would  follow  that  knowledge  spillover.  Our  estimates  in 
Appendix  Table  B6  thus  further  affirm  the  important  role  of  local 
knowledge spillovers in determining where commercialization of inno-
vative activity occurs.

4.5. Controlling for existing patterns of VC investment and corporate

The passage of the Bayh-Dole Act coincided with other regulatory 
changes that affected the amount of capital available for VCs to invest 
more generally. In particular, the Employee Retirement Income Security

this increased pool of VC also flowed disproportionately to university

clusters—particularly of a high growth, innovation-driven nature—is of 
critical interest to economists and policy makers alike. In this paper, we 
provide  evidence  that  innovative  activity  attracts  venture  capital 
financing,  which  in  turn  may  lead  to  the  production  of  additional 
innovation in the future.

Our  findings  have  several  policy  implications.  First,  intellectual 
property  policy  that  provides  incentives  for  the  commercialization  of 
university  innovation  appears  to  have  positive  effects  for  the  local 
economies nearby. Not only does the new, post-Bayh-Dole Act access to 
commercializable university innovation increase local agglomeration of 
related industries (Hausman, 2022), but also, as we show in this paper, 
the resulting rise in local industrial innovative activity draws venture 
capital,  a  crucial input  for the  high growth entrepreneurs  working  to 
develop university related ideas. The role of VC in this next step on the 
path of ideas from universities to private sector innovation is likely to be 
vital: VC is present in all highly successful clusters of innovation, and we 
provide evidence suggestive of its role in university-related high growth 
entrepreneurship.  The Bayh-Dole Act  thus  released  valuable commer-
cializable innovation to university economies after 1980, creating the 
opportunity  for  VC  dollars—which  may  otherwise  have  gone  to  feed 
established corporate innovation—to hone in on the frontier innovation 
being developed around universities.

Second,  our  results  inform  local  development  policy,  speaking 
particularly  to  policy  makers  seeking  to  cultivate  IDE  ecosystems. 
Because VC funds invest disproportionately locally (Chen et al., 2010), 
the  ability  to  draw  VC  to  a  region  to  fund  innovation  clusters  is 
considered  essential.  We  bring  new  identification  to  the  question  of 
whether local innovation can attract VC, showing that a positive shock 
to  frontier  commercializable  innovation  draws  private  capital  invest-
ment to the region. The importance of strong local innovation may be 
one  reason  why  policy  efforts  to  exogenously  provide  seed  capital  or 
attract venture capital to a region—such as tax breaks for early stage 
investment and the formation of local government backed funds—have 
met with mixed success (Denes et al., 2023; Lerner, 2009). The evidence 
in this paper suggests that spending money on programs to encourage 
local  commercializable  innovation  may  be  more  productive  in  devel-
oping  local  ecosystems  than  spending  on  programs  to  create  venture 
funds  directly.  Specifically,  by  encouraging  formal  technology  codifi-
cation and transfer, informal knowledge sharing, and density of skilled 
workers, policy makers can harness the substantial power universities 
have to stimulate local high growth entrepreneurial clusters.
