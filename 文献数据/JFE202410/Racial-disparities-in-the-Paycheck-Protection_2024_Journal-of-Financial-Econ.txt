1. Introduction

Consistent with contemporaneous research, we document that minority-owned firms were more likely than
observationally similar white-owned firms to receive PPP loans from nonbank lenders than from banks.
However, we show that this substitution to nonbanks was only partial, resulting in significantly lower PPP
take-up by minority-owned firms, particularly Black-owned ones. Location and firm characteristics explain
about two-thirds of the 25 percentage point disparity in PPP take-up by Black-owned firms. While there was
greater substitution to nonbanks in more racially biased locations, overall take-up was still lower in those
locations. Access to professional help with applications facilitated use of nonbanks and mitigated disparities.

The Paycheck Protection Program (PPP), authorized by the CARES
Act in March 2020, was a key component of the U.S. government’s
response to the adverse economic effects of the COVID-19 pandemic.
Under the PPP, the Small Business Administration (SBA) guaranteed
about $800 billion in low-interest loans made by financial institutions
to businesses with up to 500 employees, promising to forgive these
loans if borrowers maintained employment and certain other fixed
expenses. A number of concerns have been raised about the design of
this program, including whether it targeted firms most needing support,
whether it was subject to considerable amounts of fraud, and whether
it provided equal access to minority-owned firms.

This paper provides the first systematic micro-level analysis of the
take-up of PPP loans by minority-owned businesses. We document
significant disparities in PPP borrowing and explore the role of location,
firm characteristics, borrowing relationships, access to CPAs, and racial
bias in explaining these disparities. We do so by studying Florida
restaurants, exploiting state administrative data on restaurant licenses,
corporate records, voter registration, lien filings, and mortgage records,
as well as detailed information on restaurants from Yelp. Restaurant

license data give us the population of restaurants in Florida, almost
all of which should have been eligible to receive PPP loans. From
corporate records, we determine the identity of the restaurant owner.
Voter registration data tell us the restaurant owner’s self-identified
racial and Hispanic identity. Data on firms’ existing secured business
loans and on home mortgages taken out by the firm’s owners enable us
to examine the effect of prior borrowing relationships on PPP take-up.
Detailed data on restaurant characteristics from state licenses and Yelp
allow us to control for differences among restaurants that could affect
PPP loan supply and demand.

To help us understand racial disparities in the PPP, we also contrast
the PPP with the Economic Injury Disaster Loan (EIDL) program, an
existing SBA program that was significantly expanded to mitigate the
adverse economic impact of the pandemic. As part of this program,
the SBA made non-forgivable low-interest loans directly to small busi-
nesses without using financial intermediaries. It also made grants up to
$10,000 under the EIDL Advance program. Studying the EIDL program
in combination with the PPP allows us to control for differences in firm
and owner characteristics that might affect the demand for emergency

We start by documenting that Black-owned restaurants are 25.5
percentage points less likely than white-owned restaurants to receive
PPP loans. The difference is 10.7 percentage points for Hispanic-owned
restaurants and 2.3 percentage points for Asian-owned restaurants.
Female-owned restaurants are 4.8 percentage points less likely to re-
ceive PPP loans. All of these disparities are statistically significant at
1%, except for Asian-owned restaurants, which experience a disparity
that is statistically significant at 10%.

The disparities in overall PPP borrowing are driven by disparities in
bank borrowing. Black-owned restaurants are 34.6 percentage points
less likely than white-owned restaurants to receive PPP loans from
banks, while Hispanic-owned restaurants and Asian-owned restaurants
are 11.3 and 10.0 percentage points less likely to receive PPP loans
from banks. The difference for female-owned restaurants is 5.9 percent-
age points. Except for Hispanic-owned restaurants, these disparities in
bank borrowing tend to be offset by greater borrowing from nonbank
PPP lenders, largely fintechs. While the substitution is large enough
to eliminate disparities in PPP borrowing for Asian-owned restaurants,
this is not the case for Black-, Hispanic- and female-owned restaurants,
which are still less likely than white-owned restaurants to receive PPP
funding. Moreover, the racial disparities we document in the PPP do
not exist in the EIDL program; if anything, minority-owned restaurants,
particularly Hispanic-owned ones, are more likely to receive EIDL
loans. As noted, these loans are not intermediated by banks, but rather
are made directly by the SBA.

What explains these disparities in PPP take-up, particularly the
much lower rate of PPP borrowing by Black-owned restaurants? We
consider six potential explanations: (1) location; (2) firm characteris-
tics; (3) other sources of differences in demand for emergency support;
(4) pre-existing borrowing relationships; (5) access to professional help
with PPP applications; and (6) racial bias.

The first explanation, location, accounts for about 10%–25% of
the disparity in PPP borrowing by minority-owned businesses. Once
we include ZIP code fixed effects in our regressions, estimated dis-
parities in PPP borrowing fall from 25.5 to 19.0 percentage points
for Black-owned businesses and from 10.7 to 8.9 percentage points
for Hispanic-owned businesses. About two-thirds of the ZIP code fixed
effect can be explained by two ZIP code characteristics: bank branches
per capita and median household income. Restaurants in ZIP codes with
fewer bank branches per capita and lower household income are less
likely to get PPP loans.

We examine the second explanation, firm characteristics, by con-
trolling for restaurant size, age, and a variety of other characteristics
derived from Yelp data, including credit card acceptance and the
number of reviews and photos. Older, larger, and more heavily visited
and reviewed restaurants are more likely to receive PPP funding.
Controlling for these characteristics reduces the disparity for Black-
owned restaurants by another 10 percentage points, or 40% of the
unconditional disparity. However, Black-owned restaurants are still
9.2 percentage points less likely to receive PPP loans. For Hispanic-
owned restaurants, the disparity in PPP borrowing is cut by a similar
percentage with the inclusion of these firm characteristics, resulting in
a 5.7 percentage points disparity in PPP borrowing.

We next explore whether the estimated disparities in PPP take-up
could be explained by lower demand for PPP loans by minority-owned
restaurants, even though our extensive list of firm characteristics and
ZIP code fixed effects are meant to soak up most of the variation in
demand. On its face, this explanation of disparities is unlikely to have
much traction given that minority-owned restaurants substituted from
bank PPP loans to nonbank PPP loans and EIDL loans. Nevertheless,
minority-owned restaurants may have been more vulnerable to the
pandemic and thus more likely to shut down and not apply for PPP
loans. However, we do not find that this is the case; controlling for ob-
servable characteristics, minority-owned restaurants are no more likely
to shut down than white-owned restaurants. Moreover, our findings on
a restricted sample of surviving restaurants are similar to our findings
in the full sample. Controlling for restaurant visits during the pandemic

also does not affect the estimated racial disparities in PPP borrowing.
We also address the concern that racial disparities are driven by
lower demand for PPP loans from minority-owned restaurants by ex-
amining PPP take-up in a sample of firms that received grants of
$1,000 per employee, up to a total of 10 employees, as part of the
EIDL Advance program. Focusing on these grant recipients allows us
to analyze PPP borrowing in a sample of firms with a demonstrated
awareness of government emergency support programs and demand
for such support. It also allows us to directly control for the number of
employees. We find that among firms that receive EIDL Advance grants,
Black-owned businesses are significantly less likely to receive PPP loans
than white-owned businesses with the same number of employees.

Having ruled out differences in demand as an explanation of condi-
tional disparities in PPP take-up, we next investigate the role of bank
relationships, access to professional help with PPP applications, and
racial bias. Existing bank relationships may be associated with higher
take-up of PPP loans for a number of reasons. During the first round
of the PPP, banks may have prioritized their existing clients because
of limited funds and capacity to process applications (Bartik et al.,
2023; Balyuk et al., 2021; Li and Strahan, 2021). Although funding and
processing constraints were significantly relaxed in the second round,
banks still had to trade off the costs and benefits of processing PPP
applications. Some banks and loan officers may have felt that the cost
of helping small businesses with weaker documentation and without
access to professional help was high compared to the income generated
by successfully approved loans. Existing relationships, especially those
with credit exposures, would create an additional incentive for a bank
to help a small business navigate the PPP application process. Bank
borrowing relationships may also proxy for a firm’s financial sophistica-
tion, record keeping, and access to CPAs and other professional service
providers.

While we cannot measure an entire banking relationship, including
different types of loans, checking accounts, credit cards, and merchant
services, we use data on Uniform Commercial Code (UCC) filings to
determine whether a firm had outstanding secured loans.1 These loans
likely capture the main variation across restaurants in their use of
banking services, as almost all of the restaurants in our sample accept
credit cards and thus also use business checking accounts and merchant
services. We also use mortgage records to identify firms whose owners
had taken out home mortgages. We find that while minority-owned
restaurants are much less likely to have outstanding secured bank loans
or to have taken out home mortgages from banks, controlling for prior
bank borrowing does little to reduce the measured disparities in bank
PPP borrowing.

We next study whether disparities in access to professional services
— which many firms used to aid them in filling out their applications —
can help explain disparities in PPP take-up. To analyze this possibility,
we construct measures of access to Certified Public Accountants (CPAs).
The first measure is an indicator for whether a firm uses a CPA as
its registered agent in its corporate registration with the state.2 Our
second measure is the number of CPAs in the firm’s ZIP code, which
tries to capture the available supply of providers that could have helped
small firms with their PPP applications. We separately calculate the
total number of such CPAs as well as the number of Black CPAs, and
we interact these measures of access to CPAs with the minority-owner
dummies. We find that Black-owned restaurants benefit significantly
more from having a CPA as their registered agent and from having
Black CPAs in their ZIP codes. Black-owned firms with a CPA registered

Finally, we explore how racial bias may have affected both the
overall take-up of PPP loans as well as their source, i.e. bank versus
nonbank lenders. It is possible that there was racial bias in the way
banks processed PPP loan applications. Indeed, audit studies by the
National Community Reinvestment Coalition (NCRC) find that Black,
Hispanic, and female business owners received worse treatment from
banks in response to inquiries about PPP loans (National Community
Reinvestment Coalition, 2020b,a). It is also possible that even if there
was no discrimination in the application process at banks, a legacy of
past discrimination and poor treatment discouraged minority-owned
businesses from even approaching banks for a PPP loan.

We use measures of implicit and explicit racial bias from Project
Implicit, which offers online tests to measure a person’s implicit asso-
ciations and biases.4 Our findings indicate that Black-owned restaurants
in counties where white test takers exhibit more explicit and implicit
bias towards Black people are significantly less likely to receive PPP
funding from banks. The effect is large: a one standard deviation
increase in implicit bias is associated with a 19.1 percentage point
reduction in the probability that a Black-owned restaurant receives a
PPP loan from a bank. Moreover, in more racially biased counties,
Black-owned businesses are much more likely to substitute to nonbank
PPP loans and EIDL loans from the SBA, though the former effect is
statistically significant only when using implicit bias as our measure.
Because applications to nonbanks and the SBA are typically made
online, while applications to banks often involve more personal interac-
tion, there may have been less scope for racial bias in the application
process at nonbanks and the SBA. This may help explain why Black
business owners are more likely to use these funding sources.

Finally, we broaden our analysis to other industries and other
states. While we do not have the full population of eligible firms in
other industries to study PPP take-up, we can study disparities in PPP
borrowing among the sample of firms that received EIDL Advance
grants. Studying this sample has the further benefit of allowing us to
expand our sample to include firms in Georgia, Louisiana, and North
Carolina, other states with publicly available voter registration data
that includes self-reported race. With this larger sample, we can sepa-
rately control for the interaction between Black business ownership and
county characteristics that may be correlated with racial bias. We can
also ask whether minority-owned businesses benefit more from existing
bank borrowing relationships.5 This may be the case if minority-owned
firms are subject to greater administrative burdens or existing bank
borrowing relationships help firms overcome the effects of racial bias.
In regressions with ZIP code cross industry fixed effects and controls for
firm age, number of employees, and secured borrowing relationships,
Black- and Hispanic-owned businesses are significantly less likely to
receive PPP loans. The magnitudes of racial disparities are broadly

similar to our sample of Florida restaurants. Furthermore, Black-owned
businesses are even less likely to receive PPP loans in more racially
biased counties. Finally, the effect of existing borrowing relationships is
significantly stronger for Black- than for white-owned businesses, sug-
gesting that existing borrowing relationships help mitigate disparities
in PPP take-up but do not completely eliminate them.

Our paper is part of a now sizable literature studying the functioning
and impact of the PPP,6 including a number of recent studies on racial
disparities in the PPP. Wang and Zhang (2020) show that ZIP codes
with a greater percentage of Black residents had less PPP take-up,
measured by the ratio of PPP loans to the number of establishments
in a ZIP code. They find that less PPP take-up is related to a lower
concentration of branches of PPP-approved lenders in ZIP codes with
more Black residents. Consistent with this finding, Erel and Lieber-
sohn (2022) document that fintech lenders originated a larger share
of PPP loans in ZIP codes with a larger minority population share.
While these findings are important in that they suggest that location
matters in accessing PPP funding, our firm-level approach allows us
to look beyond location and examine the role of firm characteristics,
borrowing relationships, access to professional help, and racial bias.
Indeed, we show that ZIP code fixed effects account for only one-fifth of
racial disparities in PPP borrowing, while firm characteristics, access to
professional help, and racial bias have a much larger collective impact.
In a contemporaneous paper analyzing the full population of PPP
borrowers, Howell et al. (2024) show that Black-owned businesses that
receive PPP funding are less likely to get funded by small banks than
they are to get funded by either fintechs or the four largest banks. In
a similar vein, Fei (2022) use the sample of PPP borrowers matched
to Yelp-listed restaurants to show that minority-owned restaurants are
more likely to get their PPP loans from fintech lenders rather than from
banks. Both Howell et al. (2024) and our paper find that Black-owned
businesses are more likely to borrow from fintechs in more racially
biased locations and that controlling for prior banking relationships
does not meaningfully reduce average disparities in bank PPP funding.
All three papers suggest that online loan application processes attenuate
racial disparities in PPP take-up. Howell et al. (2024) show that adop-
tion of automated processes by banks is associated with an increase in
the share of their PPP lending to Black-owned businesses, suggesting
that automation could reduce racial disparities.

There are at least four distinctive features of our paper. First, and
most importantly, our focus on restaurants and then EIDL Advance
grant recipients allows us to analyze a set of firms that were likely to
qualify for the PPP. We are thus able to examine racial disparities in
PPP access, not just whether minority-owned firms are more likely to
get their PPP funding from banks or nonbanks. The prior literature has
been mostly silent on whether there are racial disparities in firm-level
PPP take-up. Our methodology allows us to conclude that even though
Black-owned businesses rely more on nonbank lenders, this substitution
is not enough to offset the disparity in bank PPP funding. Our analysis
further suggests that racial bias has real effects on PPP take-up.

Second, we provide novel evidence that lack of access to profes-
sional service providers such as CPAs is associated with significantly
larger disparities in PPP take-up. Our results point to an important
limitation of nonbank lenders in expanding access and reducing racial
disparities in access to credit. Because nonbanks — most of them
fintechs — have limited capacity to help firms fix any deficiencies in
their applications, fintechs work best when minority-owned businesses
have access to professional service providers.

gram, the other significant emergency support program for small busi-
nesses, which was expanded during the pandemic. We show that there

Fourth, by linking business owners to voter registration data, we

disparities as well as the conclusions one draws about the sources of
these disparities. Predicted race tends to underestimate unconditional
disparities, overstate the significance of location, and significantly un-
derestimate conditional disparities. Predicted race can also affect one’s
conclusions regarding interaction terms, i.e., cross-sectional variation
in disparities.

In a more recent paper, Chernenko et al. (2024) use the 2020 Small
Business Credit Survey conducted by the Federal Reserve Banks to study
the relative importance of disparities in loan applications and approvals
in explaining racial disparities in the PPP. Using a very different sample
and set of controls, Chernenko et al. (2024) find remarkably similar
unconditional and conditional disparities in the take-up of PPP loans.
They show that racial bias affects both application behavior and loan
approvals, and they provide evidence that residual disparities in the
take-up of PPP loans are likely due to disparities in the administrative
burdens experienced by Black-owned firms in determining eligible loan
amounts and providing documentation for the requested loan amounts.
Our finding that disparities are mitigated when Black-owned firms have
greater access to CPAs is consistent with the explanation put forward
in Chernenko et al. (2024).

In addition to contributing to the literature on the PPP, our paper
contributes to a broader literature on discrimination in small business
lending. Blanchflower et al. (2003), Cavalluzzo and Wolken (2005),
Blanchard et al. (2008), and Fairlie et al. (2022) use survey data to
show that minority-owned businesses are more likely than white-owned
businesses to be turned down for bank loans and are less likely to apply
for loans for fear of being turned down. Fairlie et al. (2022) show that
these effects are stronger in locations with greater racial bias, consistent
with our findings. These studies suggest that our findings could, in
part, be driven by a historical legacy of discrimination that discouraged
Black-owned businesses from applying for PPP loans from banks.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes
our data and reports basic summary statistics. Section 3 documents
the existence of racial disparities in PPP borrowing, showing that
they are driven by disparities in PPP borrowing from banks and that
there is only a partial substitution to nonbank sources of PPP funding.
We also document the role of location and restaurant characteristics
in explaining racial disparities in PPP borrowing. Section 4 provides
evidence against the view that racial disparities in PPP borrowing can
be explained by lower demand from minority-owned businesses for
emergency loans. We examine the role of bank relationships, access to
CPAs, and racial bias in Section 5. Section 6 broadens our analysis to
other industries and states by studying PPP take-up by EIDL Advance
recipients in Florida, Georgia, Louisiana, and North Carolina, states
with publicly available voting records that identify racial and Hispanic
identity. Section 7 concludes.

2.1. PPP and EIDL loans

Data on approved PPP and EIDL loans are from the SBA website.7
PPP loan data include all loans approved during the periods April 3–
August 9, 2020, and January 11–June 30, 2021. EIDL data include
loans approved through November 14, 2020. The SBA has not yet
released loan-level data on the EIDL loans made after this date. In 2020,
the SBA made 3.6 million EIDL loans for a total of $194 billion, while
in 2021 the SBA made about 3.8 million EIDL loans for a total of $284
billion. However, most of our analyses compare PPP and EIDL loans
extended during 2020. Both PPP and EIDL data report the borrower’s
name and location, loan amount, and approval date. PPP loan data also
report the lender’s name and location, the borrower’s industry, and self-
reported demographic information on the borrower. The vast majority
of PPP borrowers (83%) do not report information on their racial and
Hispanic identity.

After limiting the sample to borrowers located in Florida and ex-
cluding non-profit organizations, we match borrowers by name to
Florida corporate records. Details of the matching algorithm are de-
scribed in the Internet Appendix. We are able to identify 87.9% of all
PPP borrowers and 84.5% of all EIDL borrowers. Most of the unmatched
borrowers are individuals.

2.2. Potential borrowers

Much of the existing research on the PPP is constrained by the
limited data on small private firms, which comprise most eligible
borrowers. We overcome this limitation by studying the take-up of
PPP and EIDL loans by Florida restaurants.8 Because essentially all
restaurants were eligible for PPP and EIDL loans and because Florida
restaurants are subject to state licensing, we have comprehensive and
reliable data on the population of eligible firms. We obtain the list of
all Florida restaurant licenses from the Florida Department of Business
& Professional Regulation.9

rants that offer seating, while excluding food trucks, takeout-only
restaurants, and caterers. We also exclude restaurants with licenses
approved after February 15, 2020, because they would not have been
eligible for the PPP. Finally, we exclude hotel restaurants and franchise
restaurants because they are frequently owned and operated by affili-
ated entities. As a result, it can be difficult to determine whether a given
hotel or franchise restaurant received an emergency loan or benefited
from a parent receiving an emergency loan.10 Restaurants are classified

2.3. Racial and Hispanic identity of potential borrowers

We classify firms based on the first officer or director listed in
the firm’s corporate record whose racial and Hispanic identity we
can ascertain.14 For brevity, we refer to this individual as the owner,
although it is possible that this individual manages the firm without
having any ownership.

Howell et al. (2024) use a machine learning algorithm to predict
racial and Hispanic identity in the PPP sample based on the officer’s
name and the location of the business. Regression results that use
predicted racial and Hispanic identity must be interpreted with caution
because measurement error will cause the coefficients on the predicted
identity to understate the true effect of racial and Hispanic identity,
with the magnitude of the bias depending on the relative accuracy with
which the algorithm classifies different groups.

In addition to introducing biases in the estimates of the direct effect
of racial and Hispanic identity, name-based algorithms could introduce
biases in the indirect effects of racial and Hispanic identity to the extent
that the measurement error is correlated with firm and location char-
acteristics. Fryer and Levitt (2008) show that the use of distinctly Black
names varies over time and is strongly correlated with socioeconomic
status. Thus, for example, if there is less measurement error in more
racially biased locations, it is possible that the estimated disparities in
PPP take-up in more racially biased locations are overstated because
the racial bias measure is really capturing the extent of measurement
error in the name-based algorithm. Similarly, if Black-owned businesses
are less likely to have existing bank relationships, then measures of
bank relationships may provide incremental information relative to a
name-based algorithm about whether the business owner is Black.

To overcome the limitations of name-based algorithms, we take ad-
vantage of Florida voter registration data, which report each registered
voter’s self-identified racial and Hispanic identity.15 Voter registrants
can identify themselves as one of the following: (1) American Indian or
Alaskan Native; (2) Asian or Pacific Islander; (3) Black, not Hispanic;

(4) Hispanic; (5) white, not Hispanic; (6) other; or (7) multi-racial.16
Because of the small number of observations, we include voters self-
identified as American Indian or Alaskan Native or multi-racial in the
‘‘other’’ category. For brevity, we refer to ‘‘Asian or Pacific Islander’’
as Asian, ‘‘Black, not Hispanic’’ as Black, and ‘‘white, not Hispanic’’
as white. These classifications are imperfect, in part because they
do not separate race from ethnic identity. Notably, unlike the Cen-
sus, Florida voter registration only offers ‘‘Black’’ and ‘‘Hispanic’’ as
mutually exclusive categories.

We match the firm’s owner to voter registration data based on name
and location. Details of the matching algorithm are provided in the
Internet Appendix. We are able to identify a restaurant owner’s racial
and Hispanic identity for 70% of restaurants. In most of these cases, we
have a unique match within a county. If there are multiple potential
matches, but they all report the same racial and Hispanic identity, we
use what is reported even though we cannot identify the specific voter
match.

One further advantage of using voter registration data is that by
conditioning on the owner being a registered voter, we exclude some
restaurants that may have been ineligible for PPP funding.17 The down-
side is that the sample of registered voters may not be representative
of the population of restaurant owners. In particular, registered voters
may be more aware of government interventions such as the PPP and
thus may be more likely to apply for a PPP loan.

2.4. Restaurant characteristics

Because restaurant license and corporate records data have lim-
ited information on restaurant characteristics, we supplement these
administrative records data with restaurant characteristics from Yelp, a
platform for crowd-sourced information and reviews about restaurants
and other businesses. Using name, location, and phone information, we
are able to match 89% of restaurants to Yelp. Most of the unmatched
restaurant licenses are operated by golf clubs, museums, and other
establishments.

Our version of Yelp data includes information on restaurant fea-
tures, such as whether the restaurant accepts credit cards, offers de-
livery, or has outside seating. We use features that were in place just
before the pandemic. We also have user activity measures, including the
number of reviews and photos posted each week, the average rating,
and the number of page visits. The cumulative number of reviews since
inception and average ratings are as of February 2020. For restaurants
without reviews, we set the average rating to 0 and include a dummy
for no reviews in our regression analyses. We also include the average
number of page views and photos posted each month over the March
2019–February 2020 period.

2.6. Bank relationships

We measure borrowing relationships using Uniform Commercial
Code (UCC) filings.18 Lenders file UCC financing statements to establish
priority in the collateral pledged to them. Using Florida UCC filings
data,19 we match debtors to Florida corporate records. We match
lenders to the National Information Center (NIC) and Capital IQ, and
we classify them as bank or nonbank. For each restaurant in our sample,
we then check whether the firm had an active UCC filing and whether
the underlying loan was from a bank or a nonbank.

We next consider whether restaurant characteristics affect PPP loan
demand and supply. Thus, in column 4 we add firm age and size
controls, using the log number of restaurant seats as a proxy for
size. We also add a variety of restaurant characteristics from Yelp,
all measured before the pandemic. Larger and older restaurants are
significantly more likely to receive PPP loans, as are those that accept
credit cards and have more Yelp customer reviews and posted photos.
These variables presumably measure how well-established and popular
the restaurant was before the pandemic. There are two reasons to
include these controls. The first is that these characteristics could help
control for PPP loan demand if smaller, less established, and less pop-
ular restaurants were less profitable and thus more likely to shut down
during the pandemic and not apply for a PPP loan. The second is that
these characteristics could control for loan supply as PPP lenders may
have prioritized larger, more established, and more popular restaurants.
This could be because they were more likely to be existing customers
of the lender. It is also possible that lenders prioritized them because
there were more fees from making larger PPP loans or because these

restaurants were more likely to survive and become future customers
of the lender.

Importantly, controlling for the firm characteristics described above
reduces the magnitude of the coefficients on Black- and Hispanic-
owned restaurants by about half—from 19.0 to 9.2 percentage points
for Black-owned restaurants and from 8.9 to 5.7 percentage points for
Hispanic-owned restaurants. These estimated disparities are still statis-
tically significant. With the full set of controls, there are no statistically
significant disparities for Asian- and female-owned restaurants.

4. Demand for PPP loans

As previously noted in Section 3 above, an alternative explanation of
our finding of disparities in PPP take-up is that, despite our best efforts,
we have not adequately controlled for differences in firm characteristics
that could affect the demand for PPP loans. This alternative explanation
is inconsistent with the finding that minority-owned businesses make
greater use of nonbank PPP loans and EIDL loans. Nevertheless, to
further and more directly address any remaining concerns, this section
explores whether minority-owned restaurants had lower demand for
emergency loans. We first examine whether observable differences
between minority- and white-owned restaurants during the pandemic
could have driven a difference in the demand for emergency loans.
We then restrict the sample to restaurants with demonstrated demand
for emergency support to see whether the estimated disparities ex-
ist within this subsample. Both approaches suggest that differential
demand cannot explain our findings.

white-owned restaurants. This might be the case if minority-owned

4.2. Unobservable differences in demand for PPP loans

Although our regression specifications control for an extensive set
of observable restaurant characteristics — both before and during
the pandemic — there are possibly unobservable differences in de-
mand that could help explain racial disparities in PPP borrowing.
One possibility is that minority-owned restaurants were less aware
of the PPP. Indeed, in a survey conducted by the Federal Reserve,
20% of Black-owned businesses cited a lack of awareness of the PPP
as a reason why they did not apply; only 7% of white-owned non-
applicants cited this reason (Federal Reserve Banks, 2021). Even after
controlling for observable differences between Black- and white-owned
firms, Black-owned firms are 5% more likely to state this reason for not
applying (Chernenko et al., 2024). Another possibility is that minority-
owned restaurants may have had fewer employees even controlling
for location and restaurant activity. Since the size of a PPP loan is
based on payroll, restaurants with fewer employees receive smaller
loans and thus may have found that the costs of applying for a PPP
loan outweighed the benefits.

We address these concerns by exploiting data on the EIDL Advance
program.24 The EIDL Advance program provided grants of $1,000 per
employee, up to 10 employees. About half of the restaurants in our
sample received EIDL Advance grants. These restaurants were obvi-
ously aware of the EIDL program and thus very likely to have been
aware of other emergency relief programs like the PPP. At the same
time, at $1,000 per employee, EIDL Advance grants were very modest
compared to the PPP, which provided forgivable loans of 2.5 times
monthly employee wages. PPP loans would exceed the size of the EIDL
Advance grant provided a worker earned more than $400 per month, a
very modest amount. Although applying for a PPP loan after receiving
an EIDL Advance grant involved additional costs and although PPP
loans came with some strings attached to them, 80% of restaurants
in our sample that received an EIDL Advance grant also applied for
and received a PPP loan.25 Thus, by examining racial disparities in PPP
borrowing among restaurants that received EIDL Advance grants, our
analysis focuses on a sample of firms that wanted emergency loans and
were aware of their availability.

capped at $10,000, we include an indicator variable for firms with at
least ten employees. As an alternative approach, we restrict the sample
to firms with fewer than 10 employees.

Finding racial disparities in PPP borrowing in the subsample of EIDL
Advance participants shows that racial disparities in the PPP cannot be
explained by lower demand for emergency support by minority-owned
businesses or less awareness of such support programs. Another way
to establish this is to look at the subsample of restaurants that receive
EIDL or PPP loans. Similar to our analysis of EIDL Advance program
participants, we can ask whether there are racial disparities in PPP take-
up among the sample of firms that receive EIDL or PPP loans. These
restaurants have a demonstrated demand for emergency loans and an
awareness that such programs exist. In Table 6, we find that there are
racial disparities in PPP take-up within this subsample of firms, with
Black- and Hispanic-owned restaurants both significantly less likely to
receive PPP loans. As before, the disparities are driven by disparities in
bank borrowing. Minority-owned restaurants are less likely to borrow
from banks even controlling for ZIP code and restaurant characteristics.
In particular, Black-owned restaurants receiving an emergency loan
are 20.5 percentage points less likely than white-owned restaurants to
receive a PPP loan from a bank. This finding further supports the view
that unobserved differences across groups in emergency loan demand
or loan awareness do not explain disparities in PPP borrowing.

5.1. Bank relationships

This section explores the role that bank relationships could play in
explaining racial disparities in PPP borrowing. Banks may have been
more likely to help their existing clients complete their PPP applications
or correct mistakes in the submitted applications. If minority-owned
businesses had relatively weak relationships with banks, then we would
expect to see racial disparities in bank PPP borrowing without adequate
controls for these relationships. Numerous studies have shown that
Black- and Hispanic-owned businesses are more likely to be turned
down for loans, controlling for credit quality and personal wealth, and
less likely to apply for loans (Blanchflower et al., 2003; Cavalluzzo
and Wolken, 2005; Fairlie et al., 2022). In addition, minority-owned
businesses report greater dissatisfaction with their financial services

providers (Federal Reserve Banks, 2021). This evidence suggests that,
relative to white-owned restaurants, minority-owned restaurants may
have weaker relationships with banks.

While we do not observe all aspects of a restaurant’s banking rela-
tionships, restaurants in the sample likely have some kind of banking
relationship because they must have a bank account to receive credit
card payments. Nevertheless, some may have stronger bank relation-
ships than others because they use a wider variety of banking services
or they have been bank customers for a longer time. To get at the
strength of a firm’s relationship with a bank, we collect data on whether
a firm has outstanding bank loans. Although we cannot observe all of a
firm’s loans, we can observe secured loans except those secured by real
estate. According to Luck and Santos (2023), out of all loans to firms
with less than $50 million in assets by banks that are subject to the
Federal Reserve’s stress tests, only 3.6% are unsecured and 22% are
secured by real estate. Furthermore, Blanchflower et al. (2003) show
that Black-owned firms borrow with credit cards at the same rate as
white-owned firms. Thus, it is likely that by observing non-mortgage
secured loans, we are observing the most meaningful cross-sectional
variation in a firm’s borrowing.

As noted in the data section, we use Florida Uniform Commer-
cial Code (UCC) financing statements to measure secured borrowing
relationships between restaurants and their lenders, both banks and
nonbanks. Lenders file these financing statements to assert their secu-
rity interest in a loan. The security interest could be in physical capital,
such as equipment, or a general lien on the business. We consider
a restaurant to have a secured borrowing relationship with a lender
if there was an active UCC filing by that lender as of February 15,
2020. As shown in Table 2, the percentage of Black-owned restaurants
that have UCC loans with banks (4%) is considerably lower than it is
for white-owned restaurants (19%), although the percentages are quite
similar for loans from nonbanks (14%).

Existing UCC borrowing relationships could have different effects
for minority- and white-owned firms. If minority-owned firms with-
out existing bank relationships have a harder time getting help from
banks than white-owned firms without such relationships, then existing
bank relationships may have a stronger effect on the probability that
minority-owned firms receive PPP loans from banks. On the other hand,
among their existing clients, banks may prioritize white-owned firms.
Minority-owned businesses, for example, report greater dissatisfaction
with their financial services providers (Federal Reserve Banks, 2021).
Unfortunately, our restaurants sample has relatively few minority-
owned firms with existing bank relationships. There are only 20 Black-
owned restaurants with existing bank relationships. The small number
of minority-owned firms with existing bank relationships makes it
difficult to reliably estimate differential effects of UCC borrowing rela-
tionships. We therefore postpone the examination of differential effects
of UCC borrowing relationships until Section 6, where we study the
take-up of PPP loans in a much larger sample of firms across all
industries.

5.1.1. Home mortgages

Another dimension of bank relationships is home mortgages taken
out by the firm’s owners. To measure such relationships, we search
county records for mortgages taken out by the firm’s owners. This
search is limited to officers that were previously matched to registered
voters. Because we match officers and mortgagors exclusively on name,
we exclude cases where multiple registered voters in a county share the
same name. As a result, we cannot measure home mortgage borrowing
relationships for some firms in our sample. Overall, we are able to
measure mortgage borrowing for 9,967 out of 12,812 restaurant owners
in the sample.

Because it is difficult to track mortgages over time, we define our
bank (nonbank) mortgage variable to be an indicator for whether any of
the firm’s officers took out a mortgage from a bank (nonbank) lender
over the ten years before February 15, 2020. Thus it is possible that
some owners had repaid their mortgages before February 15, 2020 and
had no active relationship at the time. It is also possible that some
owners had active mortgages that they took out before February 15,
2010, and that they had not refinanced since then. Finally, note that
we limit our search to mortgages in the county in which an officer is
registered as a voter. It is possible that some officers have homes and
mortgages in other counties.

In our sample, 41% of restaurants have at least one officer who has
taken out a home mortgage during the ten years before the pandemic.

5.2. Access to professional services

While the PPP application itself was short, there were a number
of documentation requirements that many small businesses had diffi-
culty complying with. For example, as described in Chernenko et al.
(2024), sole proprietors with paid employees had to submit Form 1040,
Schedule C, along with four quarters of employer’s quarterly federal tax
return (Form 941), four quarters of state unemployment tax returns,
and proof of contributions to any benefits programs. Some lenders
also required other documents to comply with the Bank Secrecy Act
and Anti-Money Laundering regulations. In addition, there were some
ambiguities — particularly early on in the program — about what
qualified as eligible expenses. Some of the instructions for determining
the eligible amount could be difficult to interpret for less financially-
sophisticated small business owners. Thus, many businesses relied on
accountants and lawyers, as well as other professionals, to help them
fill out PPP applications and submit the required documents. Firms
that previously used accountants and lawyers were also likely better
positioned to access the documentation required for the application.

In this section, we ask whether the lower take-up of PPP loans by
Black-owned firms can be explained by whether they had less access to
accountants. To study the role of access to accountants in facilitating
PPP take-up, we use data on Florida CPA licenses.30 We construct
two measures of CPA access. First, we check whether we can match
each firm’s registered agent to the CPA license data on CPA firms and
individuals. A registered agent is a business or individual designated to
receive notice of legal or administrative actions affecting the firm. A
firm’s registered agent may be one of its officers or a third party such
as a lawyer or CPA. Black-owned businesses that use accountants as
registered agents are more likely to have access to an accountant to
help with their PPP applications and likely have greater access to the
documents they needed.

5.3. Racial bias

In this section, we look at the effect of racial bias on PPP borrowing.
Specifically, we ask whether minority-owned restaurants are less likely
to receive PPP loans in locations with more racial bias in the white
population. We use data on explicit and implicit racial bias collected

by Project Implicit.34 Project Implicit offers a number of online implicit
association tests that test takers anywhere in the world can take to mea-
sure their implicit social attitudes. We use the results of the Race test,
also known as the Black/White test, which measures an individual’s
implicit preference for white over Black people.35 The test also asks
subjects about their explicit preferences on a seven-point scale, where
1 is ‘‘I strongly prefer African Americans to European Americans’’, 4 is
‘‘I like European Americans and African Americans equally’’, and 7 is
‘‘I strongly prefer European Americans to African Americans’’.

Implicit bias has been used extensively in social sciences and has
been shown to be associated with discriminatory behavior. Glover et al.
(2017) show that the implicit bias of grocery store managers affects the
performance of minority cashiers. On days when they are supervised by
biased managers, minority cashiers are more likely to be absent from
work and take longer to scan items and check out customers. Hehman
et al. (2018) show that local implicit racial bias is associated with
disproportionate use of lethal force against Black people.

We calculate the county-level averages across white respondents
taking the test between 2008–2019. The median county has 347 valid
responses. Glades County has the fewest responses, just 14. Fig. 1 plots
county-level averages of explicit and implicit bias. Larger values and
darker colors indicate more substantial bias. The correlation between
explicit and implicit bias across all counties is 0.33. The correlation
almost doubles to 0.58 when we limit the sample to 42 counties with
at least 100 responses.

6. Analysis of other industries and states

Since restaurants were hit especially hard by the pandemic, one
may wonder whether our results apply to other industries. Because
we cannot identify the population of potential borrowers in other
industries, we restrict our analysis to firms receiving other forms of
government support that had similar eligibility criteria as the PPP.
Specifically, we examine whether there are racial disparities in the
take-up of PPP loans among the broader sample of firms that received
EIDL Advance grants, just as we did in Table 5 for restaurants. One
advantage of this approach is that because we are no longer limited to
restaurants, we can expand our sample to include firms in other states
with voter registration data that include self-reported racial and ethnic
identity and are available to researchers at a modest cost. These states
are Georgia, Louisiana, and North Carolina.38

To create this broader sample, we start by matching EIDL Advance
recipients to Florida, Georgia, Louisiana, and North Carolina corporate
records, which we access through OpenCorporates, an open database
of corporations around the world. We limit the resulting sample to
for-profit corporations and LLCs that were active as of February 15,
2020. We next match PPP borrowers to these corporate records and
use this link to determine which EIDL Advance recipients also received
PPP loans.

Because neither EIDL Advance nor OpenCorporates data include
industry classification, we match firms to the Dun & Bradstreet database
and use the firm’s primary SIC code in Dun & Bradstreet. Firms with
SIC code 9999, non-classifiable establishments, are excluded from the

analysis. We estimate regressions with ZIP code cross two-digit SIC
industry fixed effects to compare minority- and white-owned firms that
are in the same industry and same ZIP code; this corresponds to the
approach in our analysis of Florida restaurants.

We match each firm’s officers, directors, and agents to voter reg-
istration data using the same algorithm we use for our restaurants
sample. However, we make two adjustments to our method of clas-
sifying firms based on the racial and ethnic identity of their officers.
First, while Florida corporate records always report both registered
agents who are designated to receive correspondence from the state and
officers/directors who are authorized to act on behalf of the firm, not
all other states do the same. In particular some states, including Georgia
and North Carolina, report the registered agents but not necessarily
officers of LLCs. With some firms using professional registered agents
like CPAs and lawyers, we do not want to classify firms based on
the racial identity of such professional agents. We therefore exclude
individuals who are listed as registered agents of five or more firms.39
Second, in contrast to Florida corporate records, other states in our
sample are less likely to list officers in order of seniority. Thus, instead
of using the racial identity of the first listed officer we can match to
voter registration, we set minority indicators equal to one if any of the
matched officers report their racial or ethnic identity.

of county population, (b) the share of county population that identifies

restaurants the difference is a more modest 5.6 percentage points, and
for Asian-owned restaurants and female-owned restaurants there is no
appreciable difference in PPP take-up.

Disparities in PPP borrowing are driven by disparities in PPP bor-
rowing from banks. Black-owned restaurants are 16.8 percentage points
less likely than white-owned restaurants to borrow from banks, while
Hispanic-owned restaurants are 5.8 percentage points less likely, and
Asian-owned restaurants are 6.1 percentage points less likely. Non-
banks — largely fintechs — make up for a portion of these disparities
by lending at greater rates to Black-owned restaurants (7.6 percentage
points), and they make up the entire difference for Asian-owned restau-
rants. Hispanic-owned restaurants borrow from nonbanks at the same
rate as white-owned restaurants.

The disparity in bank PPP borrowing for Black-owned restaurants
appears to be exacerbated by racial bias. Restaurants located in coun-
ties where more white people express implicit or explicit biases towards
Black people are less likely to receive PPP loans from banks. They
are also more likely to substitute to nonbank PPP loans and EIDL
loans. We see this basic pattern across a wide range of industries in
Florida, Georgia, Louisiana, and North Carolina. Racial bias may have
affected banks’ administration of PPP loans, or it may have reflected
a legacy of bias that deterred Black restaurant owners from applying
for PPP loans from banks. Our methodology cannot tell apart these
two explanations, but our findings suggest that online, less personalized
applications can help mitigate bias.41 Both fintechs and the SBA used

At the same time, our results point to an important limitation of
fintech lenders in expanding access and reducing racial disparities in
access to credit. Because fintechs have limited capacity to help firms
fix any deficiencies in their applications, fintechs work best when
minority-owned businesses have access to professional help. Having
just one more Black CPA in a firm’s ZIP code is associated with a 4.3
percentage point higher take-up of nonbank PPP loans by Black-owned
firms.

Our findings also point to potential issues with the design of the
PPP that may have inadvertently affected the access of minority-owned
businesses to PPP loans. While our focus has been on understanding

racial disparities after controlling for differences in restaurant attributes
— including proximity to bank branches, age, size, and borrowing
history — these differences led to substantially lower PPP take-up by
minority-owned restaurants. Indeed, these differences explain 60% of
the disparity in borrowing by Black- and Hispanic-owned restaurants.
While it is possible that some of the attributes of minority-owned
restaurants may have lowered their demand for PPP loans, it is also
possible that there were impediments to getting PPP loans. Thus, quite
apart from issues of racial bias, a program that effectively favored
larger businesses in close proximity to banks or with strong borrowing
relationships with banks, even if it did so unintentionally, was less
successful than it might have been in serving the emergency funding
needs of minority-owned businesses.
