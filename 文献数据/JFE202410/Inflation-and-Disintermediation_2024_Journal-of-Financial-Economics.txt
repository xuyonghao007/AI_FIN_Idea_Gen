A R T I C L E I N F O

A B S T R A C T

We test a bank credit channel through which unexpected increases in inflation lead to short-run macroeconomic
fluctuations. For identification, we study an unexpected U.S. inflation increase in early 1977 and exploit dif-
ferences in state-level reserve requirements for Federal Reserve nonmember banks, which create differences in
banks’ inflation exposures. More exposed banks reduce lending, lowering local house prices and construction
employment. We provide evidence for potential mechanisms, including a bank net wealth and a loan misallo-
cation channel. Our results suggest that an important consequence of inflation is its impairment of the banking
sector.

1. Introduction

A central question in monetary economics is whether increases in
inflation lead to short-run macroeconomic fluctuations and,
if so,
through which channels.1 In the “neutrality of money” view, unexpected
increases in inflation have little aggregate real effects even in the short
run, as inflation merely changes the units of measurement. In line with
this view, Shiller (1997) argues that the public’s dislike of inflation
mainly reflects an incorrect belief that wage growth lags price inflation.
Other macroeconomic theories, however, posit reasons for why rising

inflation may be “non-neutral,” causing aggregate real effects. These
theories tend to focus on the role of nominal rigidities in labor markets
and nonfinancial firms; however, they are ambiguous about the direc-
tion of the effect. For example, New Keynesian models generally imply
that small, unexpected increases in inflation can increase output and
employment by relaxing nominal wage rigidity constraints (e.g., Ball
et al., 1988). Macroeconomic models with financial frictions also predict
that small, unexpected increases in inflation can increase output by
reducing the real debt burden of financially constrained agents (e.g.,
Bernanke et al., 1999). These classes of theories thus have a difficult

uncertainty, tax distortions, or non-indexation of contracts, increases in
inflation can have a negative impact on economic performance (e.g.,

This paper argues that much of the prior academic research and
policy work on inflation, both regarding the U.S. and international set-
tings, has overlooked a key class of channels—banking channels—-
through which rising inflation can lead to short-run macroeconomic
fluctuations. Banking channels have been widely explored in empirical
financial research as key propagation mechanisms through which
monetary policy or shocks to bank net wealth can lead to changes in
output and employment (e.g., Kashyap and Stein, 2000; Jim´enez et al.,
2012, 2014; Chodorow-Reich, 2014). Higher nominal interest rates
caused by rising inflation might therefore be a major source of impair-
ment to the banking sector, either due to banks’ interest rate mismatch
or through other mechanisms. This paper’s main contribution is to link
these widely understood banking channels from the finance literature to
the macroeconomic discussion of inflation, which has, for the most part,
abstracted away from the role of the financial sector. To do so, our paper
exploits a quasi-experimental setting from the U.S. in the 1970s to
demonstrate how rising inflation can be contractionary for both bank
lending and the macroeconomy. As a result, our paper—along with a
(2020)—provides a
contemporaneous paper by Dreschler et al.,
compelling mechanism to help explain stagflation in both U.S. and in-
ternational settings.

Our main hypothesis, that rising inflation tends to be contractionary
for bank lending, is motivated by two initial descriptive findings that we
uncover. First, in an unbalanced country-level panel from 1870 to 2016,
we find that large increases in inflation are correlated with future short-
run decreases in bank credit-to-GDP. Second, within historical and in-
ternational inflation episodes for which we obtain bank-level data-
—ranging from France and Germany in the 1920s to emerging
economies in recent decades—we find that the lending contraction is
driven primarily by the banks that are most exposed to inflation in-
creases, as measured by a balance-sheet–based measure of a bank’s
inflation exposure described below.

However, we view these results as descriptive findings to motivate
our hypothesis, given several identification challenges in interpreting
such findings. One issue is that rising inflation may in itself cause little
macroeconomic harm but may simply be a symptom of underlying
problems, such as fiscal imbalances, supply-side disruptions, or a cur-
rency depreciation. Although the second descriptive finding helps
isolate away aggregate factors by relying only on cross-sectional com-
parisons of banks that have different exposure to rising inflation, it
highlights a second identification challenge that we face, which is that
highly inflation-exposed banks might be systematically different from
other banks in a way that is correlated with their subsequent lending.

To overcome these challenges, we develop a novel identification
strategy based on state-level reserve requirements to analyze banks in
the U.S. in early 1977, when the inflation rate saw a one-time increase
from 5% to 7%, after which it plateaued for the subsequent year.
Although this increase in inflation in early 1977 was not as large as
separate and more prominent inflation increases in 1973–1974 and
1978–1981, this setting has a variety of unique identification advan-
tages, discussed below, helping to isolate rising inflation from other
factors that can affect bank lending.

Using balance-sheet data from individual banks, we construct an
inflation exposure measure of each bank’s asset-liability inflation

mismatch, which captures the sensitivity of the real value of bank equity
to unexpected shocks to future inflation expectations. To generate
plausibly exogenous variation in inflation exposure in the cross-section
of banks, we exploit across-state differences in reserve requirements for
Federal Reserve (Fed) nonmember banks, along with within-state dif-
ferences between Fed nonmember versus member banks. As reserve
requirements primarily mandate a minimum cash-to-demand-deposit
ratio, we show that higher reserve requirements substantially increase
banks’ inflation exposures, since the real value of non-interest-bearing
cash is eroded when inflation unexpectedly rises.

Specifically, our empirical approach adopts Duflo’s (2001) instru-
mental variables (IV) framework with a differential treatment effect to
analyze the effect of the early-1977 inflation increase. In this IV
framework, as Fed nonmember banks are all state-chartered and have
reserve requirements that differ by states, nonmember banks receive a
“treatment” (according to state-level reserve requirements) that differs
in intensity across states, which in turn affects their inflation exposures.
In contrast, member banks, whether nationally or state-chartered, have
uniform reserve requirements across all states (i.e., no variation across
states) and thus serve as a “nontreated” control group. Intuitively, we
think of the variation in inflation exposure as mainly driven by the
across-state differences for nonmember banks due to differences in
state-level reserve requirements; however, by also effectively differ-
encing out nonmember versus member banks within the same state, our
identification helps ensure that our results are not driven by different
macroeconomic conditions or other differences between states.

A formal first-stage analysis shows that nonmember banks with
higher state-level reserve requirements have higher inflation exposures
in December 1976 (i.e., prior to the 1977 inflation increase), as
measured by the bank-level balance-sheet measure of inflation asset-
liability mismatch. In contrast, for member banks (the nontreated con-
trol group), there is no effect on inflation exposure. In the second stage,
we find that banks with the most positive inflation exposures (as
instrumented by higher state-level reserve requirements) reduce lending
the most after the inflation increase. The estimated aggregate effect is
that U.S. loan growth is reduced by 3.2 percentage points, relative to
average loan growth of 19% in 1977.

We show that our results also hold more generally in an ordinary
least squares (OLS) setting of all U.S. commercial banks from 1976 to
2019. However, our study mainly focuses on the early-1977 episode,
which has several identification advantages. First, unlike other episodes
of accelerating inflation, early 1977 experienced a one-time jump in the
inflation rate—from 5% to 7%—where inflation subsequently remained
for a year, and, importantly for us, the jump was immediately recog-
nized by markets and priced one-for-one into higher nominal short-term
rates (but with minimal change in ex post real short-term rates). Second,
this burst of inflation is generally attributed to a single primary cause: an
increase in energy prices early in the year due to an abnormally cold
winter, which quickly filtered into broadly higher prices. Third, there
were no large currency changes, long-term interest rate movements, or
expectations of changes in monetary policy.3 In contrast, the more
prominent 1973–1974 and 1978–1981 inflation episodes coincided with
several confounding factors, including substantial changes in monetary
policy and large changes in the dollar exchange rate. Fourth, there are
data advantages: a) The Call Reports data is only available electronically
starting in 1976, precluding any analysis of previous inflation episodes;
b) The Monetary Control Act abolished state-level reserve requirements
in 1980, precluding our identification strategy after 1980; and c) State-
level reserve requirements are comprehensively reported only for 1976
(by Gilbert and Lovati, 1978).

We then show that highly inflation-exposed banks primarily reduce
lending to households (which is primarily mortgage lending), and we
use state-level aggregate data to show that states with high-inflation-
exposed banks see reductions in house price growth and construction
employment. We do not see any state-level effects on other types of
employment. These results suggest that the credit supply decrease from
inflation is transmitted to the macroeconomy in large part through a
housing effect. Our results are consistent with a large body of literature
demonstrating that banking channels tend to strongly affect construc-
tion employment and housing (e.g., Bernanke and Gertler, 1995).

We explore three specific banking channels through which higher
inflation might affect bank lending: a bank “net wealth” channel, a “loan
misallocation” channel, and a “deposit outflows” channel. Consistent
with a bank net wealth channel, in which impaired equity or earnings
lead banks to reduce loan growth, we show that more inflation-exposed
banks earn lower net interest margins, as higher inflation forces these
banks to pay higher interest expenses on interest-bearing liabilities
without receiving similarly higher interest income. The estimated
decrease in return-on-equity for the average bank is 2.5 percentage
points, compared to an average return-on-equity of 10.5%.

We also find evidence consistent with a loan misallocation channel,
in which higher expected inflation volatility prompts the most inflation-
exposed banks to shorten the maturity of their loan portfolio and real-
locate their assets away from long-term nominal loans and toward
inflation-protected assets. Furthermore, we see that inflation-exposed
banks primarily reduce loans to households (which are primarily long-
term nominal mortgage loans), rather than commercial and industrial
(C&I) loans (which are primarily short-term loans)—consistent with this
channel. This finding helps explain how the real effects we find are
concentrated in construction employment and house prices, rather than
in other sectors.

We next investigate the deposit outflows channel, in which rising
inflation can lead to an aggregate outflow of deposits due to regulatory
ceilings on time- and savings-deposit rates, as savers take their funds
outside the banking system to market-rate investments that earn higher
nominal rates. Drechsler (2020) argue the importance of this channel in
explaining accelerating inflation in 1978–1981. However, we find little
evidence for this channel in 1977—in particular, we see little change in
net inflows of either demand deposits or time and savings deposits, be it
in the time series or cross-section—and we show that deposit rate ceil-
ings did not begin to substantially bind until early 1978.

Our paper complements a growing body of literature exploring the
linkages among inflation dynamics, the pricing of financial instruments,
and corporate financing (e.g., Kang and Pflueger, 2015; Rey, 2016; Du,
2020). Section 2 presents our theoretical framework, Section 3 our data,
Section 4 our international findings, and Section 5 our main results.
Section 6 concludes.

Before presenting the model, it is important to understand repricing
of bank asset and liability items with rising inflation. In the U.S. today,
where the Fed has tight control over short-term interest rates, a rise in
interest rates is often due to an increase in the Federal Funds target rate
as a deliberate response to inflation. However, even in the absence of
monetary policy actions, nominal yields on long-term securities and
long-term lending rates can rise endogenously with higher expected
inflation, affecting bank balance sheets and future lending decisions.
Thus, we are not simply arguing that the lending contraction occurs
through standard monetary policy channels due to a deliberate tight-
ening of monetary policy. In many settings, even short-term nominal
rates can rise without monetary policy action for two reasons. First,
many central banks have, or historical had, primary operational targets
other than interest rates, targeting mainly monetary aggregates (e.g.,
bank reserves or M1) or exchange rates (e.g., pegs), leaving interest rates
to float freely (Bindseil, 2014). In the U.S. 1977 setting, the Fed did not
publicly target a constant Federal Funds rate, as they do today but
instead had a variety of targets for monetary aggregates like M1.4 As a
result, the rise in short-term interest rates in early 1977 was endogenous,
one-for-one with inflation, without any change in the Federal Open
Market Committee’s (FOMC) monetary policy stance (Romer and Romer
1989). Second, in many emerging markets, central banks only have
weak control over broader short-term interest rates, due to underde-
velopment of the financial system (e.g., lack of liquidity in interbank
markets, lack of high-quality securities to purchase). As a result, central
banks often struggle with interest rate control in inflationary settings,
resulting in volatile short-term rates (Van ’t dack, 1999).

Our model features an optimizing, risk-averse bank that operates in
competitive markets for long-term lending and market-rate short-term
borrowing. The bank’s objective is to maximize the expected net present
value of future earnings, while avoiding violating a reserve requirement
that mandates that the bank must keep at minimum fraction ϕ of its
assets in non-interest-bearing cash; otherwise, the bank incurs a regu-
latory penalty ψ proportional to assets. The bank makes long-term fixed-
rate loans, which are funded by demand deposits and by market-rate
short-term funding, and makes optimal choices for the following vari-
ables: the quantity of loans L, loan maturity M, real lending rate i,
fraction of inflation-indexed loans γ, and non-interest-bearing cash
holdings c.5 The bank faces both a demand curve for new loans (i.e.,
borrowers will demand more loans at lower real lending rates i) and a
supply curve for market-rate short-term funding (i.e., money markets
will supply more funds at higher real funding rates r).

In this section, we provide an overview of a theoretical model, which
we present in full in Appendix Section A and which illustrates how a
higher expected level of inflation or higher expected inflation volatility
can affect bank lending. The model is based on the canonical work of
Gray (1978), who studies optimal labor contracts in the presence of
volatile inflation, which we adapt to the case of the banking market.
Although our work builds on three well-established banking mecha-
nisms—“net wealth,” “loan misallocation,” and “deposit outflows”
channels—our contribution is not to present these as new channels, as
all these mechanisms work through inflation’s impact on nominal in-
terest rates and banks’ nominal interest rate exposures. Rather, the goals
of the model are threefold. First, the model theoretically delineates these
three channels and makes testable predictions for each in the case of the
1977 inflation increase. Second, it gives theoretical justification for our

We can then characterize the optimal lending, indexing, and matu-

We start with the optimal fraction of indexed loans, γ. The optimal γ
reflects a trade-off between the transaction costs of indexation and the
expected risk of bank losses generated by inflation, which could lead the
bank to violate its reserve requirement; it is increasing in inflation
volatility σ and decreasing in the indexation cost π and reserve
requirement ϕ. The first key result from our model, consistent with
Gray’s (1978) model, is that γ is either zero or one: Banks only index
their loans when the expected volatility of inflation exceeds a given
cutoff. This first result helps explain why inflation-indexed loans are
only offered in countries with very high and volatile inflation rate-
s—such as Brazil, Chile, Israel, and Iceland in the 1970s—but not
generally elsewhere.6

We next discuss the optimal choice of loan maturity M, given γ = 0.7
Our model predicts that, despite the high exposure of long-term fixed
rate loans to potential losses from future inflation increases, banks will
make long-term fixed-rate loans with staggered maturities in equilib-
rium, even when they expect volatile inflation. The reason is that banks,
in choosing the optimal loan maturity, must balance the trade-off be-
tween minimizing their exposure to inflation (which favors short con-
tract duration) with the transaction cost C of rolling over loans more
frequently (which favors long contract duration).8

Given the presence of long-term fixed rate loans, banks lose value
when inflation increases. This is because market rate short-term funding
instantly becomes nominally more expensive with higher inflation, but
long-term fixed rate loans can be refinanced only after they are due with
maturity M. Given the leverage constraint, the reduction in bank value
forces the bank to cut lending. (See Equation (A.16).) This is the net
wealth channel. The empirical prediction from this channel is that the
most inflation-exposed banks will see a fall in net interest margins and a
reduction in loan quantity. The model helps us understand why bank
losses are unavoidable in equilibrium even when banks expect high
inflation volatility: The transaction costs of rolling over loans or insti-
tutional factors favoring long-term loans make it suboptimal for banks to
completely insulate their balance sheets from future inflation increases.
A comparative statics exercise, in which we vary the expected
volatility of inflation, highlights our second channel, the loan misallo-
cation channel. Comparing the previous equilibrium to a new equilib-
rium with higher expected inflation volatility σ, we see that banks
optimally shorten the maturity of their portfolios and reduce their long-
term nominal lending (see Equations (A.11) and (A.14)) as they reba-
lance toward a new, shorter-maturity optimal asset portfolio. For the U.
S. in 1977, due to institutional arrangements in the mortgage market,
this channel entails a shift away from residential mortgages (which tend
to be long-term fixed-rate loans) and toward short-term securities and
C&I loans (which have on average short maturities in the U.S.). The

2.2. Why do different channels tend to dominate in different institutional
settings?

The model makes predictions about which channels dominate, pri-
marily based on three characteristics: expected inflation volatility,
changes in the level of expected inflation, and regulation (especially
reserve requirements and ceilings on deposit rates). If expected inflation
volatility is low or moderate, then banks will be relatively unhedged to
future inflation, as the cost of indexation outweighs the benefits. Thus,
in this scenario, the strength of the net wealth channel will vary based on
the shock to the level of expected inflation, while the loan misallocation
channel will vary based on the shock to expectations of inflation vola-
tility. If, additionally, there are ceilings on deposit rates, then the deposit
outflows channel can prevail, but only if market interest rates move
substantially above the rate ceilings.

We conjecture that the scenario above generally describes the setting
in the U.S. in 1976, when expected inflation volatility was moderate. We
defer a more detailed discussion to Section 5.6, after we present the
institutional context in greater detail. As a preview, the predicted
importance of the net wealth channel and loan misallocation channel
crucially depends on whether inflation expectations were increasing or
decreasing prior to 1977—and the historical record, we argue, presents
conflicting evidence. We thus leave it as an empirical question to what
extent these two channels are present in the data, noting that these
channels make specific empirical predictions—notably, the loan misal-
location channel predicting the shortening of average loan maturity and
a reduction in a bank’s inflation exposure—which we investigate in
Section 5.6.

3.2. Constructing the bank-level inflation exposure measure

Based on the theory in Section 2, our bank-level inflation-exposure
measure is designed to capture both the net wealth and loan misallo-
cation channels. Specifically, we construct an inflation exposure mea-
sure of each bank’s asset-liability inflation mismatch, which measures
the theoretical sensitivity of the real value of bank equity to an unex-
pected shock to future inflation expectations. For the loan misallocation
channel, this measure likewise captures the sensitivity of bank equity to
expected inflation volatility and, thus, the incentives for the bank to
rebalance toward a new optimal portfolio in response to a shift in
inflation volatility expectations. (For the deposit outflows channel,
however, we must look directly at changes in demand and time
deposits.)

In parallel, we also construct a “real rate exposure” measure, which
we will use in Section 5.5 to differentiate between banks’ inflation
exposure and their real interest rate exposure. Both the inflation expo-
sure and real rate exposure measures are based on the theoretical
response of real bank value to an increase in nominal interest rates,
which by Fisher’s Law can be decomposed into an increase in the
“inflation component” or an increase in the “real rate.” While the
inflation exposure measure is designed to capture the theoretical
response to bank value due to a rise in the inflation component (holding
the real rate constant), the real rate exposure measure is designed to
capture the theoretical expected response to bank value due to a rise in
the real rate (holding the inflation component constant).

We start by explaining how to construct the inflation exposure
measure, after which we will address the real rate exposure measure.
The inflation exposure measure is constructed by first constructing
separate asset-based and liability-based inflation exposure measures for
each bank and then combining them into a final inflation exposure
measure. To construct the asset-based exposure, we assign a value +1 to
a balance sheet item on the asset side if its value is “inflation exposed” (i.
e., real bank value decreases with a positive shock to future inflation
expectations), 0 if it is “inflation neutral” (i.e., real bank value stays
approximately the same with such a shock), and –1 if it is “inflation
protected” (i.e., real bank value increases with such a shock). Intuitively,
if a bank holds a high proportion of long-duration nominal bonds as
assets, it should be hurt by inflation; hence, long-duration nominal
bonds get a value of +1. In contrast, the real value of short-term bonds or
real assets is relatively inflation neutral and assigned a value of 0.9

In practice, we use the following algorithm to operationalize this
framework: We categorize asset items as inflation neutral (0) if any of
the following criteria are met: 1) The asset is interest-bearing at market
rates and the average maturity is less than two years; 2) The asset is
inflation-indexed or foreign-currency denominated, or consists of equity
or real assets, such as real estate or property; or 3) The asset is an
intangible or other asset (under the assumption that intangibles benefit
the real future cash flows of the bank; even if miscategorized, this third
category is small). All other assets are labeled “inflation exposed” (+1).
Column 1 of Table A5 shows how each asset class is categorized, and
Appendix Section C explains each individual asset class categorization in
detail using the criteria above. Note that C&I loans are classified as
“inflation neutral” for the U.S., because they are on average short-term
loans (with an average maturity of 1.4 years), whereas loans to house-
holds are “inflation exposed,” being mainly long-term fixed-rate mort-
gages. Then, for each bank, we calculate the asset-based measure as a
weighted average (as a proportion of total assets of each balance sheet
item) of the +1’s and 0’s. Hence, the more positive the value of this
measure is (closer to +1), the more the bank should be harmed by rising
inflation.

Similarly, we create a measure of inflation exposure for the non-
equity liabilities on the balance sheet. Items for which real bank value
stays approximately the same with a positive shock to future inflation
expectations are considered inflation neutral and assigned a value of 0.
Such items include short-term money market funding and other market-
rate short-term liabilities (since cash flows keep up with inflation). In
contrast, if rising inflation decreases the real value of a liability item, it is
labeled as inflation protected (since it increases real bank value) and
assigned a value of –1, because eroding the real value of the liability
helps real bank value. For example, since demand deposits are non-
interest-bearing across all our settings, banks benefit from these liabil-
ity items when inflation increases, since the costs of inflation are passed
through to depositors; we thus assign a value of –1 to demand deposits
and current accounts.

As with assets, we use the following algorithm to operationalize this
framework: We categorize liability items as “inflation neutral” (0)—and
“inflation protected” (–1) otherwise—if either of the following criteria is
met: 1) The liability is interest-bearing and the average maturity is less
than two years, or 2) the liability is inflation-indexed, foreign-currency
denominated, or a trading liability. Again, see Table A5 and Appendix
Section C for further details on each liability classification. Then, for
each bank, we calculate the liability-based inflation exposure of the
bank as a weighted average (as a proportion of total liabilities) of the 0’s
and –1’s.

Finally, we calculate a total inflation exposure measure for each bank
by averaging the asset and liability exposure measures. This total mea-
sure nets the total inflation exposure to bank equity holders from the
asset and liability sides and captures the bank’s mismatch to inflation: A
positive value implies that a positive shock to expected future inflation
decreases real bank value, while a negative value implies that it in-
creases real bank value. This total inflation exposure measure is the
default measure used throughout the paper. According to Table 1, this
measure (Row 11) has a mean value across banks of 0.097 (s.d. = 0.091,
i.q.r. 0.042 to 0.151) for the U.S. in 1976. Roughly 10% of banks have a
negative value, implying that these banks may benefit from rising
inflation.

3.3. Inflation exposure versus real rate exposure

For the robustness analysis in Section 5.5, which differentiates be-
tween banks’ inflation exposure and real interest rate exposure, we
additionally construct a real interest rate exposure measure for each
bank. The real rate exposure measure is constructed analogously to the
inflation exposure measure: Each asset and liability item is labeled +1,

JournalofFinancialEconomics160(2024)1039026I. Agarwal and M. Baron

0, or –1, if real bank value due to this item theoretically decreases, re-
mains the same, or increases, respectively, with a positive shock to real
interest rates, assuming the inflation component of nominal rates is held
constant. Then, as before, we take a weighted average of all assets and
all liabilities items to generate an overall real rate exposure measure for
each bank.

Importantly, the inflation exposure and the real rate exposure are not
the same, as certain balance sheet items may be inflation exposed but
not real interest rate exposed, or vice versa. For example, while non-
interest-bearing cash is inflation exposed (assigned +1), since higher
inflation decreases its real value, it is real rate neutral (assigned 0), since
the real value of non-interest-bearing cash is unaffected by a change in
real rates (assuming no change in inflation). Similarly, while non-
interest-bearing demand deposits lead real bank value to be inflation
protected (assigned –1), since higher inflation decreases the bank’s real
liability, they are real rate neutral by the same reasoning as above. Of
course, for many balance sheet items, such as long-term bonds, inflation
exposure and real rate exposure are the same. Column 2 of Table A5
shows how each asset and liability item is categorized, in terms of its real
rate exposure.10

We thus calculate both the inflation exposure and the real rate
exposure for all banks in each year. The correlation between the infla-
tion exposure and the real rate exposure of all banks in our sample in
December 1976 is approximately 70%. We will make use of this partial
correlation in Section 5.5 to differentiate the effects of inflation in-
creases and real rate increases.

5.2. Identification

A key challenge in identifying the effect of inflation on bank lending
is that the cross-sectional variation in inflation exposure across banks
might be correlated with other factors that affect bank credit. Therefore,
we adopt an IV estimation strategy and use the interaction between state
reserve requirements and Fed membership status as an instrument for
inflation exposure of banks in different states.22 We conjecture that
reserve requirements have large effects on banks’ non-interest-bearing
cash holdings relative to demand deposits. As cash is a non-interest-
bearing nominal asset, banks with higher cash holdings (relative to
deposits) should be more negatively affected by unexpectedly higher
inflation.23

In the U.S. during this period, reserve requirements mandated a
minimum ratio of non-interest-bearing cash to demand deposits, so
there are two reasons why a higher cash-to-demand deposit ratio leads
to higher bank inflation exposure. To satisfy the reserve requirement, a
bank may hold more non-interest-bearing cash (the numerator), which
negatively affects inflation exposure, because banks lose real value with
non-interest-bearing cash. Alternatively, to satisfy reserve requirement
ratios, a bank may fund itself through fewer demand deposits (the de-
nominator); as demand deposits in 1976 are non-interest-bearing, fewer
demand deposits would increase a bank’s inflation exposure. Thus, both
the numerator and denominator work in the same direction to increase
the bank’s inflation exposure. There are important nuanced differences
in reserve requirements across states (e.g., which types of assets count
toward reserves, which types of deposits are exempt, and whether sav-
ings and time deposits have different reserve requirements), which we
factor into our IV strategy, as explained in the next subsection.

Fed nonmember banks must satisfy the reserve requirement of their
respective states, while Fed member banks have uniform, nationally
imposed reserve requirements across all states. Therefore, state-level
variation in reserve requirements should differentially affect the infla-
tion exposure of Fed nonmember banks, but not Fed member banks,
across states. Our estimation strategy following Duflo’s (2001) IV
framework thus exploits both across-state variation in inflation exposure
of nonmember banks and within-state variation in exposure of member
and nonmember banks.24

5.3. First-stage regression results

As described in Section 3, we use balance sheet items from the Call
Reports in 1976 to build an inflation-exposure measure for each bank,
where a positive value implies that an increase in inflation hurts bank

show in the second stage that higher fitted values of inflation exposure
predict lower subsequent lending after the inflation increase, which, in

Regional variation in inflation. We test whether increases in state-level
inflation (using state-level data from Hazell et al. (2022) available for
the period 1978–2019) versus national inflation lead inflation-exposed
banks to decrease lending. Theoretically,
is unclear whether
state-level inflation should matter. For example, the repricing of many
balance sheet items, especially exchange-traded securities or loans and
deposits sold nationally, might be based on national inflation, since they
are traded or priced in a national market. On the other hand, small banks
might price their (locally sold) loans and deposits based on local infla-
tion. Table A10 finds that state-level inflation generally drives out the
significance of national inflation. We conclude that local variation in
inflation best explains the average lending response of banks to
inflation.

Rising inflation versus disinflation. We test whether disinflation affects
bank lending differently from rising inflation. Table A11 shows that the
results are symmetric: while rising inflation lead the most inflation
exposed banks to decrease their lending, disinflation does the reverse,
inducing the most inflation exposed banks to increase their lending.

Which channels are expected to dominate in the U.S. setting in
1976–1977, when expected inflation volatility was moderate? As argued
in Section 2, the presence of the net wealth versus misallocation chan-
nels depends on whether the inflation shock is a level increase in ex-
pectations or signals a shift in expected inflation volatility. Potentially,
both of these effects might be jointly present.

In the U.S. in 1976–1977, were banks expecting the high inflation
volatility of the recent past to persist? Or, as argued by Romer and
Romer (1989) and Cochrane (2011), were banks (incorrectly) revising
their inflation volatility expectations downward after the rapid fall in
the rate of inflation in 1974–1975, and thus the inflation jump in early
1977 came as a shock to expectations? The historical record presents
conflicting evidence. On the one hand, pointing toward high expected
inflation volatility, inflation had previously been high and volatile over
the past 10 years, with sharp and salient rises in 1968–1971 and
1973–1974. Additionally, one can cite worried discussion in 1975–1976
among academics, bankers, and policymakers about the effects of

The deposit outflows channel. The deposit outflows channel, in which
savings and time depositors shift funds from non-market-rate deposits to
market-rate securities outside the banking system, first came to promi-

market interest rates exceeded the ceilings imposed at the time on sav-

strong evidence of this channel in explaining the rise of inflation in

very important in 1966, 1968, 1973–1974, and 1978–1981 (Koch,
2015). However, we find little evidence for the importance of this
channel in 1977.

Our work focuses on demonstrating a causal effect on bank lending in
the U.S. setting. One limitation of our study is that we cannot directly
assess the magnitudes of particular channels in international inflation
episodes, due to lack of detailed data. In order to stress-test the banks in
countries other than the U.S. to changes in inflation, one would need
more detailed regulatory data of bank balance sheet exposure—such as
data differentiating between indexed and non-indexed, short-term and
long-term, and/or local- and foreign-currency-denominated loans and
securities, as well as on holdings of specific asset classes such as inter-
bank deposits and real estate. Our paper shows how regulators who have
such data might use it to better understand the inflation exposure of
banks.

6. Conclusions

Our paper demonstrates that an unexpected increase in inflation
tends to be contractionary for the banking sector, an effect that is, in
turn, transmitted to the macroeconomy. By analyzing an inflation in-
crease in the U.S. in early 1977, we show how inflation-exposed banks
reduce their lending, which in turn primarily affects house prices and
construction employment. Understanding the consequences of higher
inflation for the financial system is important, especially now for the U.
S. as it emerges from the COVID crisis, but also for emerging economies,
where inflation is a recurring problem. Our work suggests that even a
moderate increase in inflation might induce a lending contraction due to
both the net wealth channel and the loan misallocation channel.
