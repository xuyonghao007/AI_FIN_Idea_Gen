A B S T R A C T

This paper studies banks’ investment in risk management human capital following the Global Financial Crisis and 
the advent of stress testing. Our results suggest that ‘Too Big to Fail’ distortions may have weakened large banks’ 
incentive to invest in risk management talent. Stress testing, which focuses on the largest banks, spurred demand 
for skilled quantitative risk managers, but only narrowly in anticipation of a test and following poor performance 
on a test. Stress testing does not affect demand for the over 90 % of risk management jobs not linked to passing 
tests, limiting its effectiveness in improving risk management practices.

1. Introduction

This  paper  analyzes  banks’  demand  for  risk  management  (RM) 
human  capital  using  data  from  online  advertisements  for  hiring.1
Bearing and managing risk lie at the core of bank value added, but little 
is known about how banks decide whom to hire or the skills embedded 
in those hired. Existing research has focused on the role of senior officers 
in  risk  management  (e.g.,  Fahlenbrach  and  Stulz,  2011),  on  board 
members and structures (e.g., Ellul and Yerramilli, 2013; Minton et al., 
2014),  or  on  internal  risk  culture  (Fahlenbrach,  et  al.,  2012).  While 
management, governance and culture are all important – top executives 
and board members set the priorities and incentives within an organi-
zation – these few individuals cannot singlehandedly govern bank risk 
without the support of low- and mid-level employees.

We  document  a  broad-based  rise  in  demand  for  risk  management 
(RM)  expertise  during  the  years  following  the  Global  Financial  Crisis 
(GFC). The largest banks, however, demand less RM skills than smaller 
banks  (controlling  for  time  trends, exposure  to  stress  tests,  and  other 
variables).  Unlike  smaller  banks,  which  demand  more  RM  skills  in 
response to past risks, large banks show only a weak association between 
past risk exposure and demand for RM skills. These patterns are broadly 
consistent  with  “Too  Big  to  Fail”  (TBTF)  expectations  of  government 

bailouts, which weaken large banks’ incentive to manage risk more than 
small ones. Given these findings, we next examine whether regulatory 
pressure from exposure to stress tests helps substitute for large banks’ 
potentially  weaker  incentives  for  RM.  Demand  to  hire  highly  skilled 
stress-test labor does increase in anticipation of a test and following poor 
performance on a test, but most of this hiring appears aimed narrowly at 
passing  stress  tests  rather  than  broadly  at  improving  bank  risk  man-
agement practice.

We use data from Lightcast, which reports comprehensive informa-
tion for online advertisements by firms looking to hire at all levels across 
the organization. These data, we argue, capture labor demand, as they 
reflect the kinds of skills banks are seeking to employ. As such, they help 
us understand what banks are trying to achieve in their human resources 
policies,  as  opposed  to  whom  they  succeed  in  hiring  (which  would 
capture both labor supply and demand effects).2

Using Lightcast, we document that total demand for jobs that require 
any RM skills climbed more than six-fold from 26,000 job posts by banks 
in 2010 to over 167,000 posts by 2021 (Fig 1). The data clearly show 
that risk management has been a persistent and increasingly important 
factor at banks in the years following the GFC. The detailed textual job 
descriptions in the Lightcast data reveal, however, that most jobs labeled 
as “risk management” – which we define as those requiring quantitative

ture  only  the  components  of  job  descriptions  that  actually  require

jects of interest in our empirical tests. This approach also allows us to 
subdivide risk-management related skills into five subcategories, which 
we  label:  Operations,  Technology,  Lending,  Compliance,  and  Capital 
Regulation. Our RM skill measures exhibit similar trends to the cruder 
RM jobs measure, but with a much lower level.

By  refining  the  Lightcast  data,  we  can  make  progress  in  under-
standing banks’ incentives to manage risk, based on the revealed pref-
erence  of  whom  they  seek  to  employ.  We  start  by  pointing  to  the 
incentives of bank owners, managers and their regulators, which suggest 
opposing incentives in some cases. Using this conceptual framework to 
guide the empirical specifications, we report descriptive results linking 
bank characteristics to risk-management job demands (after removing 
trends). These cross-sectional correlations suggest that the largest banks 
are less likely to demand RM skills. For example, in our simplest model, 
the  largest  banks  demand  30  %  fewer  risk-management  skills  than 
smaller banks as a fraction of their total hiring (relative to the overall 
average). Moreover, small banks respond to high levels of past perfor-
mance volatility (i.e., risk) by increasing their demand for RM, while the 
large banks do not. We find no relationship between volatility, as well as 
three market-based measures of risk, and RM demand at the large banks.
The second component  of our analysis  focuses on  how stress tests 
affect  RM  demand.  Stress  testing,  the  core  innovation  in  risk-based 
capital following the GFC, implicitly recognizes the importance of risk 
management  by  directly  tying  capital  to  quantitative,  model-based 
forecasts  of  bank  losses  during  steep  downturns.  Stress  testing  also 
emphasizes qualitative dimensions of bank risk management resources. 
Overall, we find that ever-stress-tested and never-tested banks exhibit 
similar  levels  and  trends  in  demand  for  risk-management  skills  as  a 
fraction of their total hiring. But the kinds of skills they demand differ. 
For  example,  the  smaller,  never-tested  banks  focus  mainly  on  skills 
related to lending and compliance (e.g., rules related to fraud or anti- 
money  laundering);  in  contrast,  the  trends  for  larger  banks’  in-
vestments  appear  mostly  in  skills  related  to  technology  and  capital 
regulation.  Our  regressions  suggest  that  this  emphasis  is  spurred  by 
exposure  to  stress  tests,  as  well  as  from  ex-post  failure  of  the  tests; 
specifically, these two subcategories of RM skills respond most to stress 
testing, whereas skills related to lending and compliance barely do so if 
at all. We also find that risk realization (volatility) leads to more hiring 
demands for stress-test skills (unlike other RM skills).

Empirically, stress testing allows us to exploit within-bank variation 
in panel models since the set of banks subject to stress tests changes over 
time, and the effect of the test on banks also varies temporally. We show 
that banks that expect to be stress-tested in the next cycle increase their 
demand for stress-test-specific RM labor. This effect is greatest at banks 
that fail the stress tests. That said, the effects we document likely un-
derstate the overall impact of regulatory tightening on demand for risk 
management. Many dimensions of regulatory change happened to all U. 
S. banks, making it hard to pin down their causal effects empirically. We 
remove both time-invariant bank characteristics as well as time trends in 
our analysis of stress tests to address identification concerns, but this 
approach  may  also  remove  important  common  effects  of  regulatory 
change on skill acquisition. Even within the panel setting, we argue that 
the measured effect of stress test outcomes on skill demand is likely to 
understate  the  true  effect  because  banks  with  a  greater  level  of  risk 
management expertise ought to perform better on their stress tests; in 
other words, any reverse causation from a bank’s hiring of risk  man-
agement talent for stress-test jobs and test outcomes would attenuate our 
core finding.

Do stress tests improve overall risk management practices at banks? 
Here, the evidence is at best mixed. More than half of the skill demands 
related  to  stress  test  jobs  pertain  to  capital  regulation,  compared  to 
about 15 % for other RM jobs. Skills related to technology, such as data 
warehousing, data governance and software development, are also over- 
weighted  for  stress  test  jobs  compared  to  other  RM  jobs.  Stress  tests 
require substantial investment in data and software because banks must 
build internal models and provide detailed, granular data as part of the 
supervisory process.3 These two subcategories – capital regulation and 
technology – also respond most to stress tests in our regressions. Stress- 
test jobs, however, comprise <10 % of all RM skill acquisition by these 
banks, and we find no impact on the other 90 % of their RM skill de-
mand, either from exposure to or results on these tests. Bank RM demand 
stimulated by stress testing thus may largely be driven by their need to 
pass regulatory hurdles; evidence that stress tests affect overall RM de-
mands is weak.

Our paper provides a new way to test how expectations of govern-
ment  support  (“bailouts”)  affect  bank  risk-taking.  Risk  incentives  are 
especially strong for the largest banks because both their managers and 
their creditors understand that government interventions become more 
likely with greater size and complexity (see Strahan, 2013, for a review).

Most of the existing evidence on the so-called TBTF problem focuses on 
connections  between  size  and  interconnectedness  with  risk  outcomes 
(loan losses or stock-return volatility), leverage (capital ratios), or the 
cost of borrowing in uninsured markets (subordinated debt). Our anal-
ysis  is  novel  because  we  directly  measure  bank  investment  in  costly 
human capital designed to mitigate risk. If TBTF weakens incentives to 
make  these costly investments, then  large banks will be less  likely  to 
respond to higher risk than other banks.

Although the indeterminate nature of TBTF makes it challenging to 
fully rule out alternatives, our empirical tests support this interpretation. 
The key evidence is, first, that large banks (>$50 billion) have lower 
average demand for RM skills than smaller ones (controlling for trends 
and other factor like stress test exposure). Second, demand for RM skills 
is less responsive to measures of risk among the large banks. Specifically, 
the effect of ROA volatility on RM demand is differentially lower for the 
large banks; and demand for RM skills among large banks is not corre-
lated with market-based risk measures (these metrics are not available 
for the small banks). Third, the moral hazard effects on large-bank RM 
demand are only evident among the large banks: capital affects their RM 
demand positively while deposits/assets affects it negatively. As such, 
we  think our results  provide a  policy  rationale for  regulatory  mecha-
nisms to offset large-bank potential underinvestment in RM talent.

We also contribute to the literature on stress tests. In studying post- 
GFC regulatory changes, many researchers have focused on the impact 
of stress testing on credit supply (e.g., Covas, 2017; Acharya, Berger and 
Roman, 2018; Bassett and Berrospide, 2018; Cortes, et al., 2020; and 
Doerr 2021). There is also debate about the efficacy of stress tests as a 
means of regulating capital and how results should be disclosed. Hirtle, 
et al. (2009) argue that stress tests have become a key tool in banking 
supervision,  although  Frame,  et  al.  (2015) report  that  pre-GFC  stress 
testing was ineffective for the GSEs. Goldstein and Leitner (2018) model 
the tradeoffs faced by regulators regarding disclosure of stress test re-
sults. They focus on how disclosure of individual bank risks can affect 
inter-bank markets and risk sharing. But little is known about how (and 
whether)  stress  testing  reshapes  banks’  internal  risk  management 
practices.  Our  paper  fills  this  gap  by  showing  that  banks  respond  to 
stress testing by changing their investment in human capital, but all of 
this investment concentrates among a small fraction of employees hired 
for the purpose of stress test compliance.

We find that banks respond to stress testing in a limited way, but 
recent  changes  in  the  stress  testing  regime  may  further  limit  their 
response, as these changes reduce the severity and scope of the tests. The 
2018  reforms  to  the  Dodd-Frank  Act,  for  example,  gave  the  Federal 
Reserve discretion as to which banks with assets between $50 and $250 
billion  would  be  required  to  undergo  annual  supervisory  stress  tests 
(called the Comprehensive Capital Analysis and Review, or CCAR).4 As a 
result, banks such as Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) were not put through 
these tests even though they had grown above the $50 billion threshold 
by 2018.5 Whether or not this change led to SVB’s failure is unknowable,

2. Stress testing

We focus on supervisory stress testing, which began in the wake of 
the  GFC  in  2009  with  the  Supervisory  Capital  Assessment  Program 
(SCAP). This program succeeded in allaying concerns about bank health 
and led to rapid bank recapitalization. Following the success of SCAP, 
the Fed decided to implement annual supervisory stress tests for large 
banks and rebranded the program as the Comprehensive Capital Anal-
ysis and Review (CCAR). The CCAR process, until 2020, explicitly tied 
bank dividend and capital distributions to the stress test results. CCAR 
captures  each  bank’s  quantitative  exposure  to  its  adverse  economic 
scenarios (nine-quarter ahead potential paths for risk drivers) by con-
structing hypothetical nine-quarter ahead paths for several regulatory 
capital ratios (e.g., the Tier 1 capital ratio, the Total risk-based capital 
ratio, and the Tier 1 leverage ratio). Until 2020, the forecasted paths of 
the capital ratios embedded each bank’s planned dividend increases and 
share repurchases. If one of the capital ratios fell below the regulatory 
minimum during the forecast horizon, however, the bank was required 
to reduce its planned capital payouts. CCAR also provides a qualitative 
assessment of each bank’s overall risk management practices.6

In addition to CCAR, the Dodd-Frank Act requires the Fed to conduct 
a parallel set of annual stress tests, known as the Dodd-Frank Act Stress 
Tests  (DFAST).  These  began  in  2013  and  differ  from  CCAR  in  the 
assumed path for capital distributions. DFAST also requires banks to run 
(and  disclose)  their  quantitative  stress  test  results  using  their  own 
internally  developed  models.  Under  DFAST,  each  bank  is  required  to 
simulate its capital ratios using both the Fed’s scenario (common to all) 
and also using its own scenario. We focus on measures using the Fed’s 
scenario. From 2011 to 2018, all banks with consolidated assets above 
$50 billion were subject to CCAR and DFAST. Effective in 2019, how-
ever, the ‘Tailoring Rule’ was implemented, according to which, banks 
with less than $250 billion consolidated assets received less regulatory 
scrutiny. The Fed moved some banks to a biannual testing cycle, while 
others, including Silicon Valley Bank, were exempt from being tested 
entirely.7

During each year in our sample, the Fed first releases the results of 
DFAST in a document containing the results from the Fed’s proprietary 
model. About one week later, they release a second document with re-
sults  from  CCAR,  again  containing  quantitative  results  based  on  the 
Fed’s model, along with a discussion of the qualitative results of the test. 
Banks also release their results around the same time, using a common 
set of scenario assumptions dictated by the Fed.

data from CCAR and DFAST. First, we utilize all testing cycles from 2011 
to 2021 to construct indicators for whether a bank is tested in the current

In some cases, a bank’s capital plan under CCAR is outright rejected; in

tative portion of CCAR in 2020 and 2021.

Our third set of measures equal the difference between the stressed 
value for a given capital ratio (under the severely adverse scenario) from 
its value at the start of the test, based on each bank’s internal model (i.e., 
using DFAST data). These variables are available from 2013 to 2021 and 
measure the exposure of a bank’s portfolio to the test. We focus on the 
results from banks’ models under the Fed’s scenarios, instead of Fed’s 
model, because they represent banks’ own assessments of their exposure 
to the scenarios before the results under the Fed’s model are disclosed.

3. Building a measure of RM demand from job posting data

3.1. Data overview

To assess banks’ labor demand, we analyze granular job posting data 
provided by Lightcast (formerly known as Burning Glass Technologies). 
Lightcast collects information from online job advertisements via data 
scraping techniques. These data cover the near universe of online job 
postings continuously from 2010 through 2021.8 Each data entry con-
tains  a unique job posting, with information such as the name of the 
employer, location of the job, industry, required education/experience, 
and  occupation  classification.  One  distinctive  feature  of  the  Lightcast 
database is that it provides a detailed description of required skills listed 
in each job ad. We hand-match large banks from FR-Y9C data with job 
posting data from Lightcast through bank/employer name and location. 
We consider a bank for our sample if it ranks among the top 300 banks 
by total assets in any year during our sample period, 2010–2021.

To build our measure of RM demand, we start with all jobs listed in 
Lightcast  with  at  least  one  skill  that  falls  within  the  “Financial  Risk 
Management”  or  “Financial  Regulations”  categories  and  define  the 
variable  RM  Jobs  as  the  percentage  of  total  job  listings  with  this 
requirement  for  each  bank-year.  Previewing  our  discussion  of  the  re-
sults, we find that the sample mean of RM Jobs is 20.86 %, meaning that 
about one in five (=1/0.2086) bank job posts require some amount of 
RM knowledge. A quick perusal of the detailed job descriptions reveals, 
however, that many of these positions are low-level bank jobs such as 
tellers  or  branch  managers,  or  require  very  general  skills  such  as 
communication,  multi-tasking,  writing,  management,  presentation, 
sales, etc. Thus, the RM Jobs variable alone may not capture what we as 
researchers  or  banks  as  employers  consider  pertinent  to  risk 
management.

To more accurately measure the RM talent that banks demand, we 
apply machine learning techniques to analyze the skills listed in the RM

job posts. Specifically, we deploy a structural topic model to filter the 
RM  subset  of  jobs  to  isolate  those  that  require  skills  related  to  the 
measurement and management of risks from a quantitative or modeling 
point of view. We define the measure RM Skills as the fraction of job 
skills demanded by banks that the model assigns to a RM-related skill 
cluster. Again previewing the results, the sample average of RM Skills is 
6.64 %, meaning that under one-third (=0.0664/0.2086) of the skills 
listed in RM Jobs posts fit these criteria. Alternatively interpreted, the 
mean of RM Skills implies that under one-in-fifteen (=1/0.0664) bank 
job posts are hypothetically fully dedicated to RM, which is markedly 
fewer than the one-in-five statistic implied by the mean of RM Jobs. In 
our tests, we find that RM Skills correlate more meaningfully with our 
variables  of  interest.  Thus,  this  topic  modeling  approach,  which  we 
illustrate in Section 3.2 and analyze in Section 3.3, allows us to more 
finely measure the human capital that banks demand to manage risk.

3.2. Illustrative example

To  understand  our  procedure,  we  first  describe  a  detailed  toy 
example; we then use the example to explain how we build our actual 
dataset.

Lightcast cleans, processes, and tokenizes the textual data and maps 
the processed texts into their universe of job attributes to generate lists 
of  skills.  The  bold  text  in  Table  1,  Panel  1  highlights  phrases  that 
Lightcast may identify as requisite skills for one typical job. In addition 
to this job (Job #1), suppose that we have a collection of RM job posts 
across two banks, Bank A and Bank B, and suppose that each job post is 
represented by the corresponding list of skills in Table 1, Panel 2. We 
input this skill list into a probabilistic topic model. Topic modeling is a 
technique  in  language  processing  that  allows  researchers  to  discover 
hidden thematic structures within a collection of text documents. It is an 
application  of  unsupervised  machine  learning,  wherein  the  algorithm 
directly  identifies  topic  patterns  in  documents  without  requiring 
explicitly labeled examples of those topics. This approach allows us to 
analyze the distribution of risk management skills within banks while 
remaining agnostic regarding which specific skills correspond to various 
dimensions of risk management. In the parlance of topic modeling, our 
group of job skill lists is a corpus, each job’s list of skills is a document, 
and each skill is a word.

We employ  the algorithm package ‘stm’  by Roberts, et  al. (2019), 
which  implements  the  structural  topic  model  (STM).  The  STM  is  an 
advancement  on  the  correlated  topic  model  (CTM),  which  builds  on 
latent  Dirichlet  allocation  (LDA).  LDA  operates  on  the  principal 
assumption that each document is composed of a mixture of latent topics 
and each topic is characterized by a distribution of words (Blei, et al., 
2003).  The  algorithm  attempts  to  reverse-engineer  the  topics  based 
solely on the empirical distributions of words. This process begins by 
randomly  assigning  each  word  occurrence  to  a  topic.  The  algorithm 
proceeds by iteratively reassigning word occurrences to topics based on 
(i) the prevalence of other words assigned to the topic within the same 
document and (ii) the prevalence of the topic across all occurrences of 
the word within the corpus. Over many iterations, the algorithm refines 
the  initial  random  topic  assignments,  gradually  converging  toward  a 
stable representation of the corpus’s inherent topic structure. The CTM’s 
key  innovation  is  its  capacity  to  model  correlations  between  topics, 
enhancing  the  model’s  fit  (Blei  and  Lafferty,  2007).  That  is,  a  job 
requiring loan origination skills will also likely need knowledge of credit 
risk. The STM combines and extends three existing models: CTM, the 
Dirichlet-Multinomial  Regression  (DMR)  topic  model  and  the  Sparse 
Additive  Generative  (SAGE)  topic  model  to  create  a  semi-automated 
approach  to  modeling  topics,  which  can  also  incorporate  covariates 
and  metadata  in  the  analysis  of  text  (Roberts,  et  al.,  2019).  This 
approach is particularly useful for analyzing open-ended textual data.

We present the model-output topic structure for the toy example jobs 
in Table 1, Panel 3 as a matrix of word-topic associations.9 Each entry 
represents a log-odds ratio, with values closer to 0 indicating stronger 
associations between the word and the topic. For instance, the param-
eter value of (cid:0) 1000 for the association of ‘Basel’ with Topic 1 suggests 
that the word has virtually no relation to that topic, while the associa-
tion between ‘Basel’ and Topic 3 is relatively strong.

To decipher how model-identified topics map to real-world concepts, 
researchers often rely on FREX words, a portmanteau of ‘frequent’ and 
‘exclusive’ (Bischof and Airoldi, 2012). These words appear commonly 
within one topic but rarely in others, making them valuable for post-hoc 
interpretation of the model’s output. This is crucial since the unsuper-
vised nature of the algorithm prevents us from prescribing word-topic 
associations  ex-ante.  We  report  the  top  5  strongest  FREX  words  from

the example in Panel 4.10 From these words, we infer that Topic 1 per-
tains to Technology, Topic 2 to General Business skills, and Topic 3 to 
Capital Regulation.

For each job, the model also generates topic scores, which estimate 
the proportions of a job’s content that aligns with each topic. We present 
these scores in Panel 5 for the example jobs. In our analysis, we interpret 
each score as an expression of the fraction of a job’s skills related to each 
of the topics. For instance, Job #1 is overwhelmingly related to Topic 3 
(Capital Regulation) since its score for that topic is 87 %. This makes 
sense, as Job #1 requires skills related to “Basel,” “CCAR,” “Communi-
cation,”  “Derivatives,”  “Economics,”  “Risk  Management,”  “Stress 
Testing,” and “Teamwork.” Job #4, in contrast, leans toward Topic 1, as 
it  requires  skills  related  to  “Information  Security,”  “Information  Sys-
tems,”  “Presentations,”  “Risk  Management,”  “Software  Engineering,” 
“Troubleshooting,” and “Unix.”

To derive our bank-year level measures of RM demand, we first read 
through the FREX words (and also the most common words) to deter-
mine which topics identified by the algorithm map into relevant risk- 
management skills and tasks. This requires some judgment from us. In 
the toy example, we would define Topics 1 and 3 as relevant for risk 
management,  and  we  would  leave  Topic  2  out.  To  construct  risk- 
management  demand,  we  sum  the  topic  scores  across  the  RM  jobs 
within each bank, and express that as a fraction of all jobs listed by that 
bank  in  that  year.  For  example,  Bank  A  has  4  risk  management  jobs 
advertised,  so  the  total  gross  score  for  Topic  1  would  be  1.07 
(=0.04+0.12+0.10+0.81). If Bank A advertised for 8 jobs in total (so 
RM Jobs = 4/8 = 0.50), Topic 1 would receive a score of 1.07 × 0.50 =
0.134. The interpretation: 13.4 % of Bank A’s total labor demand comes 
from Topic 1, a sub-component of its total RM demand. We do the same 
for Topic 3, giving it a score of 0.20 (= (0.87+0.09+0.59+0.06) × 0.50). 
With these in hand, we define the overall RM Skills variable as the sum 
across each relevant topic, or 0.134+0.2 in this example. As is true in the 
overall  data,  RM  Skills  (0.334)  <  RM  Jobs  (0.50),  since  RM  Skills 
removes  skills/tasks  which  are  outside  the  risk  management  area. 
Overall, the topic modeling delivers a measure of total RM demand more 
precisely than the crude RM Jobs variable without imposing arbitrary 
structure on the data. It also allows us to decompose RM demand into 
intuitive sub-categories (Technology and Capital Regulation in the toy 
example).

Managers  exhibit  increases  across  all  five  sub-categories,  presumably 
because management jobs involve a wide array of different tasks. The 
increase  for  Analysts  seems  dominated  by  Technology-related  skills. 
This breakdown of the data suggests that our measures vary across time 
and across job titles in sensible ways.

downside risks, particularly when losses occur during periods of broad 
financial or economic stress. Deposit insurance lowers the costs of taking 
risks,  leading  to  standard  moral  hazard  problems.  In  addition,  the 
presence or expectation of government bailouts, particularly when they 
go beyond statutory limits (such as what we saw in March 2023) may 
further  undermine  risk  management  incentives.  In  particular,  broad 
bailouts encourage banks to ignore ‘tail risks’, as interventions are most 
likely  during  periods  of  large  losses  which  threaten  to  spread  conta-
giously or to harm non-financial parts of the economy. Such effects may 
be most salient for large, TBTF banks (because expectations of bailouts 
rationally increase with bank size) or at banks with low capital ratios 
(because shareholders at these banks have less “skin” in the game).

These distortions generate three predictions which we can take to the 
data: First, deposit financing will tend to weaken risk-management de-
mand. Deposits also may lower RM demand beyond moral hazard by 
lowering risk directly due to its stability, particularly during periods of 
financial market stress (Gatev and Strahan, 2006). Second, banks with 
more capital would have more ‘skin in the game’ and thus may be more 
motivated to manage risk. Third, the largest TBTF banks would likely 
have little incentive to do so, since the likelihood of an ex-post bailout is 
greatest  during large, systemic downturns. Relatedly, exposure to tail 
risk may reduce bank risk-management demand due to distortions from 
government guarantees. This effect, we argue, is one of the main moti-
vations  for  the  stress  test  regime,  which  focuses  specifically  on  deep 
losses and attempts to push banks to manage these risks.

While the TBTF problem distorts incentives, the largest banks do face 
greater  pressure  from  regulations  like  stress  tests  than  other  banks 
(Schneider, Strahan and Yang, 2023). Some aspects of stress testing aim 

to improve the overall risk management culture of the institution. Fed-
eral  Reserve  Regulation  YY,  for  example,  speaks  directly  to  internal 
governance over stress test models, requiring bank’s stress test model 
review at the Board of Directors level and requires them to “establish 
and  maintain  a  system  of  controls,  oversight,  and  documentation, 
including policies and procedures, that are designed to ensure that its 
stress testing processes are effective in meeting the requirements in this 
subpart.”13 Whether or not such rules spillover to broader kinds of risk 
management  investments,  which  is  the  Fed’s  intent,  is  an  important 
empirical question, as banks may manage RM just to pass stress tests 
while minimizing compliance costs. As such, in our later analysis, we 
subdivide RM investment into jobs specifically related to passing stress 
tests v. other risk management tasks.

Beyond  the  critical  tensions  between  bank  owners  and  regulators, 
differences  in  shareholder  composition,  managerial  incentives,  and 
corporate  governance  may  affect  banks’  appetite  for  RM.  We  expect 
banks  with  more  long-term  shareholders  to  be  more  attentive  to  risk 
management because long-term shareholders are more likely to expe-
rience negative realizations of risk – particularly rare tail events – over 
their holding periods than short-term shareholders.14 Additionally, we 
expect bank managers with more wealth tied to their banks’  value to 
demand more risk management through natural risk aversion, although 
this  may  be  tempered  by  option-based  pay  that  rewards  volatility.

In our first set of panel regressions, we examine the impact of stress 
tests on banks’ labor demand, including all banks which ever undergo 
the CCAR test. We hypothesize that banks scheduled to be tested in the 
following cycle expand their stress testing work force in anticipation of 
the test, and we compare this effect to non-stress test jobs. Specifically, 
we estimate the following regression: 

RMi,t=αt+ηi+βStress Testedi,t  + 1+Control Variables+εi,t,                 (2)

where Stress Testedi,t + 1 indicates that bank i is tested in year t + 1, and

bank total demand in stress-test jobs and other RM jobs, and we split this

RM Skills of stress-test jobs and other RM jobs. The largest banks were 
tested  across  all  cycles,  while  others  were  added  and  dropped.  This

limitation  below  by  exploiting  within-bank  variation  in  stress-test  re-

Table 9 reports similar models, splitting to focus on stress test jobs in

We next investigate how banks’ labor demand responds to past stress 
test performance (as opposed to expected upcoming tests), focusing now 
only on bank-years for which stress test performance data are disclosed. 
This  limits  our  sample  to  215  bank-years  from  2013  to  2021  (recall 
Appendix  Table  1).  We  hypothesize  that  banks  that  fail  tests  subse-
quently increase their demand for stress-test-related labor. As described 
above, we build a categorical variable, Failure Score, that takes values of 
0 (no objection or capital adjustments), 1 (conditional non-objection), 
and 2 (objection) depending on the Fed’s response to the bank’s capi-
tal plan under CCAR to capture the severity of a failure (Schneider, et al., 
2023).  Additionally,  we  consider  the  three  capital  ratios  consistently 
disclosed in all testing cycles: the Tier 1 Capital Ratio, the Total Risk-based 
Capital Ratio (TRBC), and the Tier 1 Leverage Ratio. We define a bank’s 
Exposure for each ratio as the difference between the ratio’s actual value 
at  the  beginning of the  test and  the  ratio’s projected  minimum value 
over  the  forecast  horizon  under  DFAST  (Cortes,  et  al.,  2020).  Thus, 
Exposure  measures  how  much  a  bank’s  capital  ratio  is  projected  to 
decline  during  the  test,  with  higher  values  corresponding  to  greater 
losses.

We formalize this analysis by regressing current period hiring on past 

stress test performance, as follows: 

RMi,t = β Failure Scorei,t(cid:0) 1 + λ Exposurei,t(cid:0) 1 + Controls + αt + ηi

+ εi,t

(3) 

As in Eq. (2), we start with the simple measure (Stress Test Jobs v. Other 
RM  Jobs),  then  report  parallel  models  with  RM  Skills  and  its 

subcategories. Controlling for the three measures of Exposure enables us 
to separate the effect of failing the test from the overall riskiness of the 
bank.33

Tables 10 and 11 present results from estimating Eq. (3) with jobs 
and skills as outcome variables, respectively. Columns 1 to 3 of Table 10
show that banks increase their demand significantly for stress test jobs 
following a stress test failure. The effect of failure on hiring for other RM 
jobs, reported in columns 4 to 6, is not statistically significant. Consis-
tent with Table 8, we again find statistically positive effects of failure on 
hiring for both highly educated and less educated stress test workers, 
although the relative magnitudes are similar for high v. low educated 
workers. And, also consistent with Table 8, we find no effect for either 
group among non-stress test jobs (columns 5 and 6).

Table 11 reports similar regressions, now focusing on RM Skills of 
stress test jobs in panel A and of other RM jobs in panel B. As in Table 9, 
the  overall  effect  of  failing  stress  tests  is  quantitatively  large,  but  its 
effects again only appear for three of the skill subcategories (operations, 
technology and capital regulation) and only for stress test jobs. And, the 
effects on skills acquisition related to capital regulation comprise >50 % 
of the total (=0.086 / 0.156).

We hope to draw causal inferences from the results in Tables 8–11. 
That is, we would like to argue that stress tests cause banks to alter their 
human  capital  investment,  not  the  reverse.  In  the  first  set  of  panel 
models (Eq. (2)), where we focus on just the effect of the (exogenous) 
stress  test  cycle,  this  interpretation  seems  easy  to  justify  because  the 
stress test cycle depends only on actions taken by the Federal Reserve, 
not the bank. Moreover, Fig. 7 suggests that banks only respond once 
they know that a test is imminent (i.e., no effect before 2013). In our 
second set of models, we focus on stress test outcomes, which naturally 
depend on bank decisions. Here, reverse causality could bias the effects 
we observe down because hiring fewer skilled employees for stress test 
compliance would likely raise the probability of failing the test. In fact, 
we  find  a  positive  effect  on  stress  test  hiring  on  the  odds  of  failure. 
Hence, the true causal effect is, if anything, likely to be more positive 
than our estimates would suggest.

Our  analysis  of  both  the  extensive  (Tables  8  and  9)  and  intensive 
(Tables 10 and  11) margins are  complementary. In both cases, banks 
respond to an upcoming future test or to poor performance on a past test 
by demanding skills which (plausibly) allow them to pass stress tests: 
they hire more workers with advanced degrees for stress tests jobs, and 
in jobs with skill demand that would be directly applicable to such tests. 
This is the upside of our results. Stress tests seem to spur banks to hire 
very  highly  educated  and  quantitative  people.  But  the  downside  is 
substantial. In both simple time series plots (recall Fig. 5) and also in 
more careful panel analysis, stress tests do not increase banks’  invest-
ment in risk management for jobs not specifically linked to passing the 
tests. This is worrying. Stress test skill demands comprise only 10 % of 
the total demand for RM skills, and <1 % of total bank hiring. The most 
parsimonious explanation is that banks hire aggressively to pass stress 
tests, but the tests themselves do not move the overall risk culture of the 
institution.

5. Conclusion

This paper provides new evidence on bank internal risk management 
practices since the Global Financial Crisis. We study the jobs and skills 
demanded  for  risk  management  by  banks  during  the  post-GFC  years. 
Detailed  textual  data  on  job  descriptions  and  skill  demands  from 
Lightcast allow us to capture the near universe of bank online job ad-
vertisements.  Using  natural  language  processing  technology,  we 
pinpoint exactly what skills banks demand, and how those demands vary

ment  in  risk  management  skills  increased  sharply  across  the  banking 
system,  consistent  with  a  strong  response  to  the  failures  during  the 
Global Financial Crisis. However, cross-sectional patterns suggest that 
the largest banks may underinvest in RM skills, consistent with expec-

post-GFC regulations such as stress testing helped alleviate this distor-
tion  by  spurring  banks  to  hire  more  RM  skills,  with  an  emphasis  on 
highly educated workers with advanced degrees. But this effect seems 
limited to banks’ efforts to pass those tests, as we find no spillovers to 
their investments in risk management for other jobs, which constitute 
around 90 % of their total RM skill demands.
