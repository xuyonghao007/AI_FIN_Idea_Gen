1. Introduction

We analyze interactions between fiscal and monetary stimulus in a new Keynesian model with nominal 
mortgage debt that can be inflated away. Redistributive transfers are most impactful when followed by a 
temporary deviation from inflation-targeting monetary policy. Unlike other fiscal policies, redistribution causes 
inflation even in the absence of long-run debt sustainability problems, and inflating away mortgages results 
in additional redistribution. In a quantitative model with mortgage refinancing frictions, transfer payments 
provide much more stimulus than the redistributive effects of inflation. Borrowers gradually refinance after 
their mortgages are inflated away, dampening their immediate consumption response and reducing their 
long-run labor supply.

The US government responded to the 2020 Covid crisis with its largest fiscal and monetary stimulus in history. The government’s response to 
the crisis involved large-scale redistribution through both transfer payments and social insurance programs resulting in deficits of $3.1 trillion in 
2020 and $2.7 trillion in 2021. During the post-Covid recovery from April 2020 up to the start of 2022, unemployment fell rapidly (from over 14% 
to 4%), house prices surged by 30%, and inflation reached a peak level over 9%. Despite this surge in inflation, the Federal Reserve temporarily 
avoided raising interest rates until March 2022, when inflation was already over 8%. This paper shows that the combination of redistribution and 
a monetary policy that temporarily deviates from targeting inflation provides powerful economic stimulus and causes a surge in inflation. This 
inflation results in additional redistribution from savers to borrowers, with a persistent economic impact lasting long after the intervention.

We first theoretically analyze the interaction between fiscal and monetary policy in a two-agent New Keynesian model with nominal debt 
contracts similar to Eggertsson and Krugman (2012). In the model, the consumption of patient savers depends on the present value of their future 
income, while the consumption of impatient borrowers depends only on their current income. Inflation erodes the real value of borrower debt, 
allowing them to consume more. We examine two forms of fiscal policy: redistribution from savers to borrowers, and changes in the stock of 
outstanding government debt that only savers hold. We analyze how the impact of these two fiscal interventions depends on the monetary policy 
rule that determines the response of interest rates. Motivated by the Federal Reserve’s delayed response to inflation in 2022, we examine shorm-term
deviations from the monetary policy rule as well as more standard changes in long-term policy rules. The impact of redistribution, unlike an increase 
in government debt, depends primarily on the short-term rather than the long-term monetary policy rule. 

The impact of changes in the stock of government debt is determined by the long-term policy regime in our model. Two policy regimes yield 
a determinate equilibrium. In the ‘‘monetary dominance regime’’ (Leeper, 1991), where taxes adjust to pay for government debt and monetary 
policy actively responds to inflation, there is no real effect of increasing government debt. This lack of real effect still holds if monetary policy 
temporarily deviates from an active policy rule. In the ‘‘fiscal dominance regime’’, where passive monetary policy allows inflation to pay for 
government spending instead of increased taxes, an increase in government debt causes a boom in output and inflation. This boom is similar to, 
but larger than, the impact of this policy in an economy without household debt.

Redistribution has the greatest impact when monetary policy is temporarily passive before returning to the monetary dominance regime. 
Transfers to the borrower increase total consumption demand, and this results in a burst of inflation unless the monetary authority aggressively 
raises interest rates. Monetary policy that even temporarily ignores inflation results in a feedback loop akin to the debt-deflation of Fisher (1933). 
Transfers to the borrower increase consumption demand and inflation, and this inflation erodes the real value of borrower debt, effectively resulting 
in even more transfers. The impact of this feedback loop increases with the outstanding stock of household debt and can even diverge to an infinite 
response with enough household debt.

We then quantify the impact of redistribution and temporarily passive monetary policy in a richer model with realistic mortgage contracts. The 
model features borrowers and savers with segmented housing markets, a mortgage market with costs of refinancing, wage and price rigidities, and 
fiscal and monetary policy rules. One crucial difference from our simpler theoretical model is that the cost of mortgage refinancing implies that 
a borrower’s holdings of liquid assets are not perfectly substitutable for its illiquid housing wealth. We show that this generates an empirically 
realistic marginal propensity to consume (MPC) out of liquid wealth of 19.6% but only an MPC of 2.9% out of illiquid housing wealth. However, 
an increase in the borrower’s housing wealth, as occurs when inflation erodes the real value of its mortgage debt, results in a persistent drop in 
future labor supply, as (Golosov et al., 2024) find empirically about lottery winners. In our model, the immediate impact of redistribution caused by 
inflating away illiquid mortgage debt is weaker than the impact of directly sending transfer payments that can be consumed immediately. However, 
the impact of inflating away debt is also quite persistent, since illiquid wealth is gradually spent over time.

We  first  analyze  the  impact  of  fiscal/monetary  stimulus  on  the  economy  starting  at  its  steady  state,  outside  of  a  recession.  The  policy 
interventions we consider are: 1. a temporary increase in fiscal transfers, and 2. a temporarily passive monetary policy that eventually reverts 
to the monetary dominance regime. Neither fiscal transfers nor temporarily passive monetary policy cause much inflation alone, but together 
they cause a surge in inflation that peaks near 8%. This inflation erodes the real value of nominal debt and therefore redistributes from savers 
to borrowers, increasing borrower consumption by 6% and reducing saver consumption by 6%. After a few quarters of mildly increased output, 
the economy experiences a contraction of nearly 1% of GDP 2–3 years after the policy. In our model, redistribution that stimulates demand in the 
short term also causes a longer term reduction in supply. This contraction in output happens because inflating away a borrower’s mortgage debt 
results in an empirically realistic long-run reduction in labor supply.

Next, we consider the impact of the same policies in response to an exogenous decrease in consumption demand, motivated by the large 
voluntary drop in consumption during Covid (Chetty et al., 2020). Without emergency policy interventions, the economy would experience a large 
drop in output, deflation, and a rise in unemployment. These are partially offset by adding emergency unemployment insurance, but only with the 
jointly loose fiscal/monetary policy in the previous paragraph is there a surge in inflation and a complete prevention of unemployment. As above, 
this surge of inflation redistributes from savers to borrowers, causing a rise consumption and house prices for borrowers and a fall for savers. This 
redistribution to borrowers also results in a longer term reduction in output as borrowers reduce their labor supply. If instead the economy had 
experienced deflation, borrower consumption and house prices would have decreased and long run output would have increased. We also show that 
if monetary policy were to stay passive for a longer time, a given quantity of fiscal transfers would result in a larger boom in output and inflation. 
As we also find in our theoretical model, passive monetary policy after a recession increases the impact of redistribution during a recession.

Finally, in the Online Appendix, we document in detail the elements of our model that are necessary to explain key features of the recovery 
from the Covid recession. First, we show that the fiscal/monetary policy mix generates a large increase in inflation only when we use a global 
nonlinear solution method (Elenev et al., 2021a) for our model. Simpler local methods or those that ignore aggregate uncertainty about the duration 
of passive monetary policy cannot match the post-Covid surge in inflation, despite being common in the literature on monetary/fiscal interactions. 
Second, we extend our model to be consistent with the booms in house prices and in mortgage refinancing that followed the Covid recession. 
We exogenously increase households’ preferences for consuming housing, yielding a boom in house prices. In addition, we exogenously ‘‘anchor’’ 
mortgage lenders’ inflation expectations, preventing mortgage rates from rising in anticipation of future inflation. This anchoring is consistent with 
inflation swap data, since agents with rational expectations would anticipate the surge in inflation caused by the policy interventions, before the 
inflation occurs.

While our model is motivated by the stylized facts of the unique post-Covid boom, it has two broader lessons for fiscal and monetary policy. 
First, coordinated easing of fiscal and monetary policy provides a particularly strong stimulus. Redistribution to impatient borrowers has the most 
impact when combined with a temporarily loose monetary policy that permits a transitory increase in inflation. This is in contrast to changes in the 
stock of government debt without redistribution, where the long-run monetary policy rule determines the impact of fiscal policy. Second, one of the 
main impacts of this joint fiscal/monetary stimulus comes from ‘‘indirect redistribution’’ caused by inflating away mortgage debt (Auclert, 2019; 
Doepke and Schneider, 2006a). Fiscal/monetary policy which can be expansionary in the short term may also lead to a longer term contraction 
due to reduced borrower labor supply. This longer-term effect of inflation requires a realistic model of mortgages that are costly to refinance, 
demonstrating the importance of household finance for the transmission of monetary and fiscal policy.

Related literature  Our work primarily contributes to three literatures: research on the interaction between fiscal and monetary policy, research 
on the macroeconomic role of household debt, and research particularly focused on the 2020 Covid crisis and recovery. A key part of the first 
literature studies the fiscal theory of the price level (FTPL) (Leeper, 1991; Woodford, 2001; Sims, 1994, 2011; Cochrane, 2001; Bassetto and Cui, 
2018; Brunnermeier et al., 2022), which shows that government debt not backed by future tax revenue causes inflation if monetary policy is 
passive. One branch of FTPL research close to us is (Bianchi and Melosi, 2022; Bianchi and Ilut, 2017) which study economies where government 
debt is only partially paid for with inflation. Also close to us is (Angeletos et al., 2023) who consider when a fiscal expansion can pay for itself. 
Immediate US fiscal concerns do not seem to immediately impact inflation (Jiang et al., 2023), and fiscal-monetary interactions in our paper involve 
redistribution rather than the inability to repay government debt. Consumers in the papers above all aggregate to representative agents. The impact 
of redistribution in our borrower-saver economy is strongest when the monetary authority temporarily deviates from targeting inflation, distinct 
from the focus on the long-run government budget constraint in this literature and other work on long-term policy rules (Clarida et al., 2000; Lubik 
and Schorfheide, 2004). Our work shows that the monetary and fiscal policies that followed the Covid recession were capable of causing a surge 
in inflation without raising concerns about the long-run sustainability of government debt.

Our work is also closely related to a recent literature on the role of household debt, heterogeneity, and redistribution in the transmission of 
macroeconomic policy. Most of this literature (Iacoviello, 2005; Kaplan et al., 2018; Auclert, 2019) analyzes monetary policy, with some work on 
the sustainability of deficit spending (Kaplan et al., 2023). The only work we know that examines monetary/fiscal interactions with household 
heterogeneity are Bhattarai et al. (2023) and Bianchi et al. (2023), which show quantitatively that redistribution is more powerful in the fiscal 
dominance regime than the monetary dominance regime. Our theoretical model is closely related to Eggertsson and Krugman (2012), which analyzes

Finally, our paper contributes to the literature that studies the unusual 2020 Covid recession and the recovery following it (Guerrieri et al.,

transfers and monetary policy in a tractable special case. Section 4 describes and Section 5 calibrates our larger quantitative model. In Section 6, 
we use this quantitative model to analyze the interaction between fiscal and monetary stimulus and confront data from the post-Covid boom, 
followed by a conclusion.

2. Model

We analyze the impact of fiscal and monetary stimulus in a model with borrowers, savers, financial intermediaries, and a government. Borrowers 
partially finance their consumption with mortgages. A financial intermediary holds both mortgages and government debt to back its issuance of 
bank deposits. Output is produced by firms that face nominal rigidities in their price setting. The central bank sets monetary policy following a 
Taylor rule, reacting to both inflation and output in its interest rate choices. Finally, a fiscal authority follows a rule by which it raises lump sum 
taxes (or makes transfers) to borrowers and savers.

We first analyze fiscal and monetary policy in an analytically tractable special case of this model, where the only debt contract is a risk-free 
one period loan. Our tractable special case is quite similar to Eggertsson and Krugman (2012), the only other paper we know of that analytically 
examines the impact of fiscal policy in a model with nominal household debt. However, our application to understanding the interaction between 
fiscal stimulus and monetary policy rules is quite distinct from their analysis of fiscal responses to financial crises.

Next, we quantitatively analyze a richer model, where borrowers issue risky long-term mortgages with realistic refinancing frictions. Here, 
borrowers hold liquid assets such as bank deposits to ensure against idiosyncratic risk, while the cost of refinancing a mortgage makes borrowers’ 
housing wealth illiquid. While models with both liquid and illiquid wealth (following Kaplan and Violante, 2014) are now common in the analysis 
of fiscal policy, ours is the first such model with the realistic detail that a long-maturity mortgage is a major part of illiquid wealth. The ability 
for inflation to increase the borrower’s illiquid wealth by eroding the real value of debt is crucial for our most novel results. In addition, we add 
downward wage rigidity to the model, allowing for involuntary unemployment during a recession.

3.5. Policy interactions

We analyze the impact of two policies in this model under the three policy regimes mentioned above: debt-financed fiscal policy and balanced-
budget redistribution. In the first policy, we exogenously increase the stock of government debt 𝐵𝐺
𝑡  but transfer all the proceeds to the saver. 
In the second, we make a one-time increase in the transfer 𝜃𝑡 but hold current debt levels fixed. Deficit-financed redistribution, like the CARES 
act following the 2020 Covid recession, is a combination of these two interventions. Our main result is that the long-term policy rule determines 
the impact of debt issuance, while the impact of redistribution varies with short-term changes in monetary policy as well. While the theoretical 
literature on monetary/fiscal interactions is large, we are the first to show how the impact of different types of fiscal policies depend on different 
features of the monetary policy rule.

We first show in Appendix  B.3.1 that debt issuance has zero impact on consumption or inflation under the monetary dominance equilibrium 
or with temporarily passive monetary policy. This follows from the fact that the saver is financially unconstrained, and any increase in debt is 
matched with an equal change in the present value of the saver’s future tax payments. In the fiscal dominance regime, debt issuance increases 
output and inflation. This result generalizes (Beck-Friis and Willems, 2017), who analyze a representative agent economy and is the only previous 
work to analytically compute fiscal multipliers in the fiscal dominance regime.

consumption under the monetary dominance regime or with temporarily passive monetary policy. Under the fiscal dominance regime, this debt issuance 
causes positive inflation, and the quantity of inflation is increasing in the stock of outstanding private debt. Appendix Eq. (123) explicitly shows this policy’s

need not involve a long-term sustainability problem for government debt, which would be necessary in more traditional models with only long-lived 
representative agents.

4. Quantitative model

This section presents the additional elements active in our quantitative model, adding to the features described in Section 2 that are shared with 
the simpler model in Section 3. In addition to a banking sector, we introduce a quantitatively more realistic borrower agent who faces idiosyncratic 
liquidity shocks and borrows via long-term mortgage contracts that allow for default and refinancing. A key feature of this richer model is that 
the borrower’s holding of liquid cash holding and its illiquid housing wealth (including a mortgage) are not perfect substitutes for each other. We 
also add a financial intermediary who holds mortgages and government debt and issues deposits and equity. In addition, the model now features 
real wage rigidity as well as nominal price rigidity, allowing for the possibility of involuntary unemployment during a recession. The illiquidity 
of borrowers’ housing wealth is the crucial difference from our simpler model, since inflating away borrowers’ mortgages now has a weaker but 
more persistent impact on borrowers’ choices of consumption and labor.

5.2. Solution method

The economy’s state variables are the wealth of borrowers and of savers and the stocks of outstanding government and mortgage debt. By Walras’ 
law, we only need to track three of these state variables. We track the stock of borrower deposits, outstanding mortgage debt, and outstanding 
government debt. For our Covid crisis experiments, we hit the economy with a large unanticipated demand shock and impose a zero lower bound 
in the monetary policy rule. The shock causes the zero lower bound and the lower bound on wages to bind simultaneously, with agents rationally 
expecting these constraints to bind. To handle these nonlinearities while computing fully stochastic transition paths after the unanticipated Covid 
shock hits the model, we use a global nonlinear solution method described briefly in Appendix D.10.15

6. Quantitative model results

This section uses our quantitative model to simulate the impact of fiscal and monetary policy interventions. First, in Section 6.1 we demonstrate 
that the calibrated model delivers distinct and empirically plausible MPCs out of liquid (deposit) and illiquid (housing) wealth. Next, in Section 6.2 
we examine the impact of an increase in transfer payments combined with temporarily passive monetary policy as analyzed in our theoretical 
model. Consistent with the theory, these two policies together result in increased inflation that redistributes from savers to borrowers that neither 
policy causes alone. The erosion of mortgage debt caused by inflation has a modest but persistent impact on the economy due to the low MPC out 
of illiquid housing wealth. In Section 6.3, we examine this mix of fiscal and monetary policy in response to a recession motivated by the 2020 Covid 
crisis. The recession is triggered by all agents temporarily becoming more patient, reducing consumption demand. This combination of policies 
effectively eliminates any resulting unemployment. In section D.2, we consider modifications to our model that make it consistent with additional 
data from the post-Covid recovery.

these responses in the economy’s stationary distribution.

(2016) but is not binding in our analysis in Fig.  1. In Appendix D.3, we show that models with either lower mortgage refinancing costs or with no 
refinancing costs at all cannot empirically match the slow response of borrower labor and consumption to a reduction in mortgage debt.

The distinction between the borrower’s response to receiving transfers and having its outstanding debt reduced is crucial for understanding the 
impact of fiscal policy in our model. If the borrower receives a large quantity of transfers, as occurred in the 2020 Covid recession, it consumes 
these transfers fairly rapidly. However, if there is a surge in inflation that erodes its outstanding debt, as occurred after the 2020 recession, there is 
a smaller but much more persistent impact on its consumption and labor supply. This is the key distinction between our realistic model of long-term 
mortgage with refinancing frictions and the simpler model of one-period debt in our theoretical analysis. Since (Kaplan and Violante, 2014), the 
literature on fiscal policy has shown the quantitative importance of ‘‘two-asset’’ models where part of household wealth is illiquid. The fact that 
borrowers’ illiquid wealth takes the form of a nominal long-term mortgage that can be inflated away is new in our model and is a key fact about US 
household balance sheets (Doepke and Schneider, 2006a). This feature of our model explains why we find that a surge in inflation has a persistent 
effect on the economy.

6.3. Demand shock and policy response

Next, we analyze policies to respond to a demand-driven recession following an exogenous decrease in consumption demand. This broadly 
captures the finding in Chetty et al. (2020) that the mix of Covid itself and lockdowns fighting it removed ‘‘in person’’ consumption from the 
bundle of consumable goods, reducing aggregate demand. In our experiment, we increase households’ discount factors by 1.5 percentage points 
for 3 quarters, after which they revert to their original values with probability .4 each quarter. This demand-driven recession results in a deep 
contraction in output, the policy rate to hit the zero lower bound, and involuntary unemployment due to binding wage rigidty.

We consider three possible policy responses to this drop in consumption demand in Figs.  3–4. First, the line ‘‘Demand Shock’’ shows the impact 
of the decrease in demand with only the standard policy responses built into our monetary and fiscal rules, which includes replacing the wages of 
unemployed workers at 50%. Second, ‘‘+ Unemp. Benefits’’ shows the impact of replacing the salary of all unemployed workers at 100% instead. 
Finally, ‘‘+ Trans. & MP’’ adds a 7% of GDP fiscal stimulus and a deviation from the Taylor rule for monetary policy that puts a .73 weight on a 
fixed interest rate like in Section 6.2, with the important difference that the fixed interest target is now zero instead of the steady-state rate.

The combination of loose fiscal and monetary policy in the ‘‘+ Trans. & MP’’ response entirely prevents a drop in output and employment. 
Without a policy response, the economy would have experienced a 2.8% drop in output, which falls to roughly a 2% drop with full unemployment 
insurance. In addition, the economy would have experienced deflation up to 4%, which the loose fiscal/monetary policy transformed into a surge 
in inflation up to 8%. This surge in inflation results in redistribution from savers to borrowers, while deflation would have instead resulted in 
redistribution from borrowers to savers. With inflation, we see a boom in borrower consumption and a drop in saver consumption. These results 
mirror our result in Proposition  3, showing that a surge in inflation due to temporarily passive monetary policy after a recession can aid recovery 
during the recession. In addition, interest rates increase up to 4% and then gradually revert to steady-state as inflation recedes. Because interest 
rates do not keep up with inflation, low real rates initially increase borrowers’ marginal propensity to consume, consistent with Proposition  3. After 
inflation recedes, the borrower MPC falls due to the borrower’s increased holdings of liquid wealth.

6.3.1. The present impact of passive monetary policy in the future

In Fig.  5, we examine the impact of allowing monetary policy to remain passive for an extended period of time. The experiment ‘‘Transf. & 
Long MP’’ begins with the same fiscal expansion and temporarily passive monetary policy as considered in Fig.  3 with a .4 probability of ending 
each quarter. However, after this regime ends, there is a second stage where only fiscal policy returns to normal, with monetary policy maintaining 
its .73 weight on a passive policy rule. This second stage then has a .4 probability of reverting to the standard active monetary policy rule each 
quarter, which in expectation extends the time during which monetary policy remains passive. This experiment allows us to test the implication 
of Proposition  3 that passive monetary policy in the future increases the impact of redistribution in the present.

Consistent with Proposition  3, we find that inflation and output increase more in the ‘‘Tranf. & Long MP’’ experiment than the ‘‘Tranf. & MP’’ 
where monetary policy returns more quickly to its normal active rule. Inflation reaches 13% instead of just 8%. Output also grows more in the 
‘‘Long MP’’ experiment at first and then falls by a greater amount as well. Borrower consumption and house prices increase by more, and saver 
consumption and house prices fall by more as well. In effect, extending the time during which monetary policy remains passive in the future 
increases the impact of present redistribution.

7. Conclusion

This paper theoretically and quantitatively analyzes the impact of fiscal and monetary stimulus in an economy with household debt, where 
inflation causes redistribution. In both our theoretical and quantitative models, the impact of redistribution is greatest when combined with a 
temporarily passive monetary policy rule. When combined with temporarily passive monetary policy, redistribution causes a surge of inflation that 
causes even more redistribution from savers to borrowers. In our theoretical model, this results in a feedback loop between redistribution and 
inflation that can result in an arbitrarily large impact of redistribution policies. In contrast, our quantitative model is the first analysis of fiscal 
policy that reflects the fact that inflation mostly transfers illiquid housing wealth to borrowers by inflating away their mortgages. Because changes 
in illiquid wealth have a small but persistent impact on borrower consumption and labor supply, a surge in inflation has a persistent impact that 
reduces output years into the future. Our paper is the first to demonstrate that private nominal debt, not just government debt, plays an important 
role when analyzing the interaction between fiscal and monetary policy.

Appendix B. Simplified new keynesian model

This appendix formally proves all of our results in our simplified analytic model. First, in Appendix  B.1, we log-linearize all of the model’s 
equations. In our log-linearized model, we derive in Eq.  (108) a transition matrix that maps the state of the model at time 𝑡 to its expected state 
at time 𝑡 + 1. Next in Appendix  B.2, we compute the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of this matrix, which has two applications. First, we determine 
what possible fiscal and monetary policy rules result (locally) in a determinate equilibrium of the model. Second, we use this decomposition to 
show how the model responds to policy interventions and how this response varies depending on the fiscal and monetary policy rules. Finally, in 
Appendix  B.4, we consider the impact of fiscal interventions when the borrower is sufficiently wealthy that it voluntarily chooses to save.
