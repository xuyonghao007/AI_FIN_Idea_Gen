A B S T R A C T

This paper develops a difference-in-differences estimator that uses annual changes in the conforming loan limit
and the 80% loan-to-value (LTV) threshold to isolate the impact of easier access to credit on house prices.
Houses that become eligible for financing with an 80% LTV conforming loan increase in value by about $1.17
per square foot, controlling for a rich set of characteristics. Our estimates imply a local elasticity of house
prices to interest rates below 6, which suggests that interest rates are capitalized into prices to a lesser extent
than proposed by studies relying on more aggregate variation.

1.  Introduction

A fundamental question in finance involves understanding the im-
pact of easier access to credit and lower financing costs on asset prices.
In the housing market, easier credit conditions have been associated
with pronounced house price increases and subsequent reversals when
credit dries up. This dynamic received considerable attention in the
context of the US housing boom of the early 2000s, and earlier work
suggests that supply-side factors such as lower interest rates, financial
innovation, securitization, and reductions in collateral constraints con-
tributed to the boom. However, the literature has also shown that these
shifts  in  credit  access  and  cost  went  hand  in  hand  with  changes  in

market sentiment and expectations about future appreciation, which
in turn increased asset prices and relaxed credit constraints through
increased collateral values.3 A key difficulty in this literature is to es-
tablish the direction of causality between credit conditions and prices:
How  does  easier  access  to  credit  get  capitalized  into  house  prices
holding  constant  house  price  expectations,  demand  for  housing  and
improved collateral values?

In this paper, we develop a difference-in-differences estimator that
uses annual changes in the conforming loan limit (CLL) to generate
exogenous variation in the cost and availability of credit for individual
housing transactions. A key advantage of this approach is that, during

in the national single family house price index, and so were unrelated
to local housing markets and the local economy.4 This generates ex-

using a conforming loan. We find that a home that is easy to finance
with  a  conforming  loan,  on  average,  has  a  $1.17  higher  value  per
square foot compared to a house that falls just outside the range of
CLL funding. This result forms the basis of our estimate of how lower
interest rates get capitalized into house prices.

The CLL determines the maximum size of a mortgage that can be
purchased or securitized by Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac. This implicit
(and  since  2008,  explicit)  government  support  for  loans  below  the
conforming  loan  limit  reduces  the  cost  of  borrowing  and  provides
better access to credit for a wide range of borrowers. The difference
in interest rates between conforming and jumbo loans (those above the
conforming limit) during our sample period (before the 2008 financial
crisis)  was  estimated  to  be  up  to  24  basis  points  (McKenzie, 2002;
Ambrose et al., 2004; Sherlund, 2008; Kaufman, 2014; DeFusco and
Paciorek, 2017). Loutskina and Strahan (2009, 2011) and DeFusco and
Paciorek (2017) show that lenders screen borrowers for jumbo loans
more intensely than they do for those that apply for conforming loans,
which implies that mortgages below the conforming loan limit are not
only cheaper but also easier to obtain.

To identify the latent set of properties that are ‘‘easy’’ to finance
with  a  conforming  loan  in  a  given  year,  we  divide  the  conforming
loan limit by 0.8, a widely used loan-to-value (LTV) threshold in the
industry (Green and Wachter, 2005).5 By construction, houses below
this threshold can get a conforming loan for 80 percent of the price
of the house, whereas houses that transact above 125 percent of the
CLL  cannot.  For  transactions  above  this  price  threshold,  borrowers
either have to finance their home with an 80 percent mortgage using a
jumbo loan at a higher interest rate or, if they want to take advantage
of  the  lower  interest  rate  below  the  CLL,  they  must  use  savings  or
alternative forms of financing to make a larger down payment. Either
way,  transactions  above  the  CLL  face  a  more  constrained  access  to
finance and a higher cost of capital.

To assess the impact of easier access to conforming loans on house
prices, we run differences-in-differences regressions in which we com-
pare  transactions  just  above  the  threshold  of  125%  of  the  CLL  in  a
given year to properties that fall below the threshold in the subsequent
year, after the limit has been increased and can now be financed with
an 80% conforming loan. The intuition is that the transactions above
the threshold are harder to finance and thus their prices have been
bid  up  less  relative  to  the  underlying  fundamentals.  An  increase  in
the  conforming  loan  limit  should  affect  this  part  of  the  house  price
distribution  more  strongly  since  it  allows  new  potential  borrowers
to  enter  this  segment  of  the  market.  In  addition,  since  house  price
levels differ markedly across regions in the United States, the change
in  the  CLL  affects  different  parts  of  the  housing  stock  and  different
borrowers across areas depending on the local price level. This rules
out the possibility that differential trends in prices and income of a
particular type of house and borrower could explain the results. Finally,
we cannot use a simple regression discontinuity design within a given

By using all properties around the 125% threshold, independent of how

We  start  by  confirming  that  the  CLL  and  the  80%  cutoff  affect
borrowers’  choice  of  financing.  The  norm  in  the  mortgage  market
during this period was to borrow at a first mortgage LTV of exactly 0.8
(about 60% of transactions have an LTV of 0.8). For house purchases
just  above  125%  of  the  CLL,  a  much  larger  fraction  of  transactions
is  at  an  LTV  below  80%  and  at  exactly  the  conforming  loan  limit,
confirming that the CLL is also a key determinant of mortgage finance.
As  we  mention  above,  these  borrowers  may  use  a  conforming  loan
either because the combination of a conforming loan and other forms of
financing (savings or other forms of debt) is cheaper than a jumbo loan,
or because they are excluded from the jumbo loan market altogether.
Using the exogenous variation generated by the annual CLL changes,
we then show that transactions just above 125% of the CLL, that is,
those that are ‘‘harder’’ to finance, occur at lower values per square
foot than those below the threshold. To increase the comparability of
house characteristics, we focus on houses that transact within a band
of $10,000 dollars around the threshold of the conforming loan limit
divided by 0.8. Because we look locally around the threshold, all of
the transactions above the CLL that we include in a given year would
be eligible to be financed at 80 percent LTV in the subsequent year,
i.e. after the limit was raised. We observe a $1.17 discount per square
foot for a mean value per square foot of $220 (i.e., about 53 basis points
of the average house value). This difference is reduced to $0.67 per
square foot (30 basis points) after we control for house characteris-
tics, suggesting that part of the effect we find can be accounted for
by  differences  in  the  observable  quality  of  houses  above  and  below
the threshold. These effects are significantly different from those we
obtain when we use placebo loan limits elsewhere in the distribution,
confirming that we are picking up the causal effect of the CLL.

Our  findings  are  qualitatively  unchanged  when  we  use  two  ad-
ditional  measures  of  the  property  value  to  account  for  underlying
differences  in  house  quality:  (1)  the  residuals  of  the  logarithm  of
house prices from a hedonic regression using a large set of controls
for the underlying characteristics of the house and (2) the residuals
of the value per square foot from similar hedonic regressions. We run
the hedonic regressions by year and by metropolitan statistical area,
and  use  the  set  of  controls  available  from  deeds  registries.  Controls
include not only common variables, such as the number of rooms and
bedrooms, but also details about the type of heating, architectural type,
and building type, among many other variables.

For our main specifications, we assign transactions to the treatment
and control groups using transaction prices. We confirm that our results
are robust to assigning the treatment based on ex-ante predicted house
values  using  hedonic  regressions.  The  effects  are  quantitatively  and
qualitatively  unchanged.  Furthermore,  we  show  that  our  results  are
not driven by a focal point effect, whereby more bidders are drawn
in to bid on houses just around the threshold. First, there is no visible
bunching in the number of transactions just around the CLL divided
by 0.8, suggesting that the supply of houses on the market does not
strongly react to the CLL. We also do not find bunching in the number
of unobserved bidders for homes around the CLL, which we measure
as the share of borrowers who apply for loans but ultimately either
withdraw or do not use the loans they are approved for. If the CLL
were just a focal point for listing and sales values, we should expect
more bidders who are eligible for conforming loans. Instead, we find
that our measure of the share of failed bids is lower for borrowers below
CLL, inconsistent with the notion that the CLL/0.8 is an important focal
point for bidding.

of financing with a conforming loan, we can compute a semi-elasticity

of prices to differences in interest rates near the threshold. As we have
previously described, easier access to financing due to the conforming
loan limit represents a bundled treatment that varies among borrowers
based on their available outside options. To estimate a semi-elasticity,
we  focus  on  a  borrower  transitioning  from  a  conforming  loan  to  a
jumbo loan, and we use the difference in interest rates between the two
kinds of loans of 10 to 24 basis points estimated in the prior literature.
For buyers moving from a conforming loan below the threshold to a
jumbo loan above, we obtain local elasticity estimates ranging from
1.3  to  5.3.  These  elasticity  estimates  are  at  the  lower  end  of  what
prior research has found, suggesting a relatively muted pass-through of
changes in interest rates to house prices (a point also made by Glaeser
et al., 2013). While this is a local estimate of the elasticity around the
conforming loan limit for borrowers moving from the conforming to the
jumbo loan market, it provides one of the first empirical evaluations of
how easier access to financing due to the availability of conforming
loans is capitalized into house prices. These findings also suggest that
a  portion  of  the  benefits  of  easier  access  to  credit  is  passed  onto
homeowners in the form of increased house prices.

Our paper contributes to a large literature exploring several aspects
of  the  aggregate  relationship  between  changes  in  credit  conditions
and  house  prices. Hubbard  and  Mayer (2009), Davis  et  al. (2020),
and Justiniano et al. (2022), among others, highlight the role of lower
interest rates in driving house prices. Calomiris (2009), Mian and Sufi
(2009), and Pavlov and Wachter (2011) discuss the role of financial in-
novation and securitization, and Khandani et al. (2013), Favilukis et al.
(2017), Corbae and Quintin (2015), Landvoigt et al. (2015), Greenwald
(2018), DeFusco (2018),  and Zevelev (2021)  are  some  examples  of
work  focusing  on  how  reductions  in  collateral  constraints  affect  the
housing cycle. While the focus of this line of work is on the role of
easier credit on house prices, an influential set of studies has argued
that easier credit is often accompanied or even preceded by changes
in market sentiment and expectations about future appreciation and
that credit alone cannot explain the large boom in prices witnessed
during the housing bubble of the early 2000s (Shiller, 2008; Glaeser,
2013; Glaeser et al., 2013; Adelino et al., 2016; Nathanson and Zwick,
2018; DeFusco et al., 2022). Many prominent papers have discussed
the broader relationship between credit, asset prices and the creation
of bubbles (seminal papers include Kiyotaki and Moore, 1997; Allen
and Gale, 1998; Bernanke and Gertler, 2001; Case and Shiller, 2003;
Kindleberger  et  al., 2005; Geanakoplos, 2010; Brunnermeier  et  al.,
2012).

Closer  to  the  specific  question  we  address  in  this  paper, Favara
and Imbs (2015) use exposure to bank branching deregulation as an
instrument for credit-supply shocks at the state level to quantify the
effect on house prices. Di Maggio and Kermani (2017) use variation in
anti-predatory lending laws at the state level to quantify the impact of
boom and bust in house prices resulting from credit-supply shocks.6
Ours  is  the  first  paper  to  use  micro-level  supply  shocks  to  identify
a causal link between interest rates and house prices during normal
times.  Our  focus  on  individual-level  transactions  allows  us  to  more
precisely  quantify  a  range  of  house  price  elasticities  to  inform  the
relationship between credit supply and asset prices. Argyle et al. (2021)
explore the role of a credit supply shock on the price of durable goods
with a focus on the maturity of loans. Kaufman (2014) uses the price
divided by 80% threshold for appraisal values to study the effect of the
conforming status of a loan on its cost and contract structure. Loutskina
and Strahan (2015) follow our approach by using changes in the CLL
to shock the availability of credit at the regional level to investigate
financial integration and the propagation of shocks.

2.  The user cost model

The existing literature has focused on different versions of the user-
cost model (Poterba, 1984) to draw conclusions about the impact of
changes in the cost of credit on the price of housing. In this model, the
mortgage interest rate is the main determinant of the cost of owning
and agents are indifferent between owning and renting if the housing
market  is  in  equilibrium.  However,  the  magnitude  of  the  elasticity
between  mortgage  rates  and  house  prices  depends  on  a  number  of
different assumptions about other parameters, such as borrowers’ pri-
vate discount factors or expectations about future mortgage rates. Since
many of these parameters are not observable, it is important to get a
precise estimate of the impact of the cost of credit on prices.

To fix the intuition, we follow Glaeser et al. (2013) and describe the
basic elements of the user cost model. This result is analogous to the
setup in Hubbard and Mayer (2009) as well as a simplified version of
the user cost in Himmelberg et al. (2005). Renting a property involves
paying  rent  equal  to 𝑅𝑡 in  each  period.  Owning  a  property,  on  the
other hand, includes making a down payment 𝜃 that is a proportion
of the price of the house 𝑃𝑡 and obtaining a mortgage that is rolled
over each period, such that principal is never completely paid down.
The borrower pays interest on the mortgage at a rate 𝑟𝑡 that is deflated
by the relevant tax rate 𝜙, as well as property taxes and maintenance
costs equal to 𝜏. Both rents and house maintenance costs grow at a rate
of 𝑔. The model assumes that individuals have a private discount factor
of 𝜌𝑡. If we assume that market interest rates and private discount rates
are constant and equal to each other (i.e., 𝜌 = 𝑟), we can write the
indifference condition for users as

If the assumptions of this model hold, changes in the user cost (the
right-hand  side  of  the  equation)  lead  to  changes  in  the  equilibrium
price-to-rent ratio. For example, if the user cost is 5%, then the price
of a house should be about 20 times its market rent. In such a world,
a  drop  of  one  percentage  point  in  mortgage  rates  would  lead  to  a
decline of (1 − 𝜏) in the user cost, or 0.75, if we assume a marginal
tax rate of 25%. The price-to-rent ratio then would be 23.5, which is
an increase in the price of 17.5% (an elasticity of 17.5). This magnitude
of elasticities is proposed in Himmelberg et al. (2005) and Hubbard and
Mayer (2009) to explain both the time series and the cross-section of
house prices in the previous decades.

Glaeser et al. (2013) show that a more realistic version of the user
cost model produces lower elasticities of prices to interest rates than the
simple model. In particular, if private discount rates are not the same as
market rates, changes in interest rates will not mechanically alter how
users discount future expected house price appreciation. This change
alone can reduce the elasticity to just 8, instead of the initial 17.5.
Other mechanisms through which the elasticity could be substantially
reduced include mean-reverting interest rates, which means borrowers
anticipate having to sell a home at a time when rates are higher, or
the  possibility  of  prepaying  a  mortgage.  Our  econometric  approach
allows us to more carefully identify the magnitude of the change in
house prices due to changes in the average cost of financing due to
the conforming loan limit, since we look at movements in the cost of
capital for home buyers that are exogenous to local conditions and to
individual transactions. Our empirical results provide local estimates
for the numerator of the elasticity calculation. In Section 5.3, we discuss
the range of elasticities that is consistent with our results.

One of the advantages of using deeds registry data is the richness of
the information available about property characteristics. The depth of
information allows us to account for price differences between houses
on the basis of observable features. Specifically, we assess whether the
observed  price  impact  due  to  changes  in  the  conforming  loan  limit
is attributable to differences in house quality, or if these differences
persist even after accounting for quality.

3.3.  Empirical approach

To identify the effect of changes in credit conditions due to easier
access to conforming loans on house prices, we restrict our analysis to
two groups of transactions within tight price range that differ in the
financing available to them. The regression sample consists of houses
that transact in a band around 125% of each year’s conforming loan
limit,  as  well  as  houses  from  the  following  year  in  the  same  price
range. Specifically, we divide houses into two groups: houses below the
threshold of 125% of the year’s CLL (i.e., transactions that fall between
125% of the CLL and 125% of the CLL minus $10,000) and houses above

the threshold that transact between 125% of the CLL and 125% of the
CLL+$10,000.

By construction, in the year that the conforming loan limit is in
effect,  houses  above  the  threshold  of  125%  of  the  CLL  cannot  be
financed at 80% using a conforming loan, whereas the houses below
the threshold can be financed. Thus, home buyers who bid for houses
priced above 125% of the CLL cannot finance a full 80% of the trans-
action with the cheaper and more easily available conforming loans. In
the subsequent year, the CLL is raised, and both groups of transactions
can be financed at 80% with a conforming loan.7 Our sample includes
all transactions in this price range, regardless of the mortgage choice
made by each buyer. This ensures our estimates are not biased by the
endogenous financing choice of each specific transaction.

Consider, for instance, the year 1999: In that year, the conforming
loan  limit  (CLL)  for  single-family  houses  was  $240,000.  The  corre-
sponding threshold for houses that year is $300,000 (1.25 × 240, 000). In
that year, houses above the threshold have prices between $300,000 and
$310,000, and houses below the threshold have a transaction price be-
tween $290,000 and $300,000 (those that transact at exactly $300,000
are included in this second group). In our main regressions, we track
these two groups of houses from 1999 to 2000, with 1999 being the
year the CLL is in effect, and 2000 being the year all these transactions
could be bought using a conforming loan at a full 80% LTV. Indeed, the
CLL changed in 2000 to $252,700, so the threshold of 125% of the CLL
was $315,875, and our above-the-threshold group for 1999 could now
access an 80% LTV conforming loan.

group above the threshold of 125% of the CLL are constrained in their

3.3.1.  Empirical specification

Our main regressions estimate the size of the effect of the constraint
imposed by the conforming loan limit on the valuation of transactions
that  occur  just  above  the  threshold  of  125%  of  the  CLL.  We  run
difference-in-differences  regressions  year  by  year  with  one  indicator
variable  for  houses  priced  above  the  conforming  loan  limit  divided
by  0.8,  another  indicator  for  the  year  in  which  the  CLL  is  in  effect
(effectively the ‘‘pre’’ year), and the interaction of these two indicator
variables. We also include ZIP code fixed effects in all regressions, so
our estimates do not reflect differences between neighborhoods, but
rather variation within ZIP codes.

The sample for each year-by-year regression includes houses within
a $10,000 band around the conforming loan limit in the year when
the limit is in force, as well as the subsequent year. The ‘‘Above the
threshold’’ indicator variable takes a value of one if the price at which
a house transacts is greater than 125% of the conforming loan limit of
a certain year and less than that amount plus $10,000. This variable
is  zero  for  transactions  between  125%  of  the  CLL  and  125%  of  the
CLL minus $10,000. The ‘‘Year CLL’’ indicator variable is set to one
in the year when the CLL is in effect for each regression and zero in
the subsequent year.

We estimate this regression for each year between 1998 and 2005.
We cannot include 2006 and 2007 because the national conforming
loan limit did not change after 2006 in our sample period (house prices
dropped and the administration left the limit unchanged). We also do
not analyze the changes that were made to conforming loan limits in
2008 in high-cost areas as part of the Housing and Economic Recovery
Act  of  2008;  the  limit  was  chosen  by  the  government,  as  opposed

the standard deviation of the estimated coefficients and dividing the
standard deviation by the square root of the number of coefficients.
We test the robustness of our results to serial correlation in the error
term. We do so by constructing Newey–West standard errors and find
that all the results are unchanged.

to the Fama–MacBeth procedure described above. To understand the
pooled specification, it is useful to review how the regression sample is
created. Each change in the CLL defines a specific set of transactions
associated  with  that  year  and  the  next.  For  example,  the  change  in
CLL from 1998 of $277,150 to the 1999 limit of $240,000 defines a
set  of  houses  around  the  1998  threshold  (1.25 × 𝐶 𝐿𝐿,  or  $346,438
in 1998). In 1998, the houses below this threshold are eligible to be
funded with an 80% conforming loan, but houses that transact above
are  not.  However,  houses  around  the  same  1998  threshold  in  1999
are all eligible to be financed with a conforming loan because of the
change  in  the  conforming  loan  limit.  All  transactions  in  1998  and
1999 within this $10, 000 band around the threshold are assigned to a

for all years in a pooled specification that includes event-cohort fixed

Our approach is not a regression discontinuity design but rather a
differences-in-differences design for each pair of years. We take this
approach  for  a  couple  of  reasons:  First,  the  threshold  that  we  use
does not imply a sharp discontinuity in the ease of financing a home.
For  a  house  just  one  dollar  above  the  threshold,  a  home  buyer  can
still obtain a conforming mortgage by simply providing one additional
dollar of equity, thereby keeping the total cost of financing the house
nearly  unchanged  (as  we  discuss  in  detail  in  the  previous  section
when we describe Fig. 6). As we move progressively away from the
threshold, transactions become harder to finance. For our differences-
in-differences estimator to be valid, all we need is that houses above
the threshold are somewhat harder to finance, though not necessarily
discontinuously so.

The second reason for not using a regression discontinuity design is
that in the year that the limit is in effect, home buyers choose to buy
houses above or below the threshold, meaning the position with respect
to  the  limit  is  not  exogenous.  On  the  contrary,  our  differences-in-
differences specification uses the exogenous change in the conforming
loan limit to compare a group of transactions that are above the limit
in a year, but below in the next with a group of transaction that are
always below the limit. This approach achieves a clean identification
of the effect of credit availability on house prices.

Our identification strategy can account for the possibility of differ-
ential growth rates within the distribution of house types across the
country. Differential growth rates affect different parts of the housing
stock across areas depending on the price level of the area, so the use
of  one  threshold  for  the  whole  country  mitigates  concerns  that  one
‘‘special’’ type of home buyer drives our estimate.

4.  Evidence on financing choices and effects on quantities

As pointed out earlier, the first stage in our empirical strategy is to
demonstrate that the changes in the conforming loan limit significantly
affect the financing choices of borrowers. As Fig. 1 shows, the most
frequent choice on the part of borrowers is to have an LTV of exactly
80% (represented by the large mass along the diagonal of the figure).
This holds also for jumbo loans, i.e. those above the conforming loan
limit. The main exception to this rule occurs exactly at the conforming
loan limit, where a significant mass of borrowers chooses an LTV below
0.8 by sticking to a conforming loan (in 2000, the limit was $252,700,
and, in 2004, it was $333,700, and we can see a significant mass at
these points in Panels (a) and (b) of Fig. 1). The data show that in the
year in which the CLL is in effect, about 45% of the houses below the
threshold in our sample are bought with an LTV of exactly 80%. For
houses just above this boundary just 19% of borrowers pick 80% LTVs,
which means using a jumbo loan for these transactions. Additionally, on
average 55% of the transactions just above the threshold are financed
using  a  conforming  loan,  which  means  having  an  LTV  lower  than
80%. These borrowers end up with an LTV of 77%–79.5%, which is a
very infrequent choice anywhere else in the distribution. Again, these
borrowers might have a lower LTV because they choose to stay below
the  CLL  due  to  the  cost  of  the  loan  or  because  they  are  excluded
from  the  jumbo  market  altogether.  Whatever  the  reason,  this  group
of borrowers is ‘‘constrained’’ in the set of options available to them
for financing their house. We plot the fraction of borrowers choosing
a jumbo loan around the threshold of 1.25 x CLL in Figure 2 of the
Online Appendix.

the year that the limit is in effect (the year in which the CLL is in
effect is the ‘‘pre’’ year in the regressions). This effect is statistically
and economically significant given how little variation there is in the
modal choice of LTV of borrowers. The second column of both Panels
in Table 3 shows that borrowers also obtain, on average, smaller loans

in the year that the limit is in effect and when the price of the house
is above the threshold, consistent with the result on LTVs.

4.1.  Number of transactions

There  are  several  reasons  to  expect  quantities  to  change  due  to
differential cost of credit, including different levels of down-payment
(Stein, 1995) or sellers waiting for buyers to obtain better credit condi-
tions (Genesove and Mayer, 1997). In fact, unless the supply elasticity
of houses is very low (or zero), we expect the price effect due to a
change in the demand for housing to be accompanied by a change in
the number of transactions.

As discussed in Section 3.3.1, we do not use a regression disconti-
nuity approach to address the question of the change in the quantity
of transactions. Fig. 3 confirms that this would produce no significant
result.  This  figure  shows  the  number  of  transactions  relative  to  the
threshold in each year. The figure is centered on zero, that is, the trans-
actions at exactly 125% of the CLL. The figure shows no discontinuity
in the number of transactions above and below the threshold.

We consider the difference in the share of transactions in our sample
that falls above and below the threshold in the year that the limit is in
effect and in the subsequent year in a differences-in-differences setup.
This test is equivalent to a T -test for the mean of the variable ‘‘Above
the threshold’’, which compares the average of this variable in the year
that the limit is in effect (the ‘‘pre’’ year) and in the subsequent year.
If the change in the CLL affects the quantity of transactions, we should
see an increase in the share of observations above the threshold when
the limit is raised, as credit becomes cheaper for those transactions.

Table 4 shows the effect for the quantity of transactions. This effect
translates  into  a  share  of  transactions  above  the  threshold  approxi-
mately  30  basis  points  lower  (statistically  insignificant)  in  the  year
that the conforming loan limit is in effect when we do not include ZIP
code fixed effects (columns (1) and (2)). Columns (3) and (4) show the
Fama–MacBeth and pooled versions of the regressions when we include
ZIP code fixed effects. The point estimates are larger, at about 1.6 to 1.7
percentage points, and they are statistically significant. This regression
shows that cheaper credit affects both quantities and prices, but the
effect on quantities is economically small overall.

5.  Ease of access to conforming loans and house prices

Table  5 presents  the  results  for  our  baseline  specification  of  the
effect on house prices. This table presents Fama–MacBeth coefficients
from year-by-year regressions, as previously described in Section 3.3.1.
The coefficient of interest in Panel A of Table 5 is that on the interaction
variable,  and  it  shows  that  houses  above  the  threshold  of  the  CLL
divided by 0.8 transacted at a value per square foot that was lower
by about $1.17 in the year that the CLL was in effect.

The other coefficients on the regressions for value per square foot
are  consistent  with  what  we  know  about  house  prices  over  this  pe-
riod. Houses above the threshold of 125% of the CLL (i.e., the more
expensive houses in the regression sample) are associated with a higher
average  value  per  square  foot.  In  unreported  analyses,  we  find  that
more expensive houses are generally associated with a higher value
per square foot (i.e., price rises quicker than house size in the whole
distribution of transactions). This is also the case within the regression
sample.  Also,  the  ‘‘Year  CLL’’  dummy  variable  is  associated  with  a
strong negative effect, reflecting the strong increase in house valuations
that we saw in this period in the United States. Given that the year in
which the CLL is in effect is always the ‘‘pre’’ year in the regressions,
we expect those transactions, on average, to be associated with a lower
value per square foot.

5.2.  Falsification tests

We want to rule out that our results are driven by differences in
secular trends between houses on either side of the threshold of 125%
of the CLL. Specifically, if more expensive houses have, on average,
lower house price growth from one year to the next relative to less
expensive houses, we might obtain the results reported in Table 5, but
we might also obtain similar results for samples with transactions above
and below other arbitrary thresholds.

To  distinguish  between  the  effect  of  true  conforming  loan  limits
and different trends along the distribution of houses, we run the same
regressions described in Section 3.3.1 for placebo loan limits. We do
this by shifting the true conforming loan limit in $10,000 steps from the
true value each year. We start at CLL-$100,000 and move 20 steps until
we reach CLL+$100,000. For each of these 21 tests, we first define the
‘‘shift’’ relative to the true conforming loan limits, and then we change
the  limits  for  all  years  by  that  amount.  For  example,  when  we  are
changing all the limits by −$20,000, this means that the ‘‘placebo’’ limit
for 1999 is $220,000 instead of the true $240,000, the ‘‘placebo’’ limit
for 2000 is $232,700 instead of $252,700, and so on. We then run the
same year-by-year regressions and produce Fama–MacBeth coefficients
for each of the 20 alternative ‘‘placebo’’ values for the CLL. Table 6
shows the results produced by this exercise.

The table shows that the coefficients of interest we obtain for all
three dependent variables (values per square foot, residuals from the
transaction amounts, and residuals of values per square foot) are among
the  lowest  of  all  those  obtained  with  the  20  ‘‘placebo’’  trials  (the
ranking is given in the last two rows of the table). The coefficient for the
value per square foot measure is the lowest of the 21 trials if we use the
whole sample or if we limit our attention to a sample of transactions
that all have an LTV between 0.5 and 0.8.9 The coefficients for the
true conforming loan limits are the third and second lowest when we
use the whole sample and the two residual measures from the hedonic
regressions as the left-hand-side variables in the regressions.

5.3.  Economic magnitude of the effect

Section 2 discusses the user cost model and highlights the substan-
tial disagreement surrounding the magnitude of house price elasticity
to interest rates, as changes to the way a standard user cost model is
specified can produce vastly different estimates. As we also discuss both
in the introduction and then in more detail in Section 3.3, harder access
to conforming loans above the threshold of 125% of the CLL translates
into a higher weighted average cost of capital for buyers of those homes
relative to homes below the threshold. Fig. 6 illustrates the evolution
of the weighted average spread of the cost of an 80% LTV loan over
the conforming loan rate for buyers with and without access to jumbo
loans.

To understand the magnitude of the effect of easier access to con-
forming loans on house prices, we use our estimate of a change in house
prices around the CLL that ranges from 30.4 to 53.2 basis points (bps).
We obtain a low of 30.4 bps when we use the residuals from the hedonic
regressions with value per square foot as the dependent variable. The
point  estimate  in  the  regressions  is  $0.67  in Table  5,  and  we  scale
this by the average value per square foot in the sample ($220/sq.ft.,
from the summary statistics table, Panel B, column 4) to obtain a 30.4
bp change. The high end of the estimate (53.2 bps) comes from the
specification that uses the raw value per square foot as the dependent
variable (point estimate of $1.17 from column (1) in Table 5, scaled by
the same $220 per square foot from the summary statistics).

6.  Conclusion

In this paper, we develop a difference-in-differences estimator that
uses localized shocks to credit conditions induced by the yearly changes
in the conforming loan limit (CLL). Our micro-level approach allows
us  to  estimate  the  impact  of  access  to  conforming  loans  on  house
prices while controlling for changes in housing markets or economic
conditions at a granular level. We find that a home that is easier to
finance with a conforming loan due to an increase in the CLL has, on
average, a $1.17 higher value per square foot compared to a closely
comparable house that is just above the threshold for a conforming
loan  (with  an  80%  loan-to-value).  The  estimated  magnitude  of  this
difference is economically important — the average value per square
foot of houses that transact around the CLL is $220, which means that
an increase of $1.17 constitutes a 0.53% increase in prices.

These results highlight that the CLL constitutes an important con-
sideration for many households when deciding how much mortgage
debt  to  take  on.  Most  borrowers  choose  an  LTV  of  80%  when  they
buy a home, but many deviate in order to stay below the conforming
loan limit when the home crosses the threshold of 125% of the CLL.
These borrowers are either unable to obtain a jumbo loan or find it
cheaper to combine a larger down payment with a conforming loan
at an LTV below 80%. As a result, these borrowers end up holding a
larger fraction of equity in their house. This tendency of households
to  ‘‘stretch’’  around  the  CLL  also  highlights  why  one  cannot  use  a
simple RDD approach, and it underscores the need for the estimator
we develop in this paper.

Finally, our results suggest that the interest rate subsidy granted by
the GSEs and, ultimately, the taxpayer not only benefits home buyers

13 

but  partially  accrues  to  homeowners  (sellers)  in  the  form  of  higher
house prices. However, our estimated elasticity of mortgage rates to
prices around the threshold we focus on is at the lower range of what
has been previously proposed using time-series variation or estimates
from major shocks to the housing market. This lower estimate might,
in part, result from our approach’s ability to more effectively control
for other factors that impact housing markets and are often correlated
with interest rate changes.
