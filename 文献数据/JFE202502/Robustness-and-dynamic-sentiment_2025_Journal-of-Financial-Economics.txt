A B S T R A C T

Errors in survey expectations display waves of pessimism and optimism. This paper develops a novel theoretical
framework of time-varying beliefs capturing this fact. In our model, dynamic beliefs arise endogenously due
to agents’ attitude towards alternative models. Decision-maker’s distorted beliefs generate countercyclical risk
aversion, procyclical portfolio weights, and countercyclical equilibrium asset returns. A calibrated version of
our model is shown to jointly match salient features in survey data and equity markets.

A large empirical literature in macroeconomics and finance docu-
ments significant dynamic variation in forecasters’ expectational errors
or  belief  distortions.  One  recurring  empirical  finding  is  that  these
distortions oscillate between periods of pessimism and optimism and
respond to economic shocks. Fig. 1 (right panel) replicates this finding
for  US  economic  activity  where  we  plot  the  difference  between  the
consensus survey forecast of GDP growth and a model forecast. One
immediate observation is that belief distortions display waves of both
pessimism  and  optimism.  For  example,  while  periods  shortly  before
recessions  are  characterized  by  optimism,  after  the  2008  crisis,  be-
liefs about future economic activity remained quite pessimistic for a
prolonged period.

Time-variation in belief distortions has spawned a large theoretical
literature  based  on  belief-based  models  such  as  diagnostic  expecta-
tions (Bordalo et al., 2020) or fading memory (Nagel and Xu, 2022),
among others. Preference-based models rely on ambiguity aversion (Ilut

and Schneider, 2014) or robust control (Bhandari et al., 2024; Hansen
et  al., 2020).  In  this  paper,  we  contribute  to  the  latter  strand  of
literature and present a novel theoretical framework where agents’ atti-
tude towards alternative models endogenously generates time-varying
pessimism and optimism. Using a calibrated model, we then show that
our model can jointly match salient features of survey data and asset
prices.

The seminal work of Hansen and Sargent (2001) posits that eco-
nomic agents with an aversion to model ambiguity seek robustness by
entertaining a family of models constructed as a neighborhood around a
baseline model and optimize against the worst-case within this family.1

Sargent robustness, Kullback and Leibler (1951) divergence (also known
as relative entropy) is used to measure the discrepancy between models.
To match the time-variation in belief distortions uncovered in the data,
we replace relative entropy by the family of Cressie and Read (1984)
divergences  and  construct  a  discrepancy  measure  which  preserves
recursivity and homotheticity.

We summarize our main theoretical contributions as follows. First,
while relative entropy has strong foundations in information and econo-
metric theory, and often offers tractability for dynamic problems, it
leads to myopic belief distortions in a setting with i.i.d. shocks. How-
ever, belief distortions that are independent of economic shocks and
constant  over  time  are  difficult  to  reconcile  with  the  empirical  pat-
terns  presented  before.  In  our  model,  Cressie-Read  divergences  ren-
der the discrepancy measure state-dependent via a unique sentiment
state variable that summarizes past shocks and belief distortions. As
a result, even in an environment with i.i.d. shocks, subjective beliefs
endogenously respond to changes in the economy.

The Cressie-Read family is parameterized by a parameter 𝜂 and nests
relative entropy as a special case when 𝜂 tends to unity. Intuitively,
the  parameter 𝜂 governs  the  desire  for  intertemporal  smoothness  of
belief  distortions.  Values  of 𝜂 close  to  unity  indicate  a  high  desire
for intertemporal smoothing for belief distortions. As 𝜂 moves away
from unity, the endogenous dynamics of belief distortions depend on
whether 𝜂 is greater or smaller than 1. When 𝜂 < 1, agent’s subjective
beliefs display momentum-like dynamics and become less pessimistic or
more optimistic after positive shocks, leading to procyclical sentiment.
For 𝜂 > 1, agent’s subjective beliefs feature reversal-like dynamics and
turn more pessimistic or less optimistic after positive shocks. Moreover,
the volatility of sentiment also responds to economic shocks and does
so asymmetrically between positive and negative shocks. When 𝜂  <
1,  pessimistic  sentiment  volatility  shrinks  after  positive  shocks  and
increases after negative shocks. The effect is opposite for 𝜂 > 1.

Second, we examine the implications of the Cressie-Read divergence
for portfolio choice and equilibrium asset pricing. We first characterize
the  agent’s  preferences  with  Cressie-Read  divergence  in  the  robust

variance multiplier in the language of stochastic differential utility à
la Duffie and Epstein (1992)). The elasticity of dynamic risk aversion
(the variance multiplier) with respect to the sentiment state variable
is exactly 1 − 𝜂. When 𝜂 < 1, risk aversion is countercyclical, leading
to  a  procyclical  portfolio  weight.  The  opposite  result  holds  for 𝜂  >

Third, we show that our construction of the intertemporal Cressie-
Read  divergence  measure  satisfies  recursivity  and  constitutes  a  re-
cursive  ambiguity  index  in  the  sense  of Maccheroni  et  al. (2006b),
thereby guaranteeing time-consistency of preferences. In addition, we
also preserve homotheticity of the resulting preferences, which benefits
the tractability of the approach. We motivate and derive our defini-
tion of the Cressie-Read divergence measure in a discrete-time setting
and we prove that relative entropy is the unique dynamic divergence
measure that satisfies recursivity. A (unique) stochastic scaling factor
is introduced for our Cressie-Read divergence measure to maintain the
same recursivity. Interestingly, we find that the stochastic scaling factor
that is instrumental in guaranteeing recursivity and time-consistency is
a power function of the sentiment state variable driving the nonlinear
dynamics of the model.

We  then  study  a  general  equilibrium  model  for  a  representative
agent with Epstein and Zin (1989) preferences and Cressie-Read diver-
gence. To this end, we calibrate our model using our empirical measure
of time-varying pessimism and optimism from Fig. 1. Using GDP survey
data from the Survey of Professional Forecasters, we define a measure
of  sentiment  (belief  distortions)  as  the  difference  between  the  one-
quarter ahead consensus forecast and a one-quarter ahead forecast from
a VAR(2). Our approach assumes that survey answers reflect subjective
beliefs.3 The calibrated model features rich dynamics and illustrates

Beyond asset prices, we also test model implications in survey data.
A key testable prediction of the model is that subjective beliefs reflect
the history of the structural shocks affecting the economy. We provide
empirical support for this and we also connect this finding to recent
empirical patterns uncovered in survey data. For example, Kohlhas and
Walther (2021) document that current realizations of output negatively
predict forecast errors, interpreting the negative coefficient as evidence
for overreaction. Using our model, we run regressions of forecast errors
on realizations of GDP and confirm the negative coefficient. The nega-
tive relationship naturally arises in our model due to the procyclicality
of the agent’s subjective beliefs discussed above.

Our  paper  builds  on  the  robust  control  literature  studying  pes-
simistic subjective beliefs such as Hansen and Sargent (2001), Anderson
et al. (2003), and Hansen et al. (2006), among many others. Differ-
ent from this literature, which imposes an entropy penalty, we show
that for values 𝜂 ≠ 1, the Cressie-Read divergence family generates
time-varying beliefs and risk aversion. Moreover, our model calibra-
tion  shows  that  time-varying  pessimism  and  optimism  induces  rich
dynamics in asset prices.

The two papers closest to ours are Hansen et al. (2020) and Bhandari
et al. (2024). The latter study the effects of pessimism and optimism on
macroeconomic fluctuations in the presence of nominal rigidities and
labor market frictions to address the unemployment volatility puzzle.
Our  paper  is  significantly  different  from  their  paper  along  several
dimensions. First, they use entropy-based robust control and allow for
exogenous variation in the strength of the preference for robustness.
We,  in  contrast,  generate  dynamic  sentiment  endogenously.  Second,
they build a macroeconomic model with frictions, while we consider
frictionless  markets  and  focus  on  asset  pricing  implications. Hansen
et al. (2020) construct twisted relative entropy in order to generate
countercyclical concerns for model misspecification and corresponding
risk premia. Our specification based on the Cressie-Read divergence can
be seen as an alternative mechanism where these effects are obtained
endogenously and within the framework of Maccheroni et al. (2006b),
thus satisfying dynamic consistency by construction. Finally, Skiadas
(2013) studies smooth divergence preferences (Maccheroni et al., 2006a)
as an extension of the entropic variational preferences of Hansen and
Sargent (2001) and finds that for small risks (Brownian uncertainty)
the certainty equivalent is approximately equal to an expected-utility
certainty equivalent, concluding that in this case smooth divergence
preferences are approximately equivalent to entropic variational prefer-
ences, which can be viewed as a form of recursive utility with increased
risk aversion. Using a different formulation of the problem, we show
that our preferences cannot be seen as isomorphic to existing (standard
or generalized) recursive preferences. Technically, our preferences are

Chamberlain (2020) offers an excellent survey on empirical methods

A growing literature infers agents’ beliefs from survey data. For ex-
ample, Adam et al. (2021) study different return surveys and reject the
hypothesis that respondents are always pessimistically biased towards
expected returns as predicted by the Hansen and Sargent (2001) model
and argue that they are often optimistic. The authors, however, do find
evidence of unconditional pessimism in forecasts about output growth
and unemployment, in line with our findings. Evidence of pessimism in
macroeconomic survey data is also confirmed in Giordani and Söderlind
(2006) who study output growth forecasts from the Survey of Profes-
sional Forecasters and Bhandari et al. (2024) who rely on the Michigan
Survey of households’ forecasts of future unemployment and inflation.
Similarly, Szőke (2022) estimates a multivariate extension of the model
of Hansen et al. (2020) and shows that the model fits the degree of
implied pessimism in interest rate survey evidence well. Other work
using macro survey data to estimate models with ambiguity aversion
and pessimism include Baqaee (2020) and Bassanin et al. (2021).

Motivated  by  empirical  and  experimental  evidence  on  belief  for-
mation,  a  large  literature  in  behavioral  economics  and  finance  pro-
poses models of deviations from rational expectations. Prominent ex-
amples include diagnostic expectations as, e.g., in Bordalo et al. (2018,
2019), and Bordalo et al. (2020), and extrapolative expectations, see,
e.g., Hong and Stein (1999), Barberis et al. (2015), Barberis (2018),
Li and Liu (2023), and Jin and Sui (2022). Our model endogenously
generates similar belief dynamics, in the sense that belief distortions
are driven by a state variable that summarizes past belief distortions
and fundamental shocks experienced by the decision-maker. The belief
dynamics displays momentum-like features when the Cressie-Read pa-
rameter 𝜂 is less than 1 and contrarian behavior when 𝜂 is larger than 1.
While our specification does not introduce the recency bias documented
in the extrapolative expectation literature, it does introduce asymmet-
ric responses after positive and negative fundamental shocks due to
their different impact on belief volatility. The endogenous response of
belief volatility to fundamental shocks differentiates our model from
diagnostic expectations where the diagnostic distribution has a constant
variance in an AR(1) model, see, e.g., Bordalo et al. (2018).

Our paper is also related to different strands of the literature that
study  time-varying  beliefs  or  risk-aversion  using  alternative  mecha-
nisms.  For example, Bidder and Dew-Becker (2016), Collin-Dufresne
et al. (2016), Dew-Becker and Nathanson (2019), and Kozlowski et al.
(2020),  among  many  others,  generate  rich  belief  dynamics  endoge-
nously in asset pricing models with simple fundamentals when agents
are allowed to learn. Asset pricing studies that feature time-varying risk
aversion include the habit models of Constantinides (1990), Detemple
and Zapatero (1991), and Campbell and Cochrane (1999) where time-
varying  risk  aversion  is  tightly  linked  to  the  level  of  consumption
relative to its recent past history. While in these models time-varying
risk  aversion  is  exogenously  imposed  on  the  utility  function  of  the
representative  agent,  in  our  setting,  stochastic  risk  aversion  arises
endogenously due to the agent’s concern for model ambiguity.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 1 provides the
theoretical framework and studies the robust utility index. Section 2
examines the partial equilibrium portfolio problem. Section 3 develops
a general equilibrium model with an Epstein and Zin (1989) representa-
tive agent with Cressie-Read divergence. Section 4 estimates empirical
proxies of time-varying sentiment that we use in Section 5 for model
calibration. Finally, Section 6 concludes. To save space, we collect all
proofs and further technical details in a separate Appendix.

Another  way  of  understanding  the  intuition  for  our  results  is  to
view  the  Cressie-Read  penalty  function  as  reflecting  a  preference  of
Nature (the fictitious malevolent agent in the max–min expected utility
interpretation) for intertemporal smoothing of the process for the belief
distortions 𝑢∗.  The  closer 𝜂 is  to 1,  the  stronger  the  preference  for
intertemporal smoothing, and the more inelastic the variance multiplier
is with respect to changes in the sentiment state variable. As 𝜂 moves
away from unity, the belief distortions are more dynamic and the sign
of  the  elasticity  of  the  variance  multiplier  depends  on  whether 𝜂 is
greater than or smaller than 1. When 𝜂 < 1, the decision-maker expects
more adverse distortions from Nature in bad times and less adverse
distortions in good times; the agent’s subjective beliefs therefore exhibit
‘‘momentum-like’’ dynamics, and pessimism gets worse in bad times,
while beliefs improve following positive shocks.

In contrast, when 𝜂 > 1, the agent expects less adverse distortions in
bad times and more adverse distortions following positive shocks. The
agent could be seen as thinking that ‘‘lightning never strikes twice’’
after  an  adverse  shock,  i.e.,  if  Nature  has  just  used  its  ammunition
in  hitting  the  agent  with  a  negative  shock,  it  will  not  do  so  again
immediately. Put differently, now the agent’s subjective beliefs exhibit
reversal-like dynamics.

1.3.  A regime-switching model between pessimism and optimism

In the previous section, while the degree of pessimism or optimism
in the agent’s beliefs depends dynamically on 𝑍1−𝜂, the agent is either
optimistic  or  pessimistic.  To  allow  for  both  sentiment  states  to  be
present, we introduce a regime-switching model in which the agent

when the agent feels less pessimistic, she is more likely to switch to the
optimistic state; conversely, when the agent feels less optimistic, she is
more likely to switch back to the pessimistic state. In other words, the
intensities of switching between states also depend on the state variable
𝑍.

dynamic and endogenously driven by a power function of the agent’s
cumulative distorted belief 𝑍, which summarizes the agent’s past belief
distortion and history of fundamental shocks.

We now turn to a long horizon with 𝑇 up to 100 years in order to
study intertemporal hedging and resulting horizon effects. The seminal
work of Merton (1973) explains how non-myopic investors tilt their
portfolio in order to hedge against future changes in the investment
opportunity set. For example, if returns on a risky asset are contempo-
raneously negatively correlated with expected returns on that asset, it
becomes less risky to hold over longer horizons, inducing investors with
longer horizons to increase their holdings. Despite returns being i.i.d.
in our setting, intertemporal hedging appears because of the investor’s
distorted beliefs; even though expected returns are constant under the
reference measure B, they are not constant under U when 𝜂 ≠ 1. In par-
ticular, because of time-varying sentiment, the belief distortion 𝑢 reacts
to return innovations. When the belief distortion is countercyclical (𝜂 <
1), positive return shocks reduce pessimism or strengthen optimism,
which  increase  the  perceived  expected  return  on  the  risky  asset.  In
other  words,  for 𝜂  < 1,  returns  and  expected  returns  under U are
positively correlated, which leads to negative intertemporal hedging
demands that grow with the horizon, see the right two panels of Fig. 3.
Recall  that  for 𝜂  > 1,  positive  return  shocks  strengthen  pessimism
or  reduce  optimism  and  therefore  decrease  the  perceived  expected
return, so we now have a negative correlation between historic returns
and expected return. This explains the positive intertemporal hedging
demand for 𝜂 > 1, which naturally grows with the horizon, see the right
two panels of Fig. 3. We also observe from Fig. 3 that the relationship
between 𝜂 and the sentiment state is asymmetric. When the agent is

4.  Estimating sentiment

We  now  estimate  a  measure  of  time-varying  sentiment  from  the
data. We first measure the difference between agents’ subjective and
objective  beliefs.  To  measure  subjective  beliefs,  we  make  use  of  a
survey on aggregate GDP growth. Objective beliefs are calculated from
a vector autoregression (VAR) from which we infer forecasts of macroe-
conomic variables. The difference between the subjective and objective
beliefs then allows us to back out an empirical proxy for the optimal
distortion in our model. Our data sample runs from January 1990 to
December 2019.27

Objective beliefs: To get a proxy for objective beliefs, we estimate
a VAR with two lags on GDP, inflation, the Fed Funds Rate, and un-
employment. Data is quarterly and available from the Federal Reserve
Economic Data (FRED). Forecasts from the VAR(2) then represent the
objective belief.28

Subjective beliefs: To measure subjective beliefs, we use the Survey
of Professional Forecasters (SPF). SPF data are publicly available pro-
fessional forecasts run by the Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia.
Forecasters provide forecasts for different horizons and we focus on
the one-quarter ahead forecast. We define the subjective belief as the
median (consensus) one-quarter ahead forecast.

Finally,  the  wedge  is  defined  as  the  difference  between  the  sub-
jective and objective beliefs about future GDP growth, measuring the
amount of pessimism or optimism in the overall economy. Fig. 1 plots
realized output growth, subjective, objective output growth, and the
resulting wedge. The figure reveals that before economic recessions,

5.2.  Belief wedges and asset prices

We can now study model-implied asset prices. Panel B of Table 4
reports the unconditional equilibrium asset pricing results produced by
the calibrated model, which is disciplined by the first three moments
of belief wedges and the proportion of each sentiment state, tabulated
in  Panel  A.  The  calibrated  model  matches  these  properties  of  the
belief wedge almost perfectly, except for the third moment, where the
calibrated model generates considerably more negative skewness, due
to  the  large  magnitude  of  the  negative  structural  shock  during  the
2007–2008 global financial crisis. We also report the 𝐴𝑅(1) coefficient
of wedge dynamics. Our model produces an 𝐴𝑅(1) coefficient of 0.84,
in between the empirical counterpart and the unit 𝐴𝑅(1) coefficient of
the entropy case.

The equilibrium asset pricing results are primarily driven by pes-
simism in the calibrated model, because the data shows that wedges are
mostly negative. The model performs well in generating a sizable risk
premium and realistic Sharpe ratio, although both fall short of the high
empirical values observed in our sample period. The quantitative suc-
cess in producing excess volatility is more limited. Higher equilibrium
volatility could be obtained in the model by injecting some stochastic
volatility  in  the  consumption  process,  following  the  insights  of  the
long-run risk literature (Bansal and Yaron, 2004 and others). Instead
we choose to fully isolate the quantitative effect of the Cressie-Read
mechanism, in particular in terms of its ability to generate interesting
dynamics despite the deliberately stylized nature of the i.i.d. Gaussian
fundamentals, which are by design void of any dynamics.

To assess the impact of Cressie-Read relative to entropy for uncon-
ditional asset pricing, we set 𝜂 =  1, 𝑞𝑂 =  0.02, and 𝑞𝑃 =  0.014, in
order to match the percentage of pessimistic periods, and use otherwise
the same parameters. We see that our Cressie-Read extension is critical
for matching the empirically observed moments of the belief wedges,
as well as for explaining asset pricing stylized facts. When 𝜂 = 1, the
belief wedge is constant in a given regime. The dynamics are therefore
solely driven by regime switching between pessimism and optimism.
This is reflected in the very poor performance of the entropy case when
it comes to matching the second and third moment of belief wedges in
Table 4. In the case of relative entropy the model also fails to address
the equity premium puzzle or the risk-free rate puzzle: we find a much
higher risk-free rate of 4.16%, a very low equity risk premium of 1.34%
percent, in addition to a constant return volatility equal to the volatility
of dividends, and a low Sharpe Ratio of 0.1. We therefore conclude by
noting that our Cressie-Read extension improves substantially on the

Importantly, the gap between the objective and the subjective risk pre-

the mean of the state variable the state space, only reaching 2% in the

(2022) and Chamberlain (2020) both point out, a central idea in robust
Bayesian analysis based on classical work of Good (1952) is to judge
the plausibility of a min–max model by examining how reasonable the
subjective belief U is that is supporting the equilibrium. It seems fair
to conclude that even the most extreme pessimism in our calibration
passes Good’s plausibility criterion. Finally, in the pessimistic regime,
the jump risk premium is an order of magnitude smaller than the total
risk premium, indicating that most of the risk premium comes from
state-dependence induced by our Cressie-Read mechanism of dynamic
sentiment, not from regime switching. When optimistic, the jump risk
premium is more important and makes up most of the objective risk
premium, which is not surprising because it reflects the risk of jumping
from optimism to pessimism.

6.  Conclusions

Our  paper  makes  the  following  contributions.  First,  we  propose
a  new  model  of  dynamic  sentiment.  In  the  model,  beliefs  exhibit
episodes  of  pessimism  and  optimism  and  are  driven  by  past  belief
distortions and fundamental shocks. These dynamic patterns arise en-
dogenously  as  agents  fear  misspecification  of  the  benchmark  model
and seek robustness by considering alternative models. We generalize
existing approaches to robustness in two ways. First, we replace the
entropy criterion that is ubiquitous in the literature on robust control
by  the  family  of  Cressie-Read  divergences.  Our  Cressie-Read  diver-
gence measure is recursive and homothetic, leading to time-consistent
preferences and tractable decision problems. This extension opens the
door to endogenous and state-dependent dynamics of belief distortions,
governed by the Cressie-Read parameter 𝜂. In particular, the dynamics
of sentiment can be procyclical or countercyclical depending on the
sign of 1 −𝜂. Sentiment is driven by past fundamental shocks, as well as
by past belief distortions, thereby generating the time-varying nature of
sentiment observed empirically. Second, our model allows for optimism
in addition to pessimism, and features a regime-switching mechanism
where the likelihood of switching depends also on the sentiment state
variable.

As a second contribution, we apply our model to understand port-
folio choice and general equilibrium asset pricing. We highlight the
ability  of  the  model  to  generate  rich  dynamics  and  to  explain  em-
pirically relevant phenomena by deliberately assuming i.i.d. Gaussian
fundamentals,  in  which  case  entropy  produces  constant  portfolios,
risk premia, interest rates, and volatility. In our model, endogenous
sentiment  gives  rise  to  stochastic  effective  risk  aversion.  When  the
Cressie-Read parameter 𝜂 is smaller than one, effective risk aversion
is countercyclical, while the opposite happens whenever 𝜂 > 1. In our
portfolio  problem,  this  induces  intertemporal  hedging  and  therefore
both  horizon-  and  state-dependent  portfolios,  despite  returns  being
i.i.d. We calibrate our general equilibrium asset pricing model using
estimates of belief distortions obtained from survey data of expectations
by professional forecasters about future economic activity. We provide

19 

Journal of Financial Economics 163 (2025) 103953 

empirical support for key testable implications of the model regard-
ing  the  relationships  between  fundamental  shocks  to  the  economy,
belief wedge dynamics, and forecast errors, as well as conditional asset
pricing.

A.3.  Motivating Cressie-Read as an alternative for KL entropy

In absence of an axiomatic justification for the use of the Cressie-
Read  divergence,  we  provide  a  short  discussion  of  its  use  in  other
disciplines  and  areas  of  economics,  as  well  as  its  specific  role  in
extending the classical KL relative entropy divergence in our set-up.

KL relative entropy is very prominent in many areas of economics,
decision theory, statistics, econometrics and of course information the-
ory. It is also central to Hansen and Sargent’s work on robust control,
where  relative  entropy  penalizes  alternative  models  that  can  easily
be  told  apart  from  the  benchmark  model  based  on  their  likelihood
ratio. Strzalecki (2011) provides a formal axiomatic characterization
of Hansen–Sargent entropy-based multiplier preferences, as being the
unique preferences that satisfy the axioms of Maccheroni et al. (2006a)
plus Savage’s Sure Thing Principle.

Our set-up deviates in two ways from Hansen–Sargent multiplier
preferences, and therefore from Strzalecki’s axiomatization: we general-
ize KL entropy to Cressie-Read divergence, and we scale the robustness
preference parameter to guarantee homotheticity. This means that our
set-up does not satisfy Savage’s Sure Thing Principle. Several papers in
decision theory advocate omitting Savage’s Sure Thing Principle, see
e.g. Gilboa (1987) and Gilboa et al. (2009).

P.J. Maenhout et al.

Journal of Financial Economics 163 (2025) 103953 

Turning to the crucial question of why we propose the Cressie-Read
divergence instead of KL relative entropy, it is useful to remember that
Cressie-Read nests KL entropy as it is a one-parameter power extension
of KL’s log likelihood criterion. As such, Cressie-Read divergence is also
widely used in other areas. In statistics and econometrics, Generalized
Empirical Likelihood estimators have been studied as alternatives to
two-step  GMM  estimation  when  facing  a  moment  restriction  model.
Duality theory provides an alternative formulation of GEL estimators
as  Minimum  Discrepancy  estimators. Newey  and  Smith (2004)  use
Cressie-Read in this context and show how well-known estimators are
retrieved for specific values of the Cressie-Read parameter 𝜂: Empirical
Likelihood  corresponds  to 𝜂 =  0,  the  Hellinger  distance  estimator
to 𝜂 =  0.5 and  Exponential  Tilting  to 𝜂 =  1,  i.e.  KL  entropy.  Im-
portantly, Kitamura  et  al. (2013)  show  that  the  Hellinger  distance
estimator  has  the  best  asymptotic  robustness  property,  in  the  sense
of being most stable when there is a small perturbation to the data
generating process. While there is clearly no connection between this
result  and  our  specific  use  of 𝜂 =  0.5 (Hellinger),  we  do  note  that
econometric theory finds it useful to venture beyond KL entropy and
that the same Cressie-Read discrepancy measure that we use is in fact
crucial  to  retrieve  many  well-known  estimators  beyond  Exponential
Tilting (the special case of 𝜂 =  1, i.e. KL). It is also worth pointing
out  that  different  strands  of  the  literature  in  econometrics  embrace
different notions of robustness, and as a consequence, obtain different
assessments of the performance of the alternative estimators. As also
acknowledged in Kitamura et al. (2013) and Schennach (2007) (see
also Chen et al. (2024) and footnote 5) for example argues that 𝜂  <
1 has  undesirable  properties  when  handling  model  misspecification
rather than data perturbation.

In statistics, the literature on robust estimation of parametric models
also commonly uses Hellinger distance (see Beran (1977) and Bickel
(1981) among others).

Crucially,  all  these  applications  of  Cressie-Read  deal  with  static
problems, while our set-up and our application of Cressie-Read is fun-
damentally dynamic. More precisely, in the same way that CRRA utility
nests and generalizes log utility, Cressie-Read nests and generalizes KL
in a dynamic setting. It is well known that CRRA utility captures both
smoothing across states and over time: risk aversion, defined in a static
setting with risk, and willingness to substitute intertemporally, defined
in a deterministic dynamic setting without risk, are both controlled by
the same power parameter. Cressie-Read can be seen in the exact same
way: there is the ‘risk’ interpretation, which is atemporal and about
choices across states, and there is the intertemporal interpretation. It
is the latter that plays a key role in our setting, as it governs how the
minimizing agent wants to allocate distortions intertemporally. KL cor-
responds to the myopic case where the minimizing agent does not care
about the intertemporal allocation, so that an IID environment produces
constant belief distortions. Cressie-Read allows us to go beyond this, as
we show in the paper.
