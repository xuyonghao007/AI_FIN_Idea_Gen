A nationwide panic forced President Roosevelt to declare a banking holiday in March 1933. The government
reopened banks sequentially using a process that sent noisy signals about banks’ health. New microdata reveals
that the public responded to these signals. Deposits at rapidly reopened banks rebounded quicker than at
comparable or stronger banks that reopened even a few days later. The stigma of late reopening shifted funds
from stigmatized to lauded banks and among communities that they served. Despite persisting over a decade, the
shift had no measurable impact on the rate at which localities recovered from the Great Depression.

“A question you will ask is this—why are all the banks not to be
reopened at the same time? The answer is simple. Your government does
not intend that the history of the past few years shall be repeated….We
do not want and will not have another epidemic of bank failures.… Let
me make it clear to you that if your bank does not open the first day you
are by no means justified in believing that it will not open. A bank that
opens on one of the subsequent days is in exactly the same status as the
bank that opens tomorrow.”

Franklin Roosevelt’s Fireside Chat “On the Banking Crisis”, March

12, 1933.

1. Introduction

During systemic banking panics, policymakers seeking to stem the
stampede must convince investors that their funds are safe (Gorton and
Tallman, 2018). This task often involves across-the-board, treat-all-de-
positors-and-firms equally interventions. Following the failure of Leh-
man Brothers in 2008, the government rushed to guarantee liabilities at
many financial firms in afflicted sectors, even those which had hitherto
been ineligible for assistance, such as money market mutual funds.
Critics - including Chang and Velasco (2000), Lacker (2008), Farhi and
Tirole (2012), Chari and Kehoe (2016), and Keister (2016) - assert that
unconditional assistance can be pernicious because expectations of
bailouts, particularly those not conditioned on pre-panic behaviors,

Regulators’ fears of “bank stigma,” where banks receiving govern-
ment assistance lose the confidence of market participants, are distinct
from the more widely researched concept of “facility stigma,” where
financial institutions become reluctant to seek assistance from govern-
ment programs that might publicize their need for assistance. The
concept of bank stigma justifies policymakers’ sweeping programs to
counteract panics, although little empirical evidence underlies their
beliefs that signals sent by triaging applicants for aid and targeting
assistance to institutions in need through no fault of their own produce
substantial, pernicious stigmas. Evidence is limited because potentially
stigma inducing interventions during systemic banking crises have not
been observed in OECD economies for nearly a century. Systemic crises
seldom occur, and policymakers fear stigma to such an extent that rescue
packages have been designed to prevent it from arising. Policymakers’
suppositions about stigma, in other words, lead them to implement
policies that prevent researchers from testing those suppositions.

To shed light on this debate, we analyze the largest and most recent
federal intervention during a systemic banking crisis that could have
sent signals about the health of banks, the Bank Holiday of March 1933.
At the nadir of the Great Depression, the U.S. government ordered all
depositories in the nation to cease operations and then over time
authorized individual banks to reopen. Officials worried that their plan
of action - which sequenced reopenings based upon imperfect infor-
mation about banks’ health as well as characteristics of their commu-
nities - might stigmatize banks because their reopening dates were
readily observed and expected to be predicated on their health. Our
essay ascertains whether sequential reopening signaled banks’ health,
the accuracy of those signals, and the impact of bank stigma on the
broader recovery.

Characteristics of the Bank Holiday facilitate identification of links
between regulatory intervention, bank stigma, and economic activity.
First, the Holiday was mandatory and the signal sent by regulators - the
date of reopening - was salient to depositors and observed by all market
participants. All banks closed on the same date and reopened when
regulators told them to reopen, eliminating any selection into treatment
or observation of
treatment. Second, before the initial waves of
reopenings, regulators did not provide financial aid to banks or intro-
duce novel financing facilities. Banks had the same balance sheets as the
day they were closed and had access to the same federal lending pro-
grams that they had access to before the Holiday began. Depositors,
likewise, had the same deposits, and borrowers had the same loans.
Third, regulators’ decisions about when to reopen banks were based on
public and private information about the health of banks as well as other
political, social, and economic goals set by federal and state authorities.

Aspects of the historical era also pose challenges for our analysis.
Data available on banks, for example, is much more detailed today than
in the past, when data from banks typically specified a few categories of
assets and liabilities and when information concerning bank manage-
ment quality and loan losses was neither widely nor systematically
available. Data about the economy are also more widely available today
than in the past. In the 1930s, for example, national income and product
accounts had yet to be created, and little data on economic activity at the
state and county level existed except that provided by periodic censuses
of populations and businesses. The limited information available en-
hances the credibility of our argument, since stigmas are more likely to
arise in limited-information environments, but may limit its external
validity, an issue that we will return to in the conclusion. Limited in-
formation also complicates our empirical analysis, since some factors
that scholars might control for when analyzing modern data - like loan
losses, management quality, or local unemployment rates - cannot be
considered because the data were not collected in the past. Limited in-
formation prevents us from completely ruling out some alternative hy-
potheses concerning factors that could have influenced the post-Holiday
rebound in deposits but which we (as well as regulators and economic
agents in the 1930s) do not observe.

Our argument proceeds in four steps. Sections 2 and 3 set the stage.
Section 2 elaborates on the characteristics of the Holiday and the
structure of the banking system that facilitate inference in our analysis.
These characteristics coincide with the prerequisites of information
revelation in the game-theoretic literature on signaling and stigma.3
Section 3 describes the novel nationwide micro-database that we collect,
collate, and digitize to enable our analysis and discusses patterns
apparent in the data.

The second step of the argument, appearing in Section 4, demon-
strates that regulators’ decisions during the Holiday were equivalent to
standard game-theoretic models of signals and stigmas, where an
informed party (regulators) makes an observable choice (reopening
date) based upon private information (bank health). We regress
reopening dates on information from bank balance sheets as well as an
array of regulatory, demographic, geographic, and political variables.
This exercise confirms the essential assumption of the models: the cor-
relation between information held by and the action chosen by the
signalers (regulators). We show reopening dates were correlated with
bank health. Banks with more resources, larger liquidity reserves, and
bigger capital buffers on average reopened sooner than banks with fewer
resources, less liquidity, and less capital. This result, therefore, dem-
onstrates that rapid reopening signaled banks’ health to market partic-
ipants. These regressions, however, also reveal the imperfection of the
signal. Many weak banks reopened early, while many strong banks
reopened late.4

predictions of signaling models, that less informed agents (depositors)
respond to the information that they receive and that stronger signals
generate stronger responses. We identify stigma’s impact by comparing
changes in deposits between the summers of 1932 and 1933 in banks
with similar health that reopened at different dates. In 1932, on average,
banks had more deposits; in 1933, on average, banks had less. We find
banks that reopened earlier had more deposits after controlling for this
downward trend than banks that reopened later. This is true even when
comparing banks that were reopened only several business days apart.
Early reopening was a strong signal that overrode other observable in-
formation about banks’ strength. In essence, depositors treated all banks
that reopened rapidly as if they were healthy banks, regardless of in-
formation available about their balance sheets. Banks that reopened
later received fewer deposits, but depositors responded more strongly to
information about their health. The impact of these signals lasted for
nearly a decade, as the differences between early and late reopeners
remained through 1940.

Building on the bank-level results, we show that deposits increased in
counties whose banks on average opened early relative to neighboring
counties with similar characteristics whose banks on average reopened
later. This analysis confirms a principal fear of policymakers today and
in the past: interventions during panics that differentiate banks based on
pre-panic behavior and characteristics - particularly those that clearly
distinguish between stronger and weaker banks - can redistribute funds
through the financial system.

Since policymakers today worry that deposit shifts could inhibit
economic recovery, the fourth step of our argument, appearing in Sec-
tion 6, examines whether stigma-induced shifts in deposits across
counties inhibited or accelerated economic recovery at the county level.
A wide range of empirical and theoretical studies (e.g., King and Levine
1993, Jayaratne and Strahan 1996, Cetorelli and Gambera 2001, Beck
and Levine 2004, Beck et al. 2010, Chodorow-Reich 2014, Gilje et al.
2016, Mian et al. 2020, Baron et al. 2021, Cohen et al. 2021) indicate
that reducing funds available through financial institutions can reduce
economic growth. We find, perhaps surprisingly, no statistically signif-
icant relationship between counties where banks reopened early (and
whose share of aggregate deposits increased as a result) and the pace of
economic recovery. Commerce and industry appear to have recovered
on average no quicker in counties whose banks reopened earlier than in
comparable counties whose banks reopened later or remained closed.
This surprising result has several potential explanations. One is federal
government programs that loaned funds directly to businesses. Another
is our finding that banks reopening early during the Holiday did not loan
more funds than banks that reopened later; the extra deposits that they
received, in other words, did not result in extra lending.

Our statistical analysis, therefore, yields novel conclusions about the
Bank Holiday of 1933 and rescues of banking systems in general. During
the Bank Holiday, depositors responded to policymakers’ actions that
signaled banks’ health. While we cannot track specific depositors, the
stigma of late reopening seems to have shifted financial resources across
banks and communities. It had, however, much less of an effect on the
rest of the economy. The historical record, in other words, provides little
support
for across-the-board in-
terventions during recent financial crises. While targeted treatments of
banks in distress did stigmatize institutions revealed to be weaker, these
shifts - which have the benefit of limiting moral hazard in the long run -
do not appear to have come at a cost of reduced commercial or industrial
activity.

Our paper sheds light on the literature focused on how financial
panics can be ended. Studies have examined tools available for stopping
runs (e.g., Calomiris et al. 2004, Cecchetti et al. 2009, Boissay et al.
2020), but as Gorton and Tallman (2018) argue, these tools often cannot
quickly change opinions of depositors, especially amidst a crisis. For
instance, these actions did not stop runs from occurring during the GFC.
Further, such government intervention has been shown to introduce

3 

moral hazard (e.g., Acharya and Yorulmazer 2007, Diamond and Rajan
2012). Similar to currency depots in the 1920s (e.g., Carlson et al. 2011),
the Bank Holiday was targeted at the confidence of depositors rather
than propping up the losses of banks. By mitigating the asymmetric in-
formation problem, the Holiday put the nation on solid financial footing
allowing funds to flow back to banks without the typical drawbacks of
government intervention.

Along the same lines, our study of the Bank Holiday relates to policy
discussions about Supervisory Capital Assessment Program of 2009 (a.k.
a. the GFC stress tests). Geithner (2014, p. 286) states that the stress
tests: “aimed to impose transparency on opaque financial institutions
and their opaque assets in order to reduce the uncertainty that was
driving the panic. It would help markets distinguish between viable
banks that were temporarily illiquid and weak banks that were essen-
tially insolvent.” He credits stress tests with reassuring the public and
encouraging them to return funds to financial institutions. Subsequent
research confirms his claims (e.g., Petrella and Resti 2013) and dem-
onstrates that public release of some information from stress tests could
be optimal (e.g., Leitner and Williams 2023). Even then, and the fact that
they came 8 months after the collapse of Lehman, the stress tests reports
were very cautious to disparage any of the banks or to imply that any
were at risk. Our analysis contributes to this stream of research by
demonstrating the benefits of the quick release of concise information
for all banks, rather than information derived from costly and
time-consuming stress tests of large institutions. We also demonstrate
that positive responses to reports from stress tests are likely to be a
universal phenomenon and not merely a feature of the nineteen finan-
cial institutions stress tested in the United States in the spring of 2009.
The transparency of the Bank Holiday’s reopening decisions provides
a new perspective on the stigma literature. Given the explicit avoidance
of bank stigma by policymakers, the modern literature has generally
focused on facility stigma. For instance, it has been discussed with re-
gard to the discount window (Peristani 1998, Furfine 2001, Afonso et al.
2011, Armantier et al. 2015, Ennis 2019) and the Primary Dealer Credit
Facility (Copeland et al. 2014, Krishnamurthy et al. 2014). The smaller
bank stigma literature has studied emergency liquidity programs and the
scrutiny that banks faced if they accepted emergency assistance (Anbil
2018, Vossmeyer 2019, Anbil and Vossmeyer 2021). Our results show
that the public perceived the date of reopening as a signal of bank
health. The public treated banks that reopened later like banks that
received emergency assistance. The Holiday presents an important dif-
ference, however, because the closures were mandatory, removing any
selection complications due to a bank’s participation decision and
allowing us to examine stigma purely from regulators’ signals.

The rapid recovery of banks and the aggregate economy immediately
following the Bank Holiday corroborates key conclusions of the theo-
retical literature on the optimal design of government programs for
recapitalization and bailouts of banking systems. Philippon and Schnabl
(2013) find that efficient recapitalization programs “eliminate both
free-riding and opportunistic participation” by banks. Philippon and
Wang (2023) find that governments can alleviate the moral hazard
induced by ex-post efficient bailouts by liquidating the least healthy
banks. Banks’ desire to avoid being the last in the pack induces tour-
nament dynamics that push banks towards the optimal level of risk. The
Roosevelt Administration recognized these issues and overcame
free-riding, opportunism, and moral hazard by requiring participation
by all banks and triaging financial institutions. Illiquid banks reopened.
Insolvent banks did not. Compulsory participation also overcame the
sorting that occurred during Global Financial Crisis when banks of
different types approached emergency lending facilities at different
rates (Drechsler et al., 2016).

The paper also helps us better understand the path of the Great
Depression. Authors have pointed to the role that deposit insurance (e.
g., Freidman and Schwartz 1963), changing inflation expectations (e.g.,
Hausman et al. 2019), and gold restrictions (e.g., Romer 1992) had on
the economic rebound, but this study shows that the Bank Holiday

helped to put the nation back on a firm financial footing before these
events occurred. Based on aggregate deposit information, the reopening
of banks was met with public confidence and a wave of deposits back

Finally, we provide evidence for the arguments of Park (1991),
Conti-Brown and Vanatta (2021), and other scholars (Sargent, 1982;
Eggertsson, 2008) who discuss the importance of confidence and ex-
pectations in the recovery from the Depression. Our research provides
evidence supporting Silber’s (2009) conclusions concerning the Bank
Holiday. Silber argued the Holiday resuscitated banking and financial
markets because the public believed Roosevelt’s promise in the Fireside
chat that all banks would be safe and because the public understood that
the Fed’s commitment - in part due to expanded powers the Fed received
in the Emergency Banking Act - to provide liquidity to financial in-
stitutions established de facto deposit insurance. We provide statistical
evidence of Silber’s supposition that the Holiday restored depositors’
confidence and that depositors generally accepted Roosevelt’s
banks-would-be-safe message, but we show that depositors did not
believe all of Roosevelt’s claims. Depositors did not believe that banks
which reopened tomorrow were just as healthy as banks that reopened
today. Rather than behaving as if they were agents responding to the
introduction of deposit insurance in Diamond and Dybvig (1983) model,
as in Silber’s analogy, we find that depositors behaved as if they were
agents in Martin’s (2006) model [which is a mathematical reformulation
of Bagehot (1873)], where loans by a lender of last resort eliminate runs
on solvent banks while retaining depositors’ desire to entrust their
savings to healthy intermediaries.

2. Historical background

This section sets out the historical information underlying our
analysis. First, it describes the panic of 1933 and the government’s
response. Second, it discusses the institutional setting and why stigma
could have arisen during that crisis. Third, it explores basic patterns in
the data.

The banking panic in February and March 1933 was the most severe
in U.S. history. In a few weeks, the nationwide run drained over a
quarter of all deposits from commercial banks (see Fig. 1). The crisis
forced banks, savings institutions, investment firms, stock markets, and
other financial firms to curtail operations, and it compelled local, state,
and the federal government to take aggressive actions, including state-
specific banking holidays. The crisis culminated on Friday, March 3,
1933, when the Federal Reserve Bank of New York’s reserves fell to their
legal limit, and it indicated that it could not open for business the next
morning. To forestall the Fed’s suspension, Roosevelt declared a
nationwide Bank Holiday beginning Monday, March 6.

The government acted rapidly to reopen the financial system. On
March 9, Congress passed the Emergency Banking Act, which empow-
ered the Secretary of the Treasury to issue licenses that allowed banks to
reopen and strengthened federal regulation of banks. Reopening de-
cisions were decentralized based on the regulator in charge of the bank
along with help from the Treasury and the Reconstruction Finance
Corporation (RFC). The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC)
and Federal Reserve were responsible for national banks and state Fed
member banks. Each state’s banking authority was responsible for their
state non-member banks. Regulators received general guidance about
how to choose banks to reopen, but no specific instructions. Officials
reopened banks that they deemed to be (i) solvent and (ii) sufficiently
liquid to satisfy reasonable withdrawal requests. Officials chose based
upon their knowledge of each bank’s financial status, standards of
practice then in use to identify unhealthy banks, and rules of thumb
rapidly devised to help them sort through the thousands of decisions that
they needed to make to get the financial system up and running in a few
days. The initial decisions were based upon existing information from

The most detailed description of the reopening process comes from
Awalt (1969) who was acting Comptroller of the Currency between
September 1932 and May 1933. He oversaw the national banking sys-
tem, witnessed the panic, and was a key director of the government’s
response. Awalt indicates that work to identify which banks should
reopen began on March 8. Authorities understood that speed was
important, and decisions would be imperfect. Some weak banks would
reopen swiftly, and some strong banks would remain closed for a pro-
longed period. Jesse Jones, head of the RFC, noted that banks slipped
through the cracks: “In those feverish days and nights, it was difficult to
decide whether a bank was truly sound…Mistakes were inevitable. A
great many unsound banks were allowed to resume business” (Jones and
Angly, 1951, p. 21).

Some of these “mistakes” were the result of conflicting goals. For
instance, Awalt indicated that regulators were concerned about the
geographic coverage of reopened banks. The nation needed banks to
facilitate payments and prevent business disruptions. “It was necessary
that, as far as possible, banks be opened to afford every community with
some banking service” (Awalt, 1969, p. 363). One of Awalt’s early steps
was to plot on a map all the banks that the OCC thought could be
reopened without additional liquidity. The initial distribution was too
sparse in some areas, which led Awalt to recommend looser criteria in
certain regions.

Policymakers understood that their actions might signal the health of
commercial banks to the public and worried about the consequences of
such inferences. On March 12, in his first Fireside Chat, Roosevelt
communicated the reopening plan directly to the public and addressed
the issue of stigma head on. He explained that most banks would be
solvent if the public once again trusted them with their funds. In his
view, Americans had been “stampeded by rumors or guesses” and a
prolonged reopening process was necessary to “enable the Government
to make common sense checkups.” He promised that only sound banks
would reopen, and they would be supported by the government. While
some banks would open before others, the President emphasized that a
“bank that opens on one of the subsequent days is in exactly the same
status as the bank that opens tomorrow.” Roosevelt’s fireside comments
and memoirs of principals such as Awalt (1969) and Jones and Angly
(1951) make it clear that regulators were concerned about stigma.

Policymakers at the time had a professional understanding of stigma
that social scientists have since formalized using game theory models.
The theoretical conditions underlying signaling games clearly existed in
the 1930s. Some agents (regulators) had more information about bank
health than others (depositors and the public). Regulators examined
banks periodically and had access to their internal data, audit reports,
and senior personnel as well as a vast array of information collected by
government agencies and private companies. Federal Reserve officials
had information on member banks’ daily reserve balances, discount
borrowing, and the quantity and quality of members’ collateral pre-
sented at the discount window. Regulators also had specialized training
and on-the-job experience in how to interpret this information. Alter-
natively, the public had access to much less information. Data services
and government bureaus published bank balance-sheet data at low
frequency.

Regulators (the informed parties) took an action based on their pri-
vate information. This action - reopening a bank on a certain date - was
readily observable, and its link to private information was public
knowledge. So, depositors and the public (the less informed parties)
could infer the information that regulators possessed but the public did
not. These signals and stigmas could have a lasting effect because the
public received little information about banks and had limited ability to
update their beliefs. Information revelation, however in this case, could
be imperfect since regulators’ actions were not a one-to-one trans-
formation of their private information about bank health. The heads of
the OCC and RFC both indicated that some banks opened earlier than

warranted by their health to satisfy policy concerns other than financial
stability and that “mistakes” were made. Some weak banks reopened
early. Some strong banks reopened late. In this case, signals about some
banks’ health could be incorrect.

In this environment, the government’s efforts to reboot the banking
system progressed rapidly. Table 1 shows that 81% of Fed member
banks and 64% of state non-member banks were allowed to reopen on an
unrestricted basis by the end of March. Another 1000 banks were fully
licensed to reopen by the end of June. The table also makes clear that
there was a middle ground for state banks not mentioned by Roosevelt:
restricted reopening. While national banks that had not reopened by
March 16 were placed in the hands of conservators, many states passed
laws that allowed their state-chartered banks to reopen subject to re-
strictions on the withdrawal of existing deposits and provision of loans.
In most cases, restricted banks could still receive new deposits that were
not subject to withdrawal limits. Several states used the approach
extensively, while others did not utilize it at all. Table 1 shows that
almost 2200 state banks were allowed to reopen under restriction during
March, but only about half of all states permitted reopenings with re-
strictions. When the dust settled, around 4000 banks either closed
permanently or had to be substantially recapitalized before reopening.
Reopenings that did occur were met with a strong positive public
response. Deposits swiftly flowed back into the banking system. Between
March 13 and 15, $370 million in gold coin and gold certificates were
deposited in the banking system, which was more than all that had been
withdrawn during 1933. In the second half of March, another $260
million was returned (Federal Reserve Board, 1934, p. 15). The stock
market also soared. The Dow Jones Industrial Average increased 15.3
percent between March 3 (the close of the last trading day before the
Bank Holiday) and March 15 (the close of the first day the New York
Stock Exchange resumed trading), and nearly doubled by the end of
June (Silber, 2009). The deposit rebound continued through the spring,
with deposits at weekly-reporting member banks increasing 22% in four
months from March 8 to July 5, 1933 (see Fig. 1).

Banks that survived the winnowing during the Holiday changed their
behavior. The Federal Reserve Board (1933, p. 209) reported that
member banks reduced borrowing at the discount window by $1 billion
between March 4 and April 5. Bankers acted as if they expected de-
positors to refrain from running on banks and business prospects to
improve.

This rebound in deposits was unlikely to have been the result of the
creation of federal deposit insurance. The rebound began nine months
before federal deposit insurance coverage came into force (on January 1,
1934), three months before the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation
(FDIC) was created by the Banking Act of 1933 (commonly called the
Glass-Steagall Act of June 16, 1933), and two months before the pro-
posal to create the FDIC seemed likely to overcome the opposition of
large banks, many senators and representatives, and Roosevelt himself.
At a press conference on March 8, 1933, when asked about guaranteeing
bank deposits, Roosevelt stated “Any form of general guarantee means a
definite loss to the Government…We do not wish to make the United
States Government liable for the mistakes and errors of individual banks
and put a premium on unsound banking in the future.” Roosevelt did not

4. Statistical evidence of signals

The initial step of our analysis examines key predictions of signaling
models. We ask why regulators reopened some banks rapidly and not
others. This step is necessary to determine what information the public
might have gleaned from the rate of reopening. We will show that the
reopening was strongly correlated with balance sheet measures about
banks’ health in the hands of regulators but the signals also contained
noise, since regulators reopened some unhealthy banks rapidly while
some healthy banks remained closed. The noise arose because regulators
had limited time and information to determine which banks should
reopen and may have been influenced by political factors and policy
objectives other than bank health.

To uncover common patterns underlying regulators’ decisions, we
employ an iterative statistical approach. Initially, we examine individual
factors related to the bank itself. These include measures of potential risk
based on balance sheet position in 1932: the logarithm of total assets,
loans to assets, paid-in capital, surplus, and undivided profits to assets (i.
e., capital to assets), and cash, exchanges, and due from banks to total
deposits (i.e., cash to deposits). These measures are similar to those
found to be correlated with the probability of bank failure in academic
papers examining similar eras such as the 1920s agricultural depression
(Jaremski and Wheelock, 2020), and Great Depression (White, 1984;
Richardson and Troost, 2009). They are also similar to measures that
regulators use to determine the health of bank balance sheets during
modern stress tests. We also calculate the changes in the logarithms of
deposits and loans from 1929 to 1932 to capture the pre-trends of banks
going into the Holiday. Depositors and regulators were certainly able to
see bank runs and loan losses across the broader span of time. Finally, we
include the logarithm of the bank’s age as well as indicators for whether
the bank was a national bank or a state Fed member bank (with state
non-member banks as the excluded group) and whether it operated any
branches outside of the city of its headquarters.

Second, we include basic demographic control variables on the
county itself. These variables include: the logarithm of population in
1929, fraction of the county living in an urban location of 2500 or more
people in 1929, the fraction of the adult population that is illiterate in
1929, the fraction of the population that is non-white in 1929, the
fraction of owner-operator farms that had mortgage debt in 1929, in-
dicators for whether the bank was located in a city that was designated a
Central Reserve City, Reserve City or had a clearinghouse in 1932, and
the logarithm of the number of banks in the county in 1932. The vari-
ables account for regional variation that could influence bank operations
but were unlikely to influence regulators directly. We, therefore, include
them in the models but do not report their statistics to save space. We
also include information on economic and political factors of each
county. These include: the logarithm of the number of farms per capita,
the logarithm of the number of manufacturing establishments per cap-
ita, and the fractions of votes for the Democrat candidates in the House
of Representatives elections of 1930 and the Presidential election of
1932.

Third, we add information on the rest of the banking community
within the bank’s town. As highlighted by Awalt, geographic proximity
was important to keep the payments system afloat. A bank’s relative
position rather than the exact value of its position thus might have been
important when there were few options available. In addition to a
dummy variable for whether the bank was the only one in the town, we
control for the fraction of those other banks that were national banks,
the fraction of those other banks that were state member banks, the
logarithm of average assets in other banks, the average ratios of loans to
assets, capital to assets, and cash to deposits in other banks in the town.
For brevity, we suppress several of these variables from our output ta-
bles but they are available upon request.

Fourth, we control for the state in which the bank was located. The
narrative evidence indicated that various regulators had different risk
preferences and Mitchener (2005, 2007) demonstrates the importance
of different regulatory regimes in each state. Fig. 2 has also shown that
certain geographic areas had a much lower proportion of reopened
banks. We include state-fixed effects to strip out any differences across
states and focus only on variation across banks within the same state.9

Appendix A1 on Robustness addresses several threats to inference
including regulators’ incentives and limited information available to
depositors. Foremost amongst these is the potential for missing infor-
mation. If unobservable characteristics like management quality or
banking services determined the fate of banks, this information’s
exclusion from our regressions might bias our estimates. To address
these issues, we re-estimate the model with samples separated by the
number of banks in a city. Since depositors’ knowledge of the charac-
teristics of each bank in their town diminished and the probability of
having additional services offered increased as the number of banks in
their town grew, the coefficients should differ across regressions if these
factors influenced reopening. We find no significant differences indi-
cating that the most prominent sources of unobservable characteristics
likely do not distort our results.

We also show that the imperfect signal is not driven by observable
risk factors that are contained in other data. Specifically, the OCC pro-
vides information on bills payable (i.e., costly funding) and separately
reports US Treasury bonds from other securities. Both an indicator for
whether the bank had any bills payable and its ratio of US Treasury
bonds to total securities significantly predict reopenings but do not
dramatically change the effect of other factors or the classification rate.

In this way, we are confident that despite the limitations of the Rand
McNally data that the results are not being driven by observable risk
factors.

1929 to 1933. We re-estimate the regressions when dropping out any
county with a city of 25,000 or more people as large cities had many
more banks competing and more diverse business interests. We also re-
estimate the regressions only for those state non-member banks that
either reopened fully or had a restricted reopening in March, as well as

5.2. Reopening dates and deposits at individual banks–surrounding
deposits

The evidence presented in the previous section suggests that those
banks that were chosen to reopen in March had higher changes in de-
posits over time. A reasonable question is where those deposits came
from. This section attempts to shed light on this question through
additional narrative and empirical evidence.

The deposit inflow was likely money returning to the system. If a
depositor’s original bank remained closed, then they would have been
unable to withdraw their deposits in order to place them with another
bank. So, the initial balance sheet change observed is more likely due to
“mattress” money returning to banks, as opposed to resources shifting
away from a bank that had not reopened yet. This agrees with Silber
(2009) who argues that the de facto government safety net for reopened
banks brought money back to the system, and the Federal Reserve Board
(1934) that argues that the return of money was from hoarders rather
than active circulation. Moreover, the differential change is not due to
the costs of moving deposits back to the original bank after it reopened.
Because they could not withdraw money from their original account,
depositors would have had to keep their original deposit account at the
non-reopened bank which would have eliminated the costs of moving
money back to their originally preferred bank once it reopened. As we
show later, quickly reopened banks maintained their extra deposits for
over a decade, suggesting choices made during the Bank Holiday shifted

deposits that would normally go to another bank in a town but could not
because that bank had remained closed. If quickly reopened banks were
more often located near these remaining closed banks, then the differ-

in the first few months). We test this hypothesis by creating an indicator
for whether a reopened bank was in the same city as a bank that had not
yet reopened in July 1933. About 11.5% of banks reopened in March
1933 were in a location that had a bank that remained closed through
July 1933, whereas 12.0% of banks reopened between April and June
1933 were in a location that had one.

To consider the closed bank hypothesis in an econometrics frame-
work, we adapted Eq. (2) to include an indicator for whether or not at
least one nearby bank remained closed after July 1933 and an interac-

Table 6 show that deposits at reopened banks did rise by more in areas
with closed banks. However, the effect is relatively small compared to
the effect of early reopening. Additionally, the interaction is insignifi-
cant, meaning that a nearby bank closure does not modify the early
reopening effect on deposit changes. The evidence suggests that the
stigma effect we are measuring is not necessarily driven by differential
closure rates around quickly reopened banks.

banks reopened in March saw a substantially larger change in deposits
that was sustained through 1940, regardless of whether they reopened
in the first few days or the latter two weeks. However, there is a sig-
nificant difference between the size of that relative change in deposits
for the quickest reopening versus others. Specifically, those that were
opened within the first few days after the Holiday saw a much higher
change in deposits than those opened in the second week, and those
opened in the second week saw slightly higher change than those
opened in the third week. Over time, the deposit differences between
banks reopened in the second and third week narrow, but the difference
for banks reopened in the first week remains. Regulators could not
feasibly review and reopen all sound banks in the first week. Never-
theless, those first, second, and third week decisions had lasting effects.
Taken as a whole, depositors preferred banks that were reopened
quickly even though many other banks opened just a few days or weeks
later. Depositors thus seem to have taken the speed of reopening as a
cleaner signal of financial stability than traditional observable funda-
mentals, stigmatizing slowly reopened banks. The persistent effect lends

5.6. Ruling out alternative explanations

While the results of the analysis are consistent with our claims con-
cerning stigma, the extant data and existing statistical methods cannot
rule out all potential alternative hypotheses. The most salient threats to
inference involve regulators conditioning reopening on information that
(i) they observed, (ii) was correlated with changes in deposits from June
1932 to June 1933 (i.e. our dependent variable), and (iii) we do not
observe and thus cannot control for. To exclude these threats (or at least
limit their plausibility), we bring to bear additional historical evidence.
The initial threat to consider is the possibility that regulators’
reopening decisions depended upon the deposits that each bank lost
during the panic. Routine procedures would not have generated this
data, but regulators could have collected this one piece of information
on an emergency basis. Regulators might have initially reopened banks
that lost the most deposits (but did not fail). This policy would seem

M. Jaremski et al.

Journal of Financial Economics 163 (2025) 103968 

sensible. Regulators were trying to get deposits to flow back into the
system. Regulators wanted to reopen healthy banks. Banks that paid out
a large fraction of their deposits in a short period of time without sus-
pending operations or failing outright demonstrated that they were
liquid and solvent. Regulators clearly knew the quality of their assets
because those assets served as collateral for loan from Federal Reserve
banks, and the Reserve Bank discount officers had appraised these assets
and had them in their possession. If regulators reopened these banks
first, then our estimates would understate stigma, since earlier reopeners
would have had a more rapid rebound than we estimate.18 Regulators
might also have done the opposite, and reopened banks with larger
deposit losses later. In this case, our estimates would be biased away
from zero, and our estimates would overstate stigma. If regulators used
one approach in some locales and the other approach in other locales,
our estimates might or might not be biased, but the standard errors of
our estimates would almost certainly be.

While it was possible for regulators to have collected this information
in the early days of the Holiday, we do not believe that they did so. We
consulted regulatory reports, memoirs, newspapers, and the archives of
the OCC and the Fed. We list these sources in the data section and
describe the most important in additional detail in Appendix Section D.
None mention efforts to collect this information or report that this in-
formation was collected. None describe regulators using this informa-
tion when making decisions. Instead, the sources do mention the
suspension of routine data collection during the Holiday.19 The sources
also mention regulators being overburdened with work. Many had to
deal with the hundreds of banks that suspended operations before the
government shuttered the banking system. All those banks had to be
evaluated to determine if they could reopen or should enter liquidation.
Many examiners were assigned to help with discount lending, which
expanded 100-fold during the three-week panic.20 Historical sources
mention all these activities, but they do not mention an effort to collect
or use data on deposit losses during the panic, which we believe they
would have, if such as effort occurred. The archives of the OCC, RFC, and
the Fed also contain no information about deposit losses during the
panic, although they contain extensive data on bank outcomes during

Another change on the liability side of banks’ balance sheets that
could also have generated the appearance of stigma is recapitalization.
Perhaps banks that reopened early received injections of capital from
federal agencies, state authorities, or the market, which encouraged
increases in deposits. The federal government did purchase preferred
stock in thousands of banks, but majority of these purchases occurred
after the incorporation of the FDIC in the summer of 1933 and most
occurred after December 1933. This recapitalization program, in other
words, was implemented almost entirely after our analysis concludes.
State authorities never purchased equity in commercial banks; their
budgets were tight and they asked federal agencies to take the lead on
this issue. The market also does not appear to have recapitalized com-
mercial banks. Increases in banks’ paid-up capital from June 1932 to
June 1933 were rare and uncorrelated with reopening dates. Moreover,
no statistical differences in capital existed between banks that reopened
early and late (see Appendix A2 Table A8).

Another possibility on the liability side of banks’ balance sheets that
could have generated the appearance of stigma is changes in banks’
deposit services. One possibility is banks that reopened earlier improved
their deposit services or offered some advantageous services to de-
positors who switched from other banks. During the Depression, some
commercial banks attracted depositors from mutual savings banks by
purchasing their mutual-savings shares. Newspapers describe this ac-
tivity and published tables of the prices (or markdowns) that banks
would pay for shares from different mutual-savings banks. Newspapers
also reveal that regulators and clearing houses expedited access to de-
posits at failed commercial banks by assigning nearby commercial banks
as agents that would provide cash or deposit services to individuals
awaiting payment of frozen funds.21 We find, however, no evidence that
commercial banks that opened earlier advanced cash against deposits in
banks that reopened later, or that a market for late-reopening deposits
existed, or that regulators in any way encouraged the transition of de-
posits or depositors towards banks that reopened early. Moreover, our
results show no significant difference between the reopening estimate
for large financial centers where such services might have emerged and
rural areas where such services would not have.

Another possibility is banks that reopened later only reopened after
forcing depositors to accept write offs on their deposits. The Fed’s ar-
chives show that a handful of banks reopened in 1932 after depositors
and stockholders agreed to symmetrically reduce their claims against
the bank so that the institution could reopen. The RFC also reports a
small number of write-offs during recapitalizations and reorganizations
that helped banks reopen in late 1933. We see no reports of write offs,
however, in March and April of 1933, when all reports indicate that all
banks reopened with the same balance sheets, liabilities, and assets that

A final question related to the liability side of the balance sheet in-
volves banks’ deposit franchise value. Drechsler et al. (2023) describe
this arrangement for modern banks, where increases (decreases) in
market interest rates relative to the rates banks pay to depositors in-
crease (decrease) the spread banks earn on deposits, a risk which banks
hedge with long-term investments whose value fluctuates inversely with
market interest rates. This dynamic did not come into play during the
Banking Holiday. Before that event, almost all banks’ investments
matured in the short term, typically a few months or less. From the start
of the panic in January 1933 to the end of our study in June 1933, de-
posit rates, loan rates, Fed policy rates, other market interest rates, and
policy rates moved little and essentially 1 for 1. So, banks’ franchise
value should have changed little during the period of our study. Changes
in banks’ deposit franchise value, in other words, could not explain the
correlation between reopening date and deposit rebound during the
Banking Holiday. We cannot rule out, however, the potential that
changes in deposit franchise value contributed to stigma’s persistence,
since amendments to the Federal Reserve Act compelled the central
bank to cap deposit interest rates. The cap for demand deposits became
binding in the later 1930s, about the same time that banks began
extending substantial quantities of medium-term loans and that banks
began purchasing substantial quantities of long-term government bonds
(Jacoby and Saulnier, 1942).

We can also rule out a range of alternatives on the asset side of banks’
balance sheets. A principal concern would be that regulators decided
reopening dates based upon updated information about the value of
banks’ assets, or prospects for banks’ lending growth, or prospects for
economic recovery in the regions around each bank. Historical sources
indicate that regulators did not update information about banks’ port-
folios while making decisions. While the Treasury’s instructions about
how to determine when to reopen banks were vague, they clearly stated
that they should be based upon existing information and did not request
the collection of new information. Even if they had, collecting infor-
mation about asset quality - unlike information about deposit losses -
would have been costly and time-consuming.22 Again, as Jesse Jones
stated, “The plunge in values, particularly market ones, made one man’s
guess as good, or bad, as another’s in assessing the probable worth of
many a bank’s portfolio” (Jones and Angly, 1951, p. 21). That is why
examiners scrutinized only portions of each bank’s past-due loans dur-
ing examinations, and examiners conducted these examinations infre-
quently, usually once or twice each year.

We believe the other possible alternatives were unlikely for similar
reasons. Examiners lacked objective information on prospects for banks’
loan growth or counties economic recovery. Neither the data nor
methods to make the projections existed. In addition, our analyses in the
previous and following sections demonstrate that reopening dates were
uncorrelated with loan expansion and economic growth. So, condi-
tioning reopening dates on accurate projections of loan and economic
growth could not have generated the correlations in our paper, even if
examiners had been able to observe it.

fail see larger declines in economic output, particularly regions where
local banks with specialized knowledge financed local industries. We
find the opposite did not occur during the expansion.

There are three likely explanations. First, economic theory indicates
that credit frictions should bind more tightly during contractions, when
liquidity constraints bind more tightly and threaten financiers’ solvency,
than during expansions, when liquidity is abundant and inexpensive
(Rocheteau and Nosal, 2017). Second, our results from the previous
section show that early reopeners followed pre-Holiday lending trends.
The extra deposits that they received did not result in extra lending for
businesses in their local community. Instead, they invested most of these
extra deposits in bonds. Many of these bonds were issued by the U.S.
government, some by state and local governments, and others by utili-
ties and large, safe corporations located throughout the United States.
Conversely, late reopeners responded to the loss of deposits in part by
reducing bond holding. So, the shift in deposits from late to early
reopeners did not result in the reallocation of credit across communities
or businesses. Instead, it merely changed ownership of a share of the
existing stock of government and corporate bonds.

Third, the federal government established an expanding set of pro-
grams to ensure that firms and entrepreneurs who needed working
capital could access those funds. These programs included (a) creating
the RFC, initially to funnel funds to commercial banks, but later to
directly finance firms in a wide range of industries; (b) amending the
Federal Reserve Act to allow the Reserve Banks to loan funds directly to
businesses; and (c) establishing a range of additional agencies to loan
funds to farms, utilities, and local governments. Firms seeking funds
from the RFC or Fed were initially directed to local banks. If the banks
were reluctant to extend credit on reasonable terms, the RFC and Fed
would then often offer to extend a portion of the loan, with the
remainder extended by the local bank. This arrangement reduced the
default risk borne by the bank and guaranteed that the loan would be
accepted at face value as collateral by the RFC or at the Discount Win-
dow, eliminating liquidity risk. Such arrangements enabled most firms
to acquire credit at a reasonable cost, and the remainder could apply to
other federal agencies for funds (Jones and Angly, 1951).

7. Discussion

In 1933, when the largest banking panic in U.S. history forced the
federal government to shut down and restart the financial system, pol-
icymakers worried the order in which they reopened banks would laude
some banks and stigmatize others, inducing depositors to shift funds
from some banks to others. Our research indicates their fears were
justified. Deposits shifted towards banks that reopened earlier and the
communities in which they operated. Although these shifts persisted for
at least ten years, they do not appear to have interrupted lending to
business or economic activity of communities.

The banking crisis of 1933 was an extreme example of the panics that
periodically plague financial markets and government policymakers.
The Banking Holiday was the largest intervention ever into the financial
system. It occurred at the trough of the deepest contraction of the
twentieth century. During recent panics, policymakers have reported
fears similar to their Depression-Era counterparts. Policymakers worried
their actions might send signals that influenced depositors, reallocated
funds through the financial system, and altered allocations of credit to
commerce and industry. Policymakers’ fears influenced their policies.
Those leading the response to the Global Financial Crisis in 2008–9 erred
on the side of stopping the present panic with broad interventions
designed to avoid casting stigmas and exacerbating the situation. The
leaders strove to conceal the identities of banks receiving assistance. The
Federal Reserve, for example, argued in public and in court that con-
cealing the names of banks that borrowed from the discount window and
other lending facilities was crucial for ending the crisis. Critics worry,
however, that such broad, concealed, and unconditional interventions
may induce moral hazard that could increase the severity of future
crises.

Our research suggests that the intertemporal implications of in-
terventions during banking crises might be better balanced. In 1933, the
sequential reopening of commercial banks sent signals about bank
health that resulted in winners and losers. The resulting flow of funds
between banks probably altered banks’ bottom lines and bankers’
pocketbooks. The impact did not, however, carry over to commerce and
industry. The three likely explanations for why it did not include (i) the
fact that credit constraints bind less during expansions, (ii) governments
have established an array of programs to extend credit directly to

industries afflicted by a contraction, and (iii) banks appear to hold
substantial excess reserves and invest heavily in bonds in the wake of
every financial crisis. All those factors appear to be as true today as
during the 1930s. This suggests that even substantial bank stigma
induced by identifying recipients of central-bank assistance, or by tar-
geting assistance at banks which need more help, or by targeting assis-
tance banks which deserve more help can be overcome today just as it
was overcome in the past.

A final question to consider is the external relevance of our example:
could stigmas arise in the world today? The limited information avail-
able about banks and the economy in the past created conditions where
stigma can arise. Limited information then has been replaced by abun-
dant information today. Detailed balance sheets of all banks in the
United States (and in most other developed nations) can be downloaded
with a few clicks of a computer keyboard. More information, in theory,
yields less stigma. In practical terms, however, the information envi-
ronment for most depositors - then and now - is the same. Most de-
positors know little about banks’ financial health and lack the
knowledge needed to assess it. During the GFC, despite publically
available balance sheet information, deposit insurance, and ubiquitous
publicity of government actions to provide support for financial in-
stitutions, confidence in the financial system was still not fully restored
until regulators examined and released information from stress tests on
the nation’s largest financial intermediaries, offering market partici-
pants a clear understanding of the health of those institutions. De-
positors’ difficulties assessing institutions is a reason that almost all
OECD nations insure bank deposits and why policymakers today, like
those in the past, fear that their actions could generate signals and
stigmas that might impact banking systems.
