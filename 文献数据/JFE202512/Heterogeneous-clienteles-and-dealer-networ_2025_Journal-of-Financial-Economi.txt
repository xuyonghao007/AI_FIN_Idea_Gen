1. Introduction

This paper studies a search-based model of OTC markets in which clients with heterogeneous trading needs 
direct  their  trades  to  one  of  ex-ante  identical  dealers.  The  main  insight  of  the  paper  is  that  the  way 
clients sort across dealers shapes dealer-to-dealer trading patterns and, in turn, generates a core–periphery 
interdealer network structure. Dealers in the model become heterogeneous because they attract different 
clients in equilibrium. Some dealers attract clients who trade frequently (e.g., index funds); others attract 
clients with infrequent trading needs (e.g., pension funds). Dealers attracting clients with frequent trading 
needs receive a larger volume of client orders, trade more with other dealers, and, as a result, form the 
core of the interdealer network. Conversely, dealers specializing in clients with infrequent trading needs form 
the periphery. I also show that accounting for client heterogeneity across dealers (a) challenges standard 
measurements and interpretations of bid–ask spreads and (b) generates predictions on bid–ask spreads and 
dealer centrality consistent with the empirical literature.

In over-the-counter (OTC) financial markets, transactions between 
broker-dealers  exhibit  a  core–periphery  network  structure.1  Ten  to 
thirty highly interconnected dealers account for a majority of dealer-
to-dealer transactions and, as a result, form the core of the interdealer 
network,  while  hundreds  of  sparsely  connected  dealers  trade  infre-
quently and form the periphery. Motivated by this fact, a growing body 
of OTC market models studies the implications of ex-ante heterogeneity 
among intermediaries, with a key implication being the core–periphery 
structure.2

In this paper, I explore the implications of heterogeneity on the 
clients’ side, a realistic but unstudied determinant of market outcomes.

I show these results in a continuous time, infinite horizon search 
model  that  builds  on  Duffie  et  al.  (2005)  and  Vayanos  and  Wang 
(2007). Dealers are ex-ante identical, but a continuum of customers 
have heterogeneous trading needs. Some customers buy a bond expect-
ing to hold it for a long time; others buy knowing they will turn around 
and sell quickly. Customers can only buy and sell through one of the 
dealers and choose which dealer to buy from. I assume that once a 
customer buys from a given dealer, she has to sell back to the same 
dealer to reverse her trade. Dealers intermediate bonds for customers 
either  by  matching  their  buyer  and  seller  clients  ‘‘in-house’’  or  by 
trading with other dealers and matching ‘‘inter-house’’ their clients with 
the clients of other dealers. I define two dealers as connected if they 
create such inter-house matches. I assume that each dealer is connected 
to every other dealer in the economy (i.e., a complete network), but 
network weights (i.e., the volume of trade between pairs of dealers) 
are determined in equilibrium. Dealers are nonstrategic agents, while 
customers make all the decisions in the model.3 Dealers buy a bond 
from a client (or a dealer) and instantaneously sell to another client 
(or a dealer) and thus do not carry inventory.4 Finally, customers and 
dealers trade at prices such that they split the total gains from an 
intermediation chain according to fixed bargaining weights.

The first main result of the paper is that dealers’ equilibrium clien-
tele differences implies a core–periphery interdealer network. In equi-
librium, clients tradeoff a dealer’s ask-price versus its bid-price. Some 
dealers charge a higher ask-price but, in return, buy back at a higher 
bid-price. Other dealers charge a cheaper ask-price but offer a worse 
(i.e., a lower) bid-price when the client has to reverse her trade and sell. 
Buyers who expect to reverse their position quickly care more about 
what happens to them as a seller. As a result, they choose the dealer 
offering the higher bid-price and are willing to pay the higher ask-price. 
Buy-and-hold investors, less concerned with turning into a seller later 
on, choose the dealer offering the cheaper ask-price. Thus, investors 
with different trading needs endogenously sort across different dealers. 
The clientele difference across dealers, in turn, generates the different 
ask-  and  bid-prices  across  dealers.  It  also  generates  a  cross-dealer 
variation  in  the  volume  of  client  orders,  the  volume  of  interdealer 
trades, and hence the network centrality of dealers.

First, in the presence of client heterogeneity across dealers and in-

dealers.

Lastly, I explore the model’s implications on bid–ask spreads and 
dealer  centrality.  In  my  model,  clients’  transaction  costs  exhibit  a 
centrality discount—the average observed transaction cost is narrower 
for clients choosing a core dealer than for clients choosing a periph-
eral dealer. I also construct a bid–ask spread capturing the round-trip 
transaction cost dealers face from each other. It exhibits a centrality 
premium—core dealers charge other dealers a wider bid–ask spread 
than peripheral dealers. Thus, the same mechanism that generates a 
centrality discount in client bid–ask spreads – specialization in clients 
– simultaneously generates a centrality premium in interdealer bid–ask 
spreads. This result is consistent with Dick-Nielsen et al. (2021), who 
document a centrality discount in client transaction costs but a central-
ity premium in interdealer bid–ask spreads. My model also predicts that 
core dealers earn a smaller markup (i.e., profit) than peripheral dealers. 
This prediction is consistent with a centrality discount documented 
by  Goldstein  and  Hotchkiss  (2020),  whose  bid–ask  spread  measure 
captures dealer markups.6

Related literature

My paper complements a growing literature that seeks to explain 
the observed core–periphery structure. Existing explanations rely on 
ex-ante dealer heterogeneity (e.g., Atkeson et al., 2015, Uslu, 2019, 
Neklyudov, 2019). I show that dealer heterogeneity can arise from equi-
librium clientele differences. Moreover, the predictions of my model 
differ from the existing theoretical work on the core–periphery struc-
ture. Models of Uslu (2019) and Neklyudov (2019), for example, imply 
that client and interdealer bid–ask spreads behave the same way as 
functions  of  dealer  centrality.  In  my  model,  client  bid–ask  spreads 
exhibit a centrality discount, while interdealer bid–ask spreads exhibit 
a centrality premium, consistent with the findings of Dick-Nielsen et al. 
(2021). Similarly, models of Uslu (2019), Neklyudov (2019), Farboodi 
et al. (2023), and Wang (2021) imply that dealers trade more with each

My paper also contributes to the OTC search literature. I depart 
from the existing search literature, first, by assuming that client-dealer 
trading relationships are persistent. Once a client buys from a dealer, 
she has to sell back to the same dealer (for evidence, see Hendershott 
et al., 2020). In existing models, clients match at random with one 
of infinitely many dealers each time they want to trade.10 Thus, they 
implicitly assume that the cost of forming a client-dealer relationship is 
zero, while I implicitly assume a positive cost. Persistent client-dealer 
relationships allow me to model and study features that are absent 
from the search literature: (1) how clients with heterogeneous trading 
needs optimally choose dealers and (2) the resulting equilibrium dealer 
heterogeneity.11

The second key departure from the existing search literature lies in 
modeling a more realistic decentralized interdealer market. In Duffie 
et al. (2005), Lagos and Rocheteau (2009), Lester et al. (2015), and Mil-
bradt (2017), for example, the interdealer market is a reduced form 
technology that allows dealers to frictionlessly buy and sell an asset. 
However, in practice, dealers face frictions, charge each other bid–ask 
spreads, have heterogeneous network centralities, and trade with each 
other at prices that reflect their network centralities (Li and Schurhoff, 
2018, Dick-Nielsen et al., 2021). To capture the above facts, dealers in 
my model are atomistic (not infinitesimal), they trade with each other 
repeatedly, and the terms and the volume of trade between pairs of 
dealers are equilibrium objects.

The last key departure from the search literature lies in generating 
persistence in dealers’ intermediation roles. Search models assume that 
agents’ private valuation of an asset changes randomly (as a way to 
generate trade in equilibrium). In some models, this assumption can 
imply that an agent who is core one period can randomly become 
peripheral  the  next  period  and  vice  versa.12  However,  in  the  data, 
dealers’ network centralities are highly persistent. Li and Schurhoff 
(2018)  document  that  core  and  peripheral  dealers  retain  their  re-
spective centralities month-to-month with probabilities 93% and 97%, 
respectively. I model clients and dealers separately and assume that 
clients experience private valuation changes, not dealers. Dealers’ iden-
tities and their equilibrium intermediation roles (e.g., whether they are 
a core or a peripheral dealer), as a result, remain stable over time.

2. Model

Time is continuous and goes from zero to infinity. There is one asset 
– a bond with supply 𝑆 paying a constant coupon flow 𝛿 – and two 
sets of agents: clients and 𝑛 ex-ante identical dealers. I index dealers by 
𝑖 ∈ 𝑁, where 𝑁 = {1, 2, … , 𝑛} is the set of dealers. Clients and dealers 
are risk neutral and discount at rate 𝑟 > 0.

2.1. Clients

Clients are the end-investors in the bond. They have an idiosyncratic 
high or low private valuation for the bond. High-valuation investors 
derive  a  flow  utility  𝛿 > 0  from  holding  the  bond.  Low-valuation 
investors derive 𝛿 − 𝑥 > 0, where 𝑥 > 0 represents a disutility of 
holding the bond. Investors’ valuations change randomly over time. 
High-valuation  investors  experience  a  valuation  shock  with  Poisson 
intensity 𝑘 and become a low-valuation investor. The low-valuation 
state is absorbing (investors do not switch back to the high-valuation 
state). Upon a valuation shock, investors immediately exit the economy 
if they do not own bonds; if they own bonds, they first sell and then exit. 
Replenishing the exiting investors, new investors enter the economy as 
high-valuation investors. Going forward, I will use clients, customers, 
and investors interchangeably.

The initial entry into the economy and the subsequent valuation 
shocks  generate  a  need  to  rebalance  asset  positions  and  trade.  In 
equilibrium, high-valuation investors want to own the bond. If they 
do not hold the bond (which is the case when they first enter the 
economy), they seek to buy the bond. After purchasing the bond, they 
optimally hold the bond until they experience a valuation shock and 
revert to a low-valuation state. As a low-valuation investor, they find 
it optimal to sell their bond to someone who likes the bond more 
(i.e., a high-valuation investor). Categorizing agents by their valuation

I assume that once a buyer chooses a dealer and purchases a bond 
from the dealer, she has to sell back to the same dealer to reverse 
her trade (consistent with the evidence in Hendershott et al., 2020 
and  Cohen,  2022).15  This  is  a  key  assumption.  If  clients  can  resell 
through any dealer, specialization would not arise. This assumption 
is  realistic.  Hendershott  et  al.  (2020),  for  example,  document  that 
insurance companies (which are the largest holders of corporate bonds) 
unwind  their  bond  trade  using  the  same  dealer  as  when  they  first 
entered  the  trade  with,  and  that  in  an  entire  year  most  insurance 
companies trade with a single dealer. I further motivate this assumption 
below.

The life-cycle of investors evolves as follows. An investor enters the 
economy as a high-valuation non-owner (i.e., a buyer), picks a dealer, 
and becomes a buyer client of that dealer. Upon buying the bond, she 
becomes an owner client of the dealer. As an owner, she holds the bond 
until she experiences a valuation shock and becomes a seller client of 
the same dealer. Upon selling the bond, the investor exits the economy.
An  alternative  interpretation  of  the  lifecycle  described  above  is 
that it is a single round-trip trade of a client. The same client could 
potentially reenter the economy with a new round-trip trade (e.g., with 
a different 𝑘) and choose a new dealer. With this interpretation, my 
model does not preclude clients from trading with multiple dealers. I 
adopt the former interpretation for convenience.

Discussion of assumption.  In practice, the assumption that an investor 
has to sell back to the same dealer captures a fixed cost of building 
a client-dealer relationship (a) at the institution level and/or (b) at 
the  bond  level  (i.e.,  per  single  round-trip  trade).  At  the  institution 
level, the fixed cost arises from master agreements financial institutions 
negotiate with each other. To enter credit default swap (CDS) and other 
derivative contracts, for example, clients negotiate International Swaps 
and Derivatives Association (ISDA) master agreements with dealers to 
establish the terms and conditions of their trades. These negotiations 
take on average 4–6 months involving lawyers from both the client 
and dealer institutions (BIS, 1998, BIS, 2018). Once the agreements are

As Proposition  1 shows, the asymmetric equilibrium features the 
following client structure across dealers. First, different dealers attract 
different clients. Dealers buying back at a higher expected bid-price 
attract investors with relatively high liquidity need. Dealers buying 
back at a lower expected bid-price attract investors with buy-and-hold 
preferences. Second, the high bid-price dealers have larger client sizes 
or, equivalently, volumes of client orders outstanding, while the low 
bid-price dealers have smaller volumes. Thus, both the composition and 
the size of clients are heterogeneous across dealers.

Corollary  1 shows that the endogenous heterogeneity in the volume

The above results imply that peripheral dealers attract buy-and-hold 
client institutions (e.g., pension funds), while core dealers specialize 
in high liquidity need client institutions (e.g., mutual funds).24 More 
broadly, my model predicts that core and peripheral dealers attract 
investment positions with short and long holding periods, respectively. 
This prediction comes from interpreting each 𝑘 to be a different in-
vestment position or trade, regardless of whether the trades come from 
the same or different client institutions. For example, the same client 
institution may have both a high-𝑘 buy order that they expect to reverse 
quickly and a low-𝑘 buy order that they expect to hold onto much 
longer. The client would then send the positions they expect to reverse 
quickly to a core dealer and their buy-and-hold positions to a peripheral 
dealer. This is a novel implication yet to be tested in the data.

Finally, since the core–periphery structure is a steady state property, 
dealers’ network centralities are persistent. A core dealer remains a core 
dealer period to period and the same for peripheral dealers, consistent 
with Li and Schurhoff (2018).

condition is necessary for dealer heterogeneity to arise. If instead 𝑧dd ≤
𝑧d, only the symmetric equilibrium exists. As mentioned earlier, the 
condition 𝑧dd > 𝑧d says that clients have a larger bargaining power in 
two-dealer chains than in one-dealer chains.

3.4. Semi-asymmetric equilibrium

In  Appendix   B.1,  I  show  that,  under  Assumption   1,  a
semi-asymmetric  equilibrium  exists,  in  which  dealers  are  identical 
within a tier but are heterogeneous across tiers. The symmetric equilib-
rium is a special case of the semi-asymmetric equilibrium with a single 
tier and 𝑛 dealers in that tier, while the asymmetric equilibrium is a 
special case with 𝑛 tiers and a single dealer in each tier.

In  Online  Appendix  F.2,  I  show  that,  under  Assumption   1  in  a 
2-dealer case, the fully asymmetric equilibrium is the only stable equi-
librium and that both the symmetric and semi-asymmetric equilibria 
are unstable. In the rest of the paper, I focus on the fully asymmetric

Fig.  6 illustrates how clients’ welfare, dealer profits, and the total 
social welfare behave as functions of the number of dealers in the 
economy.  As  the  number  of  dealers  increases  in  the  economy,  the

misallocation of bonds and hence the social welfare. What changes is 
how clients and dealers split the surplus. As the number of dealers 
increases, the aggregate volume of two-dealer chains increases relative 
to that of one-dealer chains. Because clients extract a larger fraction 
of the trading surplus in two-dealer chains than in one-dealer chains 
(recall Assumption  1), the increase in the number of two-dealer chains 
benefits clients at the expense of dealers. Clients’ welfare increases, 
while dealer profits decrease with the number of dealers. In Online 
Appendix F.3, I provide further welfare analysis and explain that clients 
are better off in the asymmetric than in the symmetric equilibrium.

In this paper, I build a search-based model in which dealers are 
ex-ante identical, but clients with heterogeneous trading needs choose 
which dealer to be a client of. I show that how clients sort across dealers 
determines the shape of the dealer-to-dealer trading patterns and, in 
particular, generates a core–periphery interdealer network structure. 
Dealers attract different clienteles in equilibrium. Some dealers attract 
clients who trade frequently (e.g., index funds); others attract clients 
with infrequent trading needs (e.g., pension funds). Dealers attracting 
clients with frequent trading needs receive a larger volume of client 
orders, trade more with other dealers, and, as a result, form the core of 
the interdealer network. Conversely, dealers that specialize in clients 
with infrequent trading needs form the periphery.

In a second set of results, I show that measuring and interpreting 
bid–ask spreads in the presence of client heterogeneity across dealers 
requires care. I show that a bid–ask spread capturing clients’ transaction 
cost differs from a bid–ask spread capturing dealer markups in the pres-
ence of client heterogeneity across dealers. This implication challenges 
the standard convention in the market microstructure literature that 
the two are equivalent. I also show that a commonly used bid–ask 
spread measure (Li and Schurhoff, 2018, Hollifield et al., 2017, Uslu, 
2019 and Hugonnier et al., 2020) captures neither client transaction 
costs nor dealer markups as a result of ignoring client heterogeneity 
across dealers. Future work should adopt client transaction cost and 
dealer markup measures that account for the client heterogeneity across 
dealers.

of one-dealer chains, increasing the number of dealers would reduce the total

As Proposition  B.1 shows, the within tier economy resembles the 
economy under the symmetric equilibrium. Dealers in the same tier 
have identical expected bid-prices and client masses. Clients are indif-
ferent between dealers in the same tier.

The dealer heterogeneity across tiers mirrors that of the asymmetric 
equilibrium. First, dealers in the high bid-price tiers attract relatively 
high-𝑘 investors, while those in the low bid-price tiers attract relatively 
low-𝑘 investors. To choose across different tier dealers, investors face 
the same tradeoff as in Lemma  3. Second, dealers in the high bid-
price tiers receive larger volumes of client orders than dealers in the 
lower tiers. Third, dealers in the high bid-price tiers trade more with 
other dealers, account for a larger fraction of the aggregate interdealer 
volume, and thus form the core of the interdealer network. It is vice 
versa for dealers in the low bid-price tiers.
