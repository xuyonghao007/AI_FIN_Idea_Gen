A B S T R A C T

Using cross-country and German administrative data on robotization, we show that the impact of robots on 
firms and labor markets is limited. First, investment in robots is small and highly concentrated in a few 
industries, representing less than 0.3% of aggregate expenditures on equipment. Second, robotization does not 
grow as rapidly as Information Technology did in the past, and current growth is driven by gains in developing 
countries. Third, firms invest in robots when they face difficulties in finding workers and subsequently increase 
employment after the investment. The total employment effect in exposed industries and regions is negative 
but modest in magnitude. We discuss why the effects of robots are limited and demonstrate that other digital 
technologies have more potential for large economic impact.

1. Introduction

Recent advances in automation are often argued to be changing 
the future of work. In what is called the fourth Industrial Revolution, 
attention is devoted to industrial robots and artificial intelligence (AI) 
algorithms. While it may be too early to assess the impact of AI, several 
prominent studies highlight robots’ significant impact on the economy. 
For example, Graetz and Michaels (2018) find that robots bring pro-
ductivity increases comparable with past improvements caused by the 
steam engine and information technologies (IT), while Acemoglu and 
Restrepo (2020), Acemoglu (2021) argue that robots are a key driver 
of worker displacement. These effects, combined with a compounded 
annual growth rate in the use of robots of 12% between 2015 and 
2022, suggest that robots may significantly transform labor markets 
and the corporate sector, leading to massive worker displacement and 
a significant increase in firms’ capital intensity.

The canonical model of the effects of technology on employment is 
that of enabling technology, which augments labor and boosts workers’ 

productivity, leading to higher wages and increased employment. Yet, 
the  looming  threat  of  automation  motivates  an  alternative  concept 
of  displacing/replacing  technology,  which  has  recently  gained  promi-
nence (Autor, 2015). According to Acemoglu (2017), the ‘‘spread of in-
dustrial robots is a perfect case study for replacing technologies’’, which 
reduce wages and employment, because robots are reprogrammable 
machines capable of performing multiple tasks without a human oper-
ator. In this paper, we empirically examine the role of industrial robots 
in firms’ investment and evaluate their effects on labor markets.

Our results suggest that the impact of robots on firm balance sheets 
and labor markets is likely to be limited. First, we show that despite om-
nipresent predictions of the transformative role of robotics, robots are 
hard to find not only in aggregate productivity statistics (Brynjolfsson 
et al., 2019) but also in firms’ capital expenditures. Using cross-country 
data on robotization, we show that investment in robots, though in-
creasing, remains a small share of total investment. The use of robots is 
almost zero outside of manufacturing, and even within manufacturing,

manufacturing. We argue that this pattern is unlikely to change in 
the near future, as robot investment does not exhibit the explosive 
growth that characterized IT in the past. Recent increases in numbers 
of  robots  sold  are  driven  mostly  by  manufacturing  gains  in  China

in advanced economies. These findings suggest that predictions of a 
transformative impact of robots may be unwarranted.

To address the empirical challenges of studying the role of robots 
in labor markets, we utilize firm-level data from the German Employ-
ment Agency. Our analysis shows that firms are more likely to invest 
in robots when it is harder to find workers and that they increase 
employment after adopting robots. Indirect employment effects among 
non-adopters, estimated in a difference-in-differences framework that 
compares high- and low-adopting industries and local areas, are nega-
tive but small. Back-of-envelope calculations suggest that in Germany, 
employment growth in regions and industries with high adoption of 
robots is lower, but only by ∼0.03% per year.

Our analysis is based on two data sets and empirical settings, which 
allows us to overcome measurement challenges common in this area 
of research. First, we combine a cross-country industry-level measure 
of  robotization  from  the  International  Federation  of  Robotics  (IFR) 
with firm-level financial data from Amadeus. We use these data sets 
to estimate the prevalence of robotics across industries and countries 
and its importance in firms’ capital expenditures. Second, we use ad-
ministrative data from the Institute for Employment Research (IAB) of 
the German Employment Agency, which include firm-level measures of 
automation and detailed employment data. We use these data to study 
how investment in automation responds to labor market conditions and 
how automation affects firms’ employment.

We start by evaluating the impact of robotization on firms’ capital 
expenditures using cross-country data. The magnitude of robot-related 
investment is small both in absolute terms and in comparison to other 
similar types of investment. We rely on reports of the numbers of robots 
installed combined with estimates of the average price of robots, as well 
as on a regression-based approach that attempts to estimate the share of 
robots in firms’ capital investment. Both approaches lead to estimates of 
the same order of magnitude and suggest that even after supplementary 
investment is included, robots do not account for a significant share 
of  firm-level  investment.  In  manufacturing,  robots’  share  in  capital 
expenditure (CapEx) is less than 2%, whereas in the overall economy 
it approaches 0.3%. Annual robotics-related expenditures amount to 
roughly $40 per worker.

It is of course possible that robots are still in their infancy and that 
their dynamic growth will soon make them much more prevalent. The 
data, however, offer limited support for this hypothesis. Studying the 
evolution of the number of robots around the world, we show that re-
cent increases in robotization are driven not by accelerating automation 
in developed countries but rather by gains in developing nations. In the 
West, robotization grows at a stable and moderate pace.1 Moreover, 
robots  remain  highly  concentrated  in  selected  industries,  especially 
automotive manufacturing, just as they were a couple of decades ago. 
Using historical data on investment in IT equipment, we contrast recent 
developments in robotics with the evolution of IT investment in the 
past. Robots in 1995 accounted for a larger share of firm investment 
than IT equipment did in 1980. Yet investment in IT was exploding in 
the late 1980s and early 1990s, and by the year 2000 it constituted

The results from our cross-country analysis show that industrial 
robots  do  not  account  for  a  significant  share  of  capital  stock.  Yet 
data limitations and identification challenges prevent us from using 
the cross-country setting to credibly examine one of the most pressing 
questions in the automation debate: the relation between robots and 
labor markets. To do that, we turn to firm-level data from Germany and 
study two questions: How does labor scarcity affect firms’ investment 
in automation, and how does automation affect employment?

To study the impact of labor scarcity on automation adoption, we 
introduce explicit measures of labor scarcity based on firms’ reports of 
recruitment and staffing challenges in the IAB survey of German estab-
lishments. These measures combine firms’ survey responses with actual 
hiring decisions and, as we demonstrate, represent meaningful differ-
ences in difficulties in hiring workers, firms’ recruitment outcomes, and 
employment growth. Regressing firm-level measures of investment in 
automation on several measures of labor scarcity reveals that firms 
have a higher adoption of automation when they face difficulties in 
finding  suitable  workers.2  This  association  is  robust  to  using  local 
labor scarcity and limiting the analysis to exporters in an attempt to 
alleviate endogeneity concerns about local demand. These and further 
results suggest that robotization may help firms alleviate labor scarcity 
problems, in particular those concerning qualified blue-collar workers, 
rather than displace existing workers. Hence, even if robotization is 
associated with lower levels of employment, the number of incumbent 
workers actually losing their jobs, and hence negative consequences of 
job loss, is limited.

To complete the analysis of the link between labor markets and 
robotization, we study the evolution of employment in firms directly 
adopting robots and in industries and areas most exposed to automa-
tion. Our analysis shows that firms adopting robots also significantly 
increase  their  employment,  and  hence  any  direct  displacement  ef-
fect appears to be limited. It is possible, however, that the negative 
employment effects documented in the existing literature are driven 
by  employment  declines  in  other  firms.  To  test  that,  we  employ  a 
difference-in-differences specification across industries and local areas. 
We exploit industry-level variation in robotization intensity and area-
level variation in the level of technology adoption, complementing the 
existing literature, which relies only on the industry-level variation. 
While  we  find  evidence  for  negative  employment  effects  of  roboti-
zation, the magnitudes of these effects are modest. Back-of-envelope 
calculations suggest that over the 10-year period between 2005 and 
2015, robotization contributed to a ∼0.3% slower employment growth 
in industries and regions with higher exposure. This estimate is ob-
tained based on a difference-in-differences framework and hence may 
reflect the growth of non-adopting industries. We find suggestive ev-
idence  that  the  limited  decline  in  employment  is  accompanied  by 
increasing labor market concentration, consistent with the growth of 
adopting firms at the expense of non-adopters.

Overall, our results suggest that the impact of robots on firm balance 
sheets  and  on  labor  markets  is  likely  to  be  limited.  They  do  not 
imply, however, that technological change as a whole does not play 
an important role in labor markets. Instead, we argue that the limited 
effects of robots on investment and labor markets are driven by the 
nature of this technology. Because robots are not widely prevalent, lack 
the potential for an extensive use across a broad range of sectors, and 
have spurred limited complementary innovations, they do not resemble 
other  historically  impactful  general  purpose  technologies,  such  as  the 
steam  engine  or  electricity.  In  contrast,  other  modern  technologies 
that  are  more  widespread  and  prevalent  than  robots  are  likely  to 
have more important economic consequences. Digital technologies such 
as advances in data processing, cloud computing, generative AI, and 
network communication are more prevalent, experience higher rates of 
improvements, and are more likely to generate complementary innova-
tions, which is why they have a larger economic impact. We provide 
evidence that investment in these digital technologies is much larger 
and more widespread than investment in robots, even in automotive 
manufacturing. At the same time, we demonstrate that the implications 
of this investment are not necessarily the same as those of robots and 
automation.

Recent papers highlight the sizable impact of robots on rising ag-
gregate productivity (Graetz and Michaels, 2018) and displacing work-
ers (Acemoglu and Restrepo, 2020). Other studies argue that the link 
between  automation  and  employment  is  more  nuanced:  robots  can 
cause a large displacement of production workers, but employment 
may grow in other occupations (Dauth et al., 2021; Humlum, 2019); 
and while employment effects of robots and other narrowly defined 
automation technologies may be negative, technology, more broadly 
defined, has had a positive impact on employment in the recent past 
(Aghion et al., 2022; Hirvonen et al., 2021). All these findings are 
consistent with a general theoretical framework, in which technology 
can have both displacement and productivity effects, and the magnitudes 
of these effects, and thus the extent to which the technology is displacing
or enabling labor, vary by the type of worker (Acemoglu and Restrepo, 
2018; Agrawal et al., 2019).

Nonetheless,  a  key  concern  in  the  debate  about  technological
change is that the displacement effect has became much stronger in 
recent years, as showcased by robotization, and that its importance 
continues to grow rapidly.3 Our findings speak to that concern in two 
ways. Our first contribution is quantifying the importance of robots 
in firms’ investment and contrasting it with other technologies. We 
show that the current magnitude of robot investment is small both in 
absolute terms and in comparison to other digital technologies and that 
recent trends in developed countries do not suggest that robotization is 
rapidly accelerating. These findings suggest that the economic impact 
of robots is likely smaller than is often presented.4 Second, using data 
from Germany, which offers a good setting to measure and identify 
the employment effects of robots in a novel way, we show that firms 
adopt automation when they cannot find workers. As a consequence, 
direct employment losses due to robotization are limited, while the 
total employment effects are negative but small.5

Our paper also highlights the importance of studying patterns of 
firms’ investment in technology adoption. We find that robot adopters 
are generally more productive and increase employment following the 
adoption (consistent with Koch et al. (2021)) and that investment in 
automation is higher for firms that are facing higher labor shortages. 
Hence, robots may allow firms to grow even if they have difficulties 
filling vacant positions and may not necessarily lead to a displace-
ment of existing workers.6 At the same time, our results improve the 
understanding of the determinants of firm investment and its implica-
tions, in particular regarding new technologies (Babina et al., 2024), 
demonstrating that firm investment is also shaped by labor constraints.

2. Data and measures of technology

We provide a brief description of the data and document basic facts 
about the key variables analyzed in the two empirical settings of the 
paper.

2.1. Cross-country analysis

We combine firm-level financial data from Amadeus with measures 
of robotization by industry, country, and year. The robotics data are 
novel and are introduced to economic literature by Graetz and Michaels 
(2018). The data come from the International Federation of Robotics 
(IFR, see IFR (2017)), which collects sales reports from robot man-
ufacturers and covers over 90% of the global robotics market. The 
manufacturers, which are located primarily in Japan, Switzerland, and 
Germany, report the number of robots sold at the country-industry-
year level, and the flow in each year is then used to calculate the 
stock  of  robots  in  operation.  Most  of  the  26  industries  for  which 
data  are  available  correspond  to  industries  defined  on  a  two-digit 
level in standard classifications such as NACE, although within some 
manufacturing industries, such as automotive, the classification is finer, 
whereas  outside  of  manufacturing  it  is  much  coarser.  Some  robots 
in the reports cannot be assigned to a specific industry. We allocate 
unclassified robots to industries based on the empirical distribution of 
classified robots.

The data begin in 1993 for a few European countries and end in 
2016. Over the years, the coverage of different markets has significantly 
improved. Industry-level data for North America are available since 
2004.  The  IFR  does  not  report  information  on  robot  prices  in  the 
same way as it does for units sold. Nonetheless, it does provide an 
approximation for average robot price, which we use to calculate the 
value of investment in robots.

In  the  data,  a  robot  is  defined  as  ‘‘an  automatically  controlled, 
reprogrammable, and multipurpose (machine)’’, which means that it is 
fully autonomous and can perform several tasks. This definition is well 
aligned with the official definition of ‘‘robot’’ by the International Or-
ganization for Standardization (ISO), which considers industrial robots

We supplement the IFR data with additional data sources. Among 
them, the EU KLEMS database (Jäger, 2016) contains information on

period 1995–2015, although some variables go as far back as 1970s. 
We use employment hours to calculate per-full-time-equivalent-worker

For most figures, we merge IFR and EU KLEMS data to meaningfully 
compute robot density. For country-specific results in the aggregate 
analysis of robotization (Section 3), we include nine developed coun-
tries for which all variables are available in both data sources: Austria, 
Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Sweden, 
and  the  United  States.  Merging  IFR  and  EU  KLEMS  data  requires 
harmonizing their industry classification, which we do by aggregat-

agriculture; mining; several manufacturing groups: food, beverage and 
tobacco; textiles, and leather; wood and paper; chemicals; metal; elec-

construction; utilities; and research and development.

We combine IFR and EU KLEMS data with firm-level data from 
Amadeus  and  use  data  for  almost  55,000  ‘‘large’’  or  ‘‘very  large’’ 
manufacturing firms from 23 European countries.7 The data comprise 
an unbalanced panel, with each firm having at most 10 observations; 
for most firms, these are the most recent available years that can be 
merged to robots’ data, that is, 2008–2016.

2.2. Firm-level analysis within Germany

In the second part of the paper, we rely on data from the Institute for 
Employment Research (IAB) of the German Employment Agency, which 
administers several data sets based on social security records and other 
complementary data collection efforts.

The two main data sets we use are the IAB Establishment Panel 
(IAB-EP) and the Establishment History Panel (BHP).8 Even though the 
two data sets are provided by the same provider, researchers are not 
allowed to freely merge them, and hence our analysis is conducted 
within one or the other. We are, however, allowed to merge in the 
aggregate data on technology adoption and labor scarcity, computed 
in IAB-EP, to the BHP data, where we conduct our employment effects 
analysis.

IAB-EP is a yearly survey of a stratified random sample of German 
establishments. The data contain rich information about firms’ per-
sonnel, investments, business policies, research and development, and 
other areas of operations. Although some variables are available in ev-
ery year, others are present for only a subset of years. In particular, our 
main automation and digitization measures are included in the 2016 
and 2017 waves of the panel, whereras the count of industrial robots 
is included only in 2019. The survey is merged with administrative 
personnel records, which contain information about firms’ workforce 
size and structure.

IAB-EP  data  are  collected  annually  by  the  German  Employment 
Agency by interviewing approximately 16,000 establishments. In a typ-
ical  year,  over  80%  of  the  sample  consists  of  continuers—
establishments that have participated in previous surveys. The remain-
ing 20% are new establishments that are randomly drawn from the 
pool  of  all  establishments  in  Germany  that  employed  at  least  one 
person subject to social security contributions as of June of the previous 
year. The population, therefore, excludes establishments run by self-
employed  owners  without  any  employees  and  some  public  entities 
that only employ civil servants who are not part of the regular social 
security system. Sampling weights are stratified by state (16 states), 
sector (6 sectors, later 19), and firm size bin (9 bins). To ensure a large 
enough sample size within each cell, the sampling overweights larger 
establishments, smaller states, and smaller industries. Cross-ownership 
does not play a role in sampling, that is, two different establishments 
are equally likely to be interviewed even if they are owned by the same 
parent firm.

Interviews are conducted by professional interviewers employed at 
Kantar, a private polling firm. The interviews typically last between 
35  and  55  min,  with  over  80%  conducted  entirely  face-to-face.  In 
the remaining cases, interviewers leave the questionnaire at the estab-
lishment for later collection—usually when respondents need time to 
gather additional information or consult colleagues to provide accu-
rate answers. For similar reasons, some questions remain unanswered 
(item  non-response  usually  does  not  exceed  1%  of  the  sample)  or 
responses contain no substantive information (e.g., “Difficult to say”), 
even though all questions are, in principle, required. The questions that 
are newly added to the survey are first tested in a cognitive pretest, 
where a separate sample of 100 establishments answers them to detect 
any potential errors or sources of confusion.

rate of 60%–70% per year. The response rate is often close to 85% 
for establishments that filled the survey in prior years, in particular 
with the same interviewer. As with any survey, nonresponse may raise 
concerns about selection bias; however, the high response rate reduces 
this risk in the case of IAB-EP. Bossler et al. (2018) examine the rep-

The BHP is a large, firm-level data set containing information for 
a 50% random sample of all German establishments. It covers over 2 
million establishments during the years 1976–2016 and contains yearly 
snapshots of firms’ personnel structure and wage information, based 
on social security records. The data include establishment location and

Our measures of automation come from three questions included 
in three waves of the IAB-EP: 2016, 2017, and 2019. In 2016, the data 
contained an extra set of questions asking firms about ‘‘automation and 
digitization’’ technologies that were defined as ‘‘autonomous robotics, 
smart factories, Internet of Things, big data analytics, cloud computing, 
[and] online platforms, among other technologies’’. Respondents were 
asked to assess familiarity with and the potential and current adoption 
of the technologies on a scale from 1 to 10 (with the option ‘‘Difficult 
to say’’): (A) How intensively has the establishment dealt with this 
topic so far? (B) What potential is there for application of such tech-
nologies in the establishment? and (C) How well is the establishment 
equipped with these technologies compared to other establishments in 
the sector?9

In 2017, the IAB-EP did not include these questions, but it added 
other measures of automation and digitization. In particular, firms were 
asked whether they use different classes of technology. These tech-

well as other digital technologies, including data processing and net-

3. Robot investment around the world

We next analyze aggregate patterns of investment in robots using 

robot data from the IFR and firm-level data from Amadeus.

3.1. Magnitudes and basic patterns of robot investment

Fig.  2 shows the evolution of the number of robots sold over time. 
The total number of robots shipped increased significantly from about 
100,000 per year between 2000 and 2010 to 300,000 in 2016, leading 
to  the  perception  of  a  rapid  acceleration  in  automation.  Although 
robot sales declined during the Global Financial Crisis, they rebounded 
between 2011 and 2016, and the positive trend grew stronger. Yet, 
as demonstrated in the figure, the perception of rapidly accelerating 
automation may be misleading from the perspective of European and 
North American countries. The growth in robot sales is fueled predom-
inantly by growth in other countries. In the West, the growth in robot 

installations is more modest: it has not accelerated in recent years but 
merely returned to its pre-crisis trend after a dip in 2009.

China accounts for almost half of the 2006–2016 growth: China 
installed slightly over 5,000 new robots in 2006 compared to almost 
90,000 in 2016. Appendix Figure A.3 illustrates the sales of robots 
shipped to the eight countries with the most robots in use in 2016. 
China saw the most dramatic increase in the number of robots shipped 
and  by  2013  surpassed  Japan  as  the  country  with  the  most  robot 
installations. South Korea, with its vibrant automotive sector, also saw 
a substantial growth pattern, especially in the final two years. At the 
same time, the growth in robot shipments is significantly lower in other 
countries, including the United States, Germany, and Italy.

The number of robots sold is not a sufficient statistic to compare 
robots and other investment. In Panel A of Fig.  3 we attempt to measure 
the value – rather than number – of robots. The figure demonstrates 
the evolution of the value of robot shipments compared to total capital 
formation and investment in equipment. Guided by IFR estimates, we 
assume  that  the  average  price  of  a  robot  is  $45,000  and  use  that 
estimate combined with the number of robots sold to calculate total 
investment in robotics. We use a single fixed price of a robot because 
there are no good data on robot prices. Data for earlier periods analyzed 
by Graetz and Michaels (2018) suggest that robot prices were declining, 
despite economy-wide inflation. We take a conservative approach and 
assume  a  constant  price.  This  approach  is  certainly  crude,  and  we 
return to the measurement question below. Given the basic measure, 
however, our estimates suggest that robots constitute 0.2–0.3% of total 
investment in equipment and less than 0.1% of total capital formation. 
Their share in total investment is relatively stable between 1995 and 
2005 and increases by almost 50% between 2005 and 2016. However, 
as demonstrated in Fig.  2, this growth is largely fueled by developing 
countries.

Panel B of Fig.  3 demonstrates the share of robots in total investment 
across industry groups in the years 1995–1997 and 2013–2015. Robots 
are very strongly concentrated in manufacturing, and the bar for non-
manufacturing is too small to be legible. A small number of robots 
can  be  found  in  the  agriculture,  mining,  and  R&D  sectors,  but  the 
vast majority of the economy does not use robots.12 Even within man-
ufacturing, moreover, robots are concentrated in selected industries,

columns 4 and 5 demonstrate that this effect is driven predominantly 
by large firms.

In Table  4 we compare the regression-implied estimates of robot 
investment with the value of shipments from the IFR. On average, our 
estimates suggest that the impact on a firm’s CapEx is in between the 
estimates implied by robot prices alone and those that triple robot 
prices to include peripherals. For the automotive industry, regression-
based estimates in the last column of Table  4 suggest that robot-related 
expenditures represent 15.3% of total CapEx, which is in line with 
the 16.7% figure implied by assuming that peripherals triple the cost 
of a robot purchase. However, for manufacturing as a whole, robots 
correspond to around 2.1% of total CapEx, which is similar to the 
∼2.4$ (7.1%) estimated share of robots in investment based on robot 
price only. Given that manufacturing constitutes around 15% of the 
economy on average and that robotization levels are very low in other 
sectors, this suggests a 0.3% share in total investment in equipment, 
again consistent with our price-based estimates for the whole economy 
(Section 3.1). These calculations suggest that investments complemen-
tary to robots may be substantial in some industries, but in total are

4. Firm investment in automation and labor scarcity

The  findings  presented  so  far  suggest  that  the  role  of  robots  in 
transforming the economy has been modest, contrasting some views 
in the literature (Graetz and Michaels, 2018; Acemoglu and Restrepo, 
2020). Yet this evidence does not address the central question of the 
debate, that is, the impact of robots on the recent past and future 
of work. Examining the link between robotization and labor markets 
is difficult because of measurement and identification challenges. To 
explore it in a more appropriate setting, we turn to analyzing firm-
level data from Germany, where we can more credibly explore the 
link between firm-level investment in automation and labor market 
conditions and outcomes.

We start by presenting several facts that complement the aggregate 
patterns discussed in Section 3. We then turn to the analysis of the 
relationship between labor scarcity and automation adoption. One of 
the most debated aspects of automation is the potential displacement 
of existing workers, in particular, older workers. Job loss, especially 
after a long job tenure, may have far-reaching negative consequences 
for affected individuals and their communities (Paul et al., 2018). In 
light  of  these  concerns,  it  is  important  to  understand  whether  any 
potential negative employment effect of automation represents actual 
displacement of existing workers or whether automation is a way of 
enabling firm growth with lower hiring intensity. Even though both

4.4. Automation and labor scarcity: Addressing endogeneity concerns

The results presented in columns 1 to 7 of Table  6 are subject to 
endogeneity concerns. First, there is a concern about reverse causality: 
a firm that adopts new, sophisticated technology may have trouble 
finding workers because the skills required to operate the technology 
are scarce. Second, there is a concern about omitted variables: firms 
that adopt new technology more intensely may be different in a way 
that is unobserved. Typically we would expect such firms to be more 
productive  and  successful  than  other  firms.  If  such  firms  are  more 
attractive to workers and hence have fewer recruiting difficulties, the 
Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) coefficient may be downward biased. But 
those firms may also have higher demand for their products, which can 
be accommodated both by hiring more workers (and thus having more 
problems finding them) and by technology adoption, introducing an 
upward bias to OLS coefficients.

To alleviate these concerns, we use a labor scarcity measure that 
is not specific to the firm but captures labor market conditions in the

To complete the picture of the relationship between robots and 
labor  markets,  we  move  to  the  analysis  of  employment  effects  of 
robots. We start by directly analyzing the evolution of employment 
in firms that invest in robots. Although this analysis is informative 
about the potential magnitude of a direct employment effect, it does not

5.1. Employment in firms adopting automation

We start by analyzing employment changes in automation-adopting 
firms. Table  7 presents the relation between measures of automation 
adoption and employment changes. The four measures of automation 
that we use vary in specificity: the broadest is the adoption of automa-
tion and digitization that firms report in the survey. The advantage of 
this measure is its intensive margin character and inclusion of various 
technology classes. The latter, however, may also be a downside. To 
focus on a narrower definition of automation, we interact the adoption 
intensity measure with the 2017 indicator for using robots, use the 
indicator  itself,  or  use  the  five-year  change  in  robot  density  based 
on 2019 measures of the number of robots used. The downside of 
the last measure is that it is highly skewed and positive for only a 
small number of establishments, including few establishments outside 
of manufacturing. To minimize the impact of outliers, we winsorize the 
change in robot density at the 1st and 99th percentiles, and include 
only manufacturing firms in the sample used in column 6.

Across all measures, we find a positive and statistically significant 
association between the adoption of automation and firm-level employ-
ment growth. A one standard deviation increase in the intensity of

5.2. Employment effects by local area and industry

Patterns documented in Table  7 suggest that the direct negative 
employment effects in firms that automate are limited. Yet it is pos-
sible that automation causes employment losses in other firms, which 
downsize  in  response  to  their  competitors  becoming  more  efficient 
through automation. Indeed, such a possibility would be consistent 
with the results obtained for French firms in Acemoglu et al. (2020). 
In  columns  3  and  4  of  Appendix  Table  A.4,  we  demonstrate  that 
while  firms’  own  adoption  of  robots  is  significantly  and  positively 
correlated with the growth of employment, the adoption of robots by 
other firms in the local area has a negative yet insignificant correlation 
with the local firm’s employment growth. These results are consistent 
with employment spillovers from robot adoption being negative but 
small. However, given that we observe adoption for only a subsample 
of German firms and hence cannot comprehensively measure ‘‘other 
firms’  adoption’’,  the  specification  has  limitations  in  terms  of  both 
identification and sample size. We attempt to shed more light on the 
potential negative effects of robots on employment among all firms by 
performing the analysis on the industry-area level in a data set that 
contains comprehensive measure of employment in German economy 
and exploiting industry-level variation in the levels of robotization and 
local-area-level variation in the intensity of automation adoption (Fig. 
6).

Methodology. To estimate the impact of robotization on employment, 
we  combine  industry-level  measures  of  robotization  with  firm-level 
administrative data from Germany. We use the number of robots per 

1000 workers, based on robot shipments data from the IFR. The vari-
ation  in  this  measure  across  industries  can  be  used  to  analyze  the 
employment  effects  of  technology,  as  demonstrated  by  Graetz  and 
Michaels (2018) and Acemoglu and Restrepo (2020). However, if all the 
identifying variation is at the industry level, it is difficult to disentangle 
the effects of technology from other industry-level changes that might 
be correlated with technology adoption. To address this concern, we 
combine  variation  in  the  intensity  of  robotization  across  industries 
with variation in technology adoption levels across local areas. This 
allows us to use within-industry variation and identify the effect of 
technology  by  comparing  firms  in  high-adoption  areas  to  those  in 
low-adoption areas. The geographic variation in adoption is captured 
by local area19 measures of intensity of automation and digitization 
adoption, computed as the average of firm-level measures from the 
IAB-EP.

We use data from the BHP – an administrative data set with informa-
tion on 50% of German establishments – and aggregate up employment 
information to the industry-area level. The main empirical specification

Comparison with Other Studies. Our estimates are based on a novel 
strategy  that  combines  industry-level  measures  of  automation  with 
local  area  intensity  of  adoption.  Given  our  findings  that  robotiza-
tion varies mostly by industry and is nonnegligible for only a small 
number of manufacturing industries, any identification strategy that 
relies solely on industry-level data may face significant challenges. In 
particular, one can be worried that what is believed to be the effect 
of automation (see, e.g., Graetz and Michaels (2018), Acemoglu and 
Restrepo (2020), Dauth et al. (2021)) is instead a reflection of a general 
decline in manufacturing, which is likely to have other reasons as well. 
In light of this concern, our ability to augment industry-level data with 
local area variation seems valuable.

At the same time, because our analysis relies on a comparison of 
different local areas, some other endogeneity concerns may arise. It 
is important to clarify that we do not need to assume that high- and 
low-adoption areas are the same except for the levels of technology 
adoption. Instead, we include area fixed effects with the goal of cap-
turing all time-invariant area-specific factors other than technology. 
The  key  identifying  assumption  is  that  the  effects  of  these  factors 
do not vary across industries in a systematic way that is correlated 
with robotization. Our strategy is similar to the strategy used at the 
country-industry  level  by  Rajan  and  Zingales  (1998).  One  concern 
about the strategy is related to agglomeration effects affecting differ-
ent industries in different ways. Over time, high-skill industries may 
become more likely to be located in selected business hubs compared to 
manufacturing firms, which could bias our estimates. Our employment 
effects also hold when controlling for past employment changes and 
hence  are  unlikely  to  be  driven  by  differential  employment  trends 
across industry-area pairs. In addition, the analysis of adoption patterns 
across Germany (Fig.  6) reveals that many areas with high adoption 
(e.g., northeastern Bavaria and western Lower Saxony) are not typical 
business  centers.  Last,  the  differential  importance  of  agglomeration 
effects can be driven to a large extent by technology, and hence it 
may  be  viewed  as  a  mechanism  through  which  technology  affects 
employment, rather than as an alternative explanation.

Yet, ultimately, the novel strategy that we propose has both ad-
vantages  and  disadvantages,  and  it  is  unclear  how  it  compares  to 
an  alternative  strategy  that  relies  on  industry-level  variation  only. 
Importantly, however, our broad conclusions do not rely on using the 
particular strategy that we introduce and can also be obtained when 
employing methods used by other papers. In particular, Acemoglu and 
Restrepo (2020) for United States and Dauth et al. (2021) for Germany 
use a strategy that calculates local labor market exposure to robotics 
based on industry composition. The change in that measure over time 
can then be related to changes in employment to recover estimates of 
the employment effects of robots.

The  exact  comparison  of  the  employment  effects  based  on  our 
estimates to those obtained in the two other studies may be problematic 
because our strategies and theirs lie in a different place on the spectrum 
between partial and general equilibrium analysis. However, back-of-

5.3. Connecting labor scarcity, automation, and employment

Our analysis suggests that firms facing difficulties in hiring work-
ers  increasingly  turn  to  automation.  These  firms  continue  to  grow 
their workforce, but at the same time, total employment in affected 
industries and regions exhibits a modest decline. To understand this 
pattern, it is important to recall that in any dynamic economy, jobs 
are constantly being created and destroyed. When these two processes 
are in balance, total employment remains stable despite continuous 
churn. Our findings suggest that the combination of labor scarcity and 
automation can tilt this balance.

To illustrate a possible interpretation of the underlying mechanism, 
consider a stylized example. Suppose Firm A receives a positive produc-
tivity shock. In a labor-abundant environment, Firm A would respond 
by hiring five workers – potentially absorbing workers laid off from 
another firm, say Firm B – leaving total employment unchanged. How-
ever, when labor is scarce (e.g., due to a mismatch in skills between 
workers leaving Firm B and those needed by Firm A), Firm A may be 
able to hire only three workers. Without access to automation, this la-
bor constraint would force Firm A to forgo some growth opportunities. 
With automation, however, Firm A can augment its workforce with 
one robot and still expand output, hiring three workers and deploying 
a robot instead of hiring five workers. In this scenario, employment 
at  Firm  A  increases  by  three  (rather  than  five),  while  Firm  B  still 

reduces its workforce by five, resulting in a net employment decline of 
two workers. The choice between hiring more workers or investing in 
automation is endogenous and shaped by relative costs. When labor is 
costly or difficult to find, automation becomes more attractive — thus, 
labor scarcity induces automation. As a result, total employment may 
decline, even though automating firms continue to grow and become 
larger than non-adopters.

Although the example is intentionally simplified, the relevance and 
magnitude  of  these  mechanisms  are  matters  for  empirical  analysis. 
Consistent with this framework, we find that labor scarcity increases 
the likelihood of automation (Table  6), that adopting firms expand their 
employment (Table  7), and that total employment in affected industries 
and regions declines slightly (Table  8).

Data limitations prevent us from merging micro-level information 
across data sets and thus from fully disentangling employment pat-
terns of adopters and non-adopters using Eq.  (2) in the BHP sample. 
However, a complementary analysis using the IAB-EP data set – where 
both adoption and employment can be observed at the establishment 
level  –  yields  supportive  evidence.  Appendix  Table  A.4  shows  that 
adopting firms experience statistically significant employment growth, 
while non-adopters in the same markets show insignificant declines. 
This divergence is consistent with reallocation effects, where adopters 
capture a greater share of the local labor market. Yet the magnitudes of 
the reallocation may be limited due to the limited prevalence of robots.
These patterns may also contribute to increased market concen-
tration.  Appendix  Table  A.8  shows  that  the  average  establishment 
size  rises  in  industry-region  cells  more  exposed  to  automation.  Re-
lated analysis suggests that the number of firms may decline and that 
employment concentration – measured by the Herfindahl–Hirschman 
Index (HHI) – may increase, consistent with reduced competition. That 
said, these concentration effects are estimated imprecisely.

Further, we assess whether labor scarcity mediates the employment 
impact of automation. When we control for labor scarcity by interacting 
location fixed effects with quartiles of scarcity (Appendix Table A.3, 
columns 9 and 10), the estimated employment effect of automation 
becomes smaller. However, the already small baseline coefficients leave 
little room for statistically significant changes.

Finally,  we  examine  whether  automation  helps  alleviate  labor
scarcity. Appendix Table A.5 shows that reported labor scarcity declines 
more  significantly  in  firms  with  higher  levels  of  automation.  This 
suggests that automation allows firms to scale up operations with a 
smaller expansion of their workforce.

the broader macroeconomic impact of automation remains limited—
consistent with the still modest prevalence of robots across firms and 
industries.

6. The limited effects of robots compared to other technologies

We  next  discuss  the  lack  of  a  significant  impact  of  robotics  by 
analyzing the characteristics of robotization and contrast its prevalence 
and impact with other technologies. We argue that since modern digital 
technologies are more widespread than robots, they are likely to have 
a larger impact in the labor market.

6.1. The nature of robotics technology and its limited economic impact

Historically, technologies such as the steam engine, electricity, and 
computers have had substantial impacts on the economy. Due to their 
transformative role, they are often classified as General Purpose Tech-
nologies (GPT) because they were widely used, were capable of ongoing 
technical improvement, and enabled innovation in multiple application 
sectors (Helpman, 1988; Jovanovic and Rousseau, 2005; Bresnahan, 
2010). We argue that the limited economic impact of robots reflects the 
fact that robots do not fully share these characteristics. While robots 
clearly  are  capable  of  further  technical  improvement,  they  are  not 
widely used and have had limited impact on innovation across the 
economy.

The ability to ignite complementary innovation and improve busi-
ness processes in multiple application sectors is a key characteristic 
of influential enabling technologies. For example, the introduction of 
electricity, besides enabling the creation of multiple new consumer 
products and increasing the comfort of households, has substantially 
changed the production process in manufacturing: working hours could 
now be extended, since daylight was no longer a limitation, and arti-
ficial light became safer and brighter compared to earlier methods of 
illumination, such as gas lamps. Complementary innovation, such as the 
electrical telegraph, soon followed and revolutionized communications. 
Today robots play a substantial role in welding and painting cars, but 
the  cars  they  produce  are  not  very  different  than  those  that  could 
be produced without robots. Although robots have enabled important 
innovations in some applications (e.g., robots may defuse explosives, 
significantly improving the safety of humans, or perform certain medi-
cal procedures with a precision unachievable by humans), the scope of 
such innovations appears limited so far.

Although robots have a limited impact as an enabling technology, it 
is still possible for a displacing technology to be impactful through the 
reallocation of resources that it frees. Mechanization of agriculture in 
the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries played such a role, but 
the critical prerequisite for this effect was the fact that agricultural sec-
tor employed over 60% of the workforce in the mid-nineteenth century. 
In contrast, the share of workers currently employed in manufacturing 
sectors with nonnegligible use of robots is in the single digits.22 Con-
sistent with the evidence in Sections 4 and 5, the employment effects 
of robots are too small to lead to a significant reallocation of resources 
in the economy.

The impact of robotics can increase in the future. Robots today 
are more successful in industries such as automotive or metal manu-
facturing, which require operations on large, solid objects in a stable 
environment, while being less successful in applications, such as the

construction  sector,  that  require  more  flexibility.  It  is  possible  that

estimates presented in Fig.  2 do not suggest that the tasks that robots 
can perform have been expanding significantly in recent decades. If that 
were the case, we should see an increasing adoption of robotization

in  industries  that  currently  have  few  robots.  Instead,  however,  we 
see the majority of adoption in countries that are catching up to the 
advanced  economies  and  in  industries  that  have  traditionally  been 
highly robotized, such as the automotive sector.

6.2. The prevalence of robots and other technologies

Do other modern technologies exhibit different characteristics and 
are  thus  more  likely  to  have  greater  impact?  Digital  technologies 
certainly seem to be more prevalent than robotics. Fig.  7 displays the 
evolution of expenditures on robots, ICT equipment, and software and 
databases between 1995 and 2015. The data used in the figure are 
based on the aggregate variables from IFR and EU KLEMS data. We 
present the data graphically for the manufacturing sector because the 
share of robots would be too small to be legible on the graph for 
the whole economy. The figure demonstrates that digital technologies 
are  much  more  prevalent  than  robots.  Even  within  manufacturing, 
where robots are most common, expenditures on robots are dwarfed by 
spending on ICT and software and databases. There does not seem to be 
a reversal of this trend. Although the relative growth rate of software 
and databases expenditure is lower than that for robotics, the former 
have much a higher base, and hence the difference between the two 
types of capital is widening.

Furthermore, even the difference in the relative growth rates may 
disappear when robotization levels increase. This is consistent with the 
evidence from the automotive industry – which has much higher rates 
of robotization than any other industry – presented in Appendix Figure 
A.11. Robot investment remained flat in the last decade, suggesting that 
when robotization reaches a relatively high level, the growth is likely 
to be lower. In contrast, we observe a stable growth of the expenditures 
on software and databases, even though this category is much larger to 
begin with.

6.3. Comparison of the effects of robots and other technologies

It  is  possible  that  even  if  industrial  robots  themselves  are  not 
transforming the economy and do not account for a significant share 
of  total  investment,  their  impact  may  be  similar  to  that  of  other 
more widespread technologies. Therefore, studying robots may provide 
important insights that are more broadly applicable.

Robots certainly share similarities with other technologies. At its 
core, automation means that a task previously performed by humans 
is done by machines, whether that machine is an industrial robot or a 
computer algorithm. However, there are important differences between 
these technologies. With a fluid definition of a task, a large group of 
technologies can be described as automation. For example, an Excel 
spreadsheet may automate the task of manually adding numbers, yet 
at the same time it creates a new task of data entry. More important, 
it may significantly increase the productivity of bookkeepers, allowing 
them to perform more tasks in the same amount of time. Thus, even 
though they share similarities, the aggregate effect of algorithms may 
be very different from the impact of robots because they differ in their 
extent of replacement and productivity effects.

those used in our analysis of robots (see Appendix Figure A.1), we 
analyze the impact of labor scarcity on the adoption of digitization 
— defined as high adoption of data- and network-related technologies 
(based on measures from 2016 and 2017 waves of IAB-EP). A posi-

The  results  reveal  a  heterogeneous  effect  of  digital  technologies 
across different industries. Although robots are rare outside of man-

in these industries are not directly related to robotics. Yet in other 
industries  (finance,  education  and  health,  professional  services)  we 
observe the opposite patterns, suggesting that the complementarity ef-
fects of technology in these industries dominate over robot-resembling 
substitution effects.

The heterogeneous effects of the relation between labor scarcity and 
technology adoption suggest that the employment effects of different 
technologies may also differ from the employment effects of robots. 
Table  9 analyzes this question directly and reports digitization coef-
ficients analogous to robotization effects shown in Table  8. We find no 
evidence of the negative employment effects of digital technologies. In 
fact, we find some suggestive evidence for positive employment effects: 
all coefficients are positive, although only some of them are statistically 
significant.

Overall,  the  evidence  suggests  that  other  technologies  are  more 
prevalent than robotics and can have different economic implications. 
These findings indicate that we should exert caution when analyzing 
the impact of robotics and extrapolating their measured impact to other 
technologies.

7. Conclusion

This paper critically examines the importance of industrial robots 
for firm investment and for labor markets. We confirm that firms are 
steadily  increasing  their  investment  in  robotization  and  that  robots 
have some potential to reduce employment, yet we demonstrate that 
their  share  in  total  investment  is  small  and  that  their  employment 
effects are modest and mitigated by endogenous adoption patterns. 

Industrial  robots  are  spectacular  technology,  but  their  applications 
remain limited. Although their usage has been growing in recent years, 
this growth has been relatively stable, and the pattern does not resem-
ble the explosive growth observed, for example, for IT technologies in 
previous decades.

Instead, other digital technologies are much more prevalent than 
robots today, and recent trends appear to further widen that difference. 
Due to their higher prevalence, digital technologies related to data 
processing  and  analysis  and  network  communications  are  likely  to 
have much bigger economic implications, and their employment effects 
are likely to be significantly different from the impact of robots. We 
certainly can learn something about firm investment and labor markets 
by studying robots, but it is important not to extrapolate too far, given 
the different characteristics of these technologies.
