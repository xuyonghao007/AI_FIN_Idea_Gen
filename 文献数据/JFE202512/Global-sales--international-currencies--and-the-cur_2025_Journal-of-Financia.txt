We document that the currency denomination of the debt of large firms in developed countries is strongly 
associated with the geographical distribution of their sales. Furthermore, those firms exhibit significant home 
currency bias and international currency bias in borrowing: controlling for the geography of sales, they borrow 
more in their home currency and the two most traded currencies, the US dollar and the euro. We also show 
that the firms’ debt currency denomination choices are associated with export invoicing currency patterns in a 
way consistent with a currency hedging mechanism. In particular, firms domiciled in countries that invoice a 
larger share of their exports in either their home currency or in a vehicle currency exhibit a weaker connection 
between the currency denomination of their debt and the geography of their sales. Moreover, firms in countries 
that invoice more of their exports in an international currency are characterized by stronger international 
currency bias in debt issuance.

1. Introduction

A  large  body  of  the  international  finance  literature  studies  dif-
ferences in asset return properties that arise from differences in the 
currency denomination of cash flows. From an investor’s perspective, 
assets  that  pay  cash  flows  denominated  in  different  currencies  are 
generally imperfect substitutes, even if they are otherwise identical. 
However, the fact that assets are imperfect substitutes for investors 
does not necessarily mean that they are imperfect substitutes for their 
issuers. Indeed, in the absence of frictions, the composition of firm lia-
bilities should be irrelevant for firm value. This implies indeterminacy 
not only for the breakdown of total firm liabilities between debt and 
equity, but also for the currency of denomination of firms’ debt.

In this paper, we consider the currency composition of firm debt, 
focusing on two main questions: is there an association between the 
currency of denomination of firm debt and the geographical distri-
bution of firm sales, and, if so, does the debt currency mix exhibit 

systematic deviations from that relationship towards particular cur-
rencies? To answer these questions, we use annual debt outstanding 
and geographical sales data for fiscal years 2009 to 2019 on large 
firms headquartered in 13 developed countries that borrow in foreign 
currency in at least one fiscal year in our sample period. Our results 
suggest an affirmative answer to both questions, providing non-trivial 
insights regarding firm debt properties.

First, we uncover a strong relationship between the currency of 
denomination of debt and the geography of firm sales, not only on 
aggregate, but also when we consider countries and industries sepa-
rately: on average, an increase of a firm’s sales share corresponding 
to a given country by 1 percentage point is associated with a roughly 
0.4 percentage point increase in the firm’s share of debt denominated 
in the corresponding currency. We also document the existence of a 
positive relationship between interest rate differences and debt shares, 
indicating  more  borrowing  in  low-yielding  currencies,  other  things

Additionally, we find no association between the currency mix of firms’

those firms pay attention to the currency exposure of their sales when

Second, we document both home currency bias and international 
currency bias in debt shares: controlling for the geography of sales, 
firms  have  more  debt  denominated  in  their  home  currency  and  in 
the two major international currencies, the US dollar and the euro. 
In our main specification, the US dollar fixed effect is 15% and the 
euro fixed effect is 6% on average, suggesting that firms issue more 
than a fifth of their debt in the two international currencies even in 
the absence of any sales in the United States and the eurozone. We 
also find that, whereas the US dollar bias in borrowing has remained 
roughly steady since 2009, the euro bias has been steadily increasing 
over the past decade, an increase that is manifested in bond borrowing, 
but not in loans or short-term debt. On the other hand, we do find 
no substantial bias towards borrowing in non-international currencies. 
Finally, the home currency fixed effect is 49%, indicating that about 
half of the average firm’s borrowing is denominated in its domestic 
currency before even accounting for the additional effect of domestic 
sales. Thus, it appears that large firms exhibit the same biases as bond 
investors – as documented in Burger et al. (2018) and Maggiori et al. 
(2020), investor holdings are biased towards bonds denominated in 
either their domestic currency or in US dollars – which suggests that 
firms’ international currency bias in borrowing may be partially due 
to firms’ attempt to access global pools of bond funding. Consistent 
with that conjecture, when we consider different types of liabilities 
separately,  we  find  that  bond  issuance  exhibits  more  international 
currency bias, and less home currency bias, compared to loans and 
short-term debt.

Our two main findings continue to hold when we focus on the 
extensive margin of debt issuance. We find that, for the firms in our 
sample, the existence of sales in a given country is associated with a 
6% higher probability of having debt denominated in the corresponding 
currency. Furthermore, we find that the extensive margin is sensitive 
to  the  magnitude  of  sales:  a  higher  sales  share  in  a  given  country 
is associated with a higher likelihood that the firm borrows in the 
corresponding currency, consistent with the existence of fixed costs of 
borrowing. Finally, we show that the extensive margin of borrowing 
exhibits strong home currency and international currency biases.

While the dataset that we employ for our main analysis provides 
valuable insights, it does not provide information regarding the cur-
rency of invoicing of each firm’s sales. In an attempt to address that 
limitation, we use aggregate (country-level) export invoicing currency 
data  (see  Boz  et  al.  (2022))  to  proxy  for  firms’  currency  exposure 
arising from cross-border exports. Using that data, we show that firms 
domiciled in countries with a higher share of exports invoiced in either 
their home currency or in a vehicle currency exhibit a weaker link 
between the currency denomination of their debt and the geography of 
their sales, consistent with the currency hedging mechanism. We also 
document that firms that are domiciled in countries which invoice a 
larger share of their exports in international currencies exhibit a higher 
international currency bias in their debt shares, a finding that also 
aligns with the hedging hypothesis.

When we consider different cross-sections of firms, we find that 
larger firms and more international firms (i.e., firms with a higher share 
of international sales) have a significantly higher international currency 
debt  bias  and  a  lower  home  currency  bias.  Furthermore,  we  show

that even firms with a low share of foreign sales exhibit international 
currency bias when they borrow: when we focus on firms that earn at 
least 80% of their revenue domestically, we still find an 8% US dollar 
fixed effect and a 5% euro fixed effect for their debt shares.

Finally, in order to explore whether there are differences between 
developed country firms, which we consider in our analysis, and Latin 
American firms, which are the focus of some of the existent literature 
on the currency denomination of firm debt, we repeat our analysis 
for  large  Latin  American  firms.  We  find  that  those  firms  exhibit  a 
much weaker link between the currency mix of their debt and the 
geographical distribution of their sales: the sales share coefficient is 
only 0.25, about half the size of the corresponding developed country 
coefficient. Furthermore, while both the US dollar bias and the home 
currency bias are strong in Latin America, the euro bias is non-existent, 
underscoring the importance of US dollar financing for Latin American 
firms, consistent with the evidence in Alfaro et al. (2023) and Gutierrez 
et al. (2023).

In summary, our results provide insights into the currency compo-
sition of large firm debt, which constitutes a sizable proportion of the 
global supply of assets. For that reason, our paper is complementary to 
the literature that focuses on the forces that shape global asset demand. 
For example, both Burger et al. (2018) and Maggiori et al. (2020) 
illustrate the importance of the currency denomination of assets for 
investor demand. Relatedly, Du and Huber (2024) document that, post-
financial crisis, non-U.S. investors have increased both their portfolio 
allocation in USD-denominated assets and the degree to which they 
hedge their exposure to USD fluctuations. Since supply and demand 
shape the allocation of assets across country portfolios, our work is 
also related to the literature that explores the composition of countries’ 
foreign assets and liabilities. Seminal papers include Lane and Milesi-
Ferretti (2007), Curcuru et al. (2008, 2010, 2013) and Bénétrix et al. 
(2015).

Our paper also contributes to the literature that explores the prop-
erties of foreign currency borrowing by firms, and more broadly to the 
literature that focuses on firms’ financial hedging of foreign currency 
exposure.  Jorion  (1990)  finds  that  the  FX  exposure  of  the  equity 
value of US multinational corporations is positively associated with 
their share of foreign sales. Focusing on an international sample of 
firms, Adams and Verdelhan (2022) find that firms’ FX gains and losses 
exhibit  a  strong  pass-though  to  their  profitability  and  are  reflected 
on the foreign currency loadings of their equity returns. Allayannis 
and Ofek (2001) find that large US firms that have a higher share of 
foreign sales are more likely to issue foreign currency debt in order 
to hedge that exposure and, conditional on issuing, to issue a higher 
amount of such debt. Relatedly, Kedia and Mozumdar (2003) find that 
US firms with more operations abroad are more likely to issue foreign 
currency debt in order to hedge their exposure to foreign currency 
fluctuations, both at the aggregate and the individual currency level. 
Furthermore, Allayannis et al. (2001) show that operational hedging 
is not an effective substitute of financial hedging, defined as hedging 
using foreign currency debt or currency derivatives, and that US firms 
with higher geographical dispersion are more likely to use financial 
hedges. Finally, Alfaro et al. (2023) document that Chilean firms do 
limited operational hedging of their currency risk and improve cash 
flow management with the use of currency derivatives.

Regarding the impact of borrowing cost differences across curren-
cies, Liao (2020) shows that firm debt issuance responds to differences 
in the currency-hedged cost of debt, suggesting that firms act oppor-
tunistically when deciding the currency denomination of newly issued 
debt. Consistent with the existence of an opportunistic element in firms’ 
behavior, Gutierrez et al. (2023) attribute the popularity of US dollar 
debt for Peruvian firms to low USD loan rates charged by local banks.
As regards theoretical contributions, Michaux (2012) develops a 
model in which firms in a small open economy optimally issue debt in 
order to hedge their currency exposure, even if they are not exporting, 
as they may be indirectly exposed to currency risk via product market

productive firms issue more foreign currency debt, as they are better

Finally, our results relate to the literature on internationally domi-
nant currencies and, in particular, the US dollar. The special properties

2. Data and descriptive statistics

In this section, we describe our data and present the summary statis-
tics for our key measures, debt shares by currency of denomination and 
sales shares by country.

2.1. Data

For our main analysis, we use annual debt and geographical sales 
data  for  fiscal  years  2009  to  2019  on  firms  headquartered  in  one 
of the following developed countries: Australia, Canada, France, Ger-
many, Italy, Japan, New Zealand, Norway, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, 
United Kingdom and the United States. All those countries are char-
acterized by well-functioning capital markets and issue widely traded 
currencies. In our analysis, we group together all firms based in France, 
Germany,  Italy  and  Spain,  as  those  four  countries  are  part  of  the 
eurozone and, therefore, they have the same domestic currency. Fur-
thermore,  we  restrict  our  analysis  to  large  firms:  only  firms  with 
market capitalization of at least one billion US dollars (as of 2019) 
are  included  in  our  sample.  We  consider  debt  outstanding  in  the 
following eleven major currencies: Australian dollar (AUD), Canadian 
dollar  (CAD),  Swiss  franc  (CHF),  Chinese  yuan  (CNY),  euro  (EUR), 
British pound (GBP), Japanese yen (JPY), Norwegian krone (NOK), 
New Zealand dollar (NZD), Swedish krona (SEK), and US dollar (USD). 
The source of our data is FactSet Revere: Geographic Revenue Exposure 
(GeoRev) data for firm sales, and Debt Capital Structure Detail reports 
for outstanding firm debt. For our industry results, we sort firms into 
industries  using  the  Revere  Business  Industry  Classification  System 
(RBICS). Furthermore, we calculate interest rate differentials between 
currencies using sovereign 5-year zero-coupon bond yields, 5-year log 
FX  changes  using  spot  exchange  rates,  and  FX-hedged  interest  rate 
differentials using 5-year cross-currency basis swap spreads; all types of 
data are sourced from Bloomberg. In Internet Appendix A, we provide 
more details on the FactSet Revere data and the swap spread data.

Given our focus on understanding the drivers of the currency de-
nomination of debt, our benchmark sample of firms includes firms that 
only have some foreign currency debt outstanding during our sample 
period. Hence, we exclude firms that only borrow in their home cur-
rency. Furthermore, to avoid time-series fluctuations that arise solely 
from changes in the composition of our sample, we only consider firms 
for which we have data for all 11 fiscal years. In short, our benchmark 
sample includes the 778 firms in our dataset for which we have data 
for all 11 fiscal years and which have debt outstanding in at least one 
foreign currency in at least one fiscal year from 2009 to 2019. In our 
robustness section, we use annual debt and geographical sales data for 
169 large firms domiciled in Latin America (in particular, in Argentina, 
Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Mexico and Peru) that have foreign currency 
debt in at least one fiscal year between 2009 and 2019.

Our analysis also incorporates aggregate export invoicing currency 
data from Boz et al. (2022). In particular, we use the annual export 
currency invoicing data for Australia, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, 
New Zealand, Norway, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, United Kingdom 

Journal of Financial Economics 174 (2025) 104184 

and the United States, for years 2009 to 2019, subject to availability 
(data for Canada are unavailable for any of the sample years). For 
each year-country pair, Boz et al. (2022) provide the country’s share of 
exports invoiced in US dollars, in euros, in the country’s home currency, 
and in all other currencies combined. Finally, to calculate country-level 
export ratios (exports to the US or the eurozone over total exports), we 
use annual data on bilateral gross exports (EXGR) from the OECD TiVA 
database.

2.2. Debt and sales summary statistics

We start our analysis by reporting the summary statistics of debt 
shares by currency and sales shares by country. Panel A of Table  1 
presents the pooled average share of debt outstanding denominated 
in each currency, both for our entire firm sample (first row) and for 
each country’s firms separately. As seen in the last column, the eleven 
currencies on which we focus account for virtually all debt issued by 
the firms in our sample. Overall, the two major international currencies, 
the US dollar and the euro, account for more than two thirds (68.52%) 
of the debt outstanding on average: the share of USD-denominated debt 
is 43% and the share of euro-denominated debt is 26%.

Importantly,  aggregate  measures  hide  significant  heterogeneity
across countries. In particular, two main facts stand out. First, there 
seems to be a tendency to borrow in domestic currency. For example, 
52% of the debt outstanding of Australian firms is denominated in 
Australian  dollars,  whereas  the  proportion  of  debt  denominated  in 
Australian dollars for non-Australian firms is almost negligible, with the 
exception of New Zealand firms. The home currency effect is observed 
globally, although its intensity varies across countries: the percentage 
of debt denominated in the home currency ranges from 48% for British 
firms to 95% for Japanese firms.

Second, both the propensity for borrowing in international curren-
cies and the breakdown between borrowing in US dollars and in euros 
are heavily influenced by the firm’s domicile. In particular, excluding 
US and eurozone firms, the share of the two international currencies 
in debt outstanding ranges from 4% for Japanese firms to 51% for 
British firms. In regards to the composition of international currency 
debt (again excluding US and eurozone firms), the largest tilt towards 
US dollar debt is observed for Canadian firms (41% debt in US dollars 
and only 2% in euros, implying that almost all of the international 
currency debt of Canadian firms is US dollar-denominated), whereas 
the biggest tilt towards euro debt is observed for Swedish firms (26% 
euro-denominated debt and 10% US dollar-denominated debt). Fur-
thermore, there seems to be a cultural and geographical element in 
the firms’ debt mix regarding international currencies: firms domiciled 
in  English-speaking  countries  (Australia,  Canada,  the  UK,  and  New 
Zealand) have significantly more US dollar debt than euro-denominated 
debt, whereas firms based in continental Europe (Norway, Switzerland, 
and Sweden) either borrow roughly equally in US dollars and euros or 
tilt their borrowing towards the euro. Finally, US and eurozone firms 
stand out, as they have almost exclusively (average shares of above 95% 
in both cases) international currency debt, but its composition differs 
markedly across those two domiciles: for American firms, the US dollar 
accounts for 90% and the euro for only 7% of their total debt, whereas 
the percentages are almost flipped for eurozone firms (9% and 86%, 
respectively).

given country. On average, the firms in our sample earn about 28%

those two markets combined account for almost half of the revenue 
of the average firm in our sample. Britain, Canada, Japan, China and 
Australia, in that order, are other significant markets for our sample 
firms, collectively accounting for about 29% of their sales. Together, all

3. The currency composition of firm debt

In this section, we document a robust positive association between 
the currency denomination of firm debt and the geographical distri-
bution of firm sales. Furthermore, we show that firms denominate a 
substantial share of their debt in the two major international currencies, 
the US dollar and the euro, even in the absence of significant sales in the 
corresponding markets. We also document that firms exhibit a degree 
of opportunism regarding the currency denomination of their debt, 
preferring to borrow in low-yielding currencies, other things equal. 
However, the association between debt and sales remains strong when 
controlling for interest rate differentials between countries.

tion of debt and global sales. To do so, we estimate panel regressions

By including those fixed effects, we control for everything that affects 
the two-way domicile-currency relationship in a given year: that in-
cludes time-invariant two-way factors (such as geographic or cultural 
distance between the country of firm domicile and each of the markets 
where the firm earns its revenues), time-varying two-way factors (such 
as  differences  in  business  cycle  phases,  in  credit  conditions,  or  in 
interest  rates  between  the  firm  domicile  country  and  each  market) 
and any macroeconomic shocks that affect either the overall supply of 
credit denominated in a given currency or the total credit demand in a 
given country. In other words, the sales share parameter 𝛽 is identified 
by  contemporaneous  differences  in  the  debt  currency  mix  of  firms 
domiciled in the same country. For example, consider two Japanese 
firms, 𝑖 and 𝑖′, that have USD debt outstanding in 2012: in this example, 
𝑗 = Japan, 𝑘 = USD, and 𝑡 = 2012. The question we attempt to answer 
is the following: why would those two Japanese firms have different 
shares of USD debt in 2012? Our hypothesis is that those differences 
arise from differences in the geographical distribution of their sales. In 
particular, we conjecture that firms denominate a higher share of their 
debt in the currency of markets that account for a higher proportion of 
their total sales, suggesting a positive sales share coefficient.

Our findings are reported in Table  2. In the first column, we report 
the regression output when we include all the firms in our benchmark 
sample and all types of debt. The sales share coefficient is 0.39 and 
statistically significant at the 1% level. Furthermore, it is economically 
significant, implying that an 1% increase in the percentage of firm sales 
in a given country is associated with an increase of 0.39% in the per-
centage of firm debt denominated in that country’s currency. Although

The rest of Table  2 consists of robustness checks. First, we restrict 
our attention to two smaller cross-sections of firms: firms that have 
debt outstanding denominated in at least two currencies, and those that 
have debt denominated in at least three currencies. The first subsample

their debt and their sales. However, we find no such evidence: for both 
subsamples, the slope coefficient is 0.43 and highly significant.

Next, in order to ensure that our results are comprehensive across 
markets and currencies, and not driven by an association between debt 
currency denomination and sales that holds only for a single country-
currency pair, we re-run our benchmark specification excluding either 
the  US  sales  and  US  dollar  debt  observations,  or  the  both  US  and 
eurozone sales and US dollar and euro debt observations. We focus on 
these two country and currency pairs due to the size of their product 
and financial markets, as well as their representation in our sample of 
firms: either market is large enough to potentially skew our results. 
However, this does not seem to be the case, as our findings do not 
materially change.

Finally, we explore whether the association between the geography 
of sales and debt currency denomination is sensitive to the type of debt. 
To do so, instead of using shares of total debt as the dependent variable 
in our regressions, we now use the share of bonds with maturity more 
than a year, of loans, or of short-term debt (defined as marketable 
securities with maturity less than a year). These three types of liabilities 
account for almost all firm debt in our sample. Although all three slope 
coefficients are positive and strongly significant, we note that, while the 
slope coefficient for the sales share is very close to the benchmark level 
of 0.39 for both long-term bonds and loans, the currency denomination 
of short-term debt exhibits a somewhat weaker connection with sales, 
as the slope coefficient drops to 0.26.

The finding that the geography of firms’ sales has a stronger asso-
ciation with the currency denomination of their long-term, rather than 
short-term, debt is consistent with firms relying more on long-term debt 
(loans and bonds) as a hedging instrument for currency exposure. Given 
that sales transactions are usually settled quickly and generate short-
term exposure, that result might at first seem counterintuitive. How-
ever, as we show in Internet Appendix B, the geographic distribution of 
firms’ sales is quite stable over time. As a result, firms’ sales-generated 
currency exposure tends to be relatively invariant across time, and, 
hence, conducive to long-term hedging.

Our analysis so far has not taken into account the potential for 
currency hedging arising from imported inputs, i.e., the possibility that 
firms may hedge some of the currency exposure arising from their 
foreign sales by purchasing inputs in a matching foreign currency. That 
possibility is particularly salient for multinational firms with global 
supply chains. In an attempt to address that issue, we construct proxies 
of  the  supplier  relationships  of  our  sample  firms  using  the  FactSet 
Supply Chain Relationships database and show that our main results are 
robust to controlling for those relationships. Our findings are discussed 
in Internet Appendix C. We need to stress that an important caveat of 
our robustness exercise is that our firm supplier data lack the granu-
larity to adequately control for the input hedging channel and, thus, 
we are unable to conclusively rule out the possibility that firms may 
hedge some of the currency exposure arising from their global sales by 
purchasing inputs in a matching foreign currency. However, it is worth 
noting that the existent empirical literature does not find evidence of 
a quantitatively strong input hedging effect, so its importance may be 
limited.1

We establish the robustness of our findings in two additional ways. 
First, in Internet Appendix D, we document a strong positive association 
between debt share changes and contemporaneous sales share changes. 
Second, in Internet Appendix E, we focus on sales observations when 
firms enter a new market, and show that, both in levels and in changes, 
the debt-sales relationship is confirmed when we consider market entry 
observations separately.

As discussed before, the advantage of including three-way fixed 
effects  in  our  pooled  regressions  is  that  they  help  us  uncover  the 
association  between  the  geography  of  firm  sales  and  the  currency 
mix  of  firm  debt  purged  from  any  economy-wide  effects,  static  or 
time-varying. However, those economy-wide effects are likely to have 
an  economically  interesting  structure,  which  may  be  obfuscated  by 
estimating a large number of fixed effects. For example, estimating a 
full set of three-way fixed effects makes it hard to determine whether 
debt issuance is characterized by persistent currency biases within one 
country or persistent differences in currency biases across countries. 
To  uncover  such  biases,  we  put  some  structure  into  the  three-way 
fixed effect, and instead of estimating a large number of economically 
opaque coefficients, we instead estimate much fewer, but economically 
more meaningful, coefficients. In particular, we consider the following 
specification:

First, to account for currency-specific biases in debt denomination, we 
include a full set of currency fixed effects 𝜃𝑘. Those help us uncover 
whether there are some global tilts in the debt denomination towards 
specific currencies, i.e., whether firms globally have a higher share of 
debt outstanding denominated in specific currencies, above and beyond 
the  share  associated  with  their  sales  in  the  corresponding  markets. 
However, currency biases are unlikely to be identical across countries 
— of particular interest is the potential for home currency bias, as 
firms may find it advantageous to issue large amounts of home currency 
debt. To account for potential currency home bias in debt issuance, we 
include 𝐻𝑖,𝑘, a dummy variable that takes the value of 1 if the domestic 
currency of firm 𝑖 is currency 𝑘, and zero otherwise, so the coefficient 
𝜃𝐻𝑜𝑚𝑒 measures the home currency bias of firms. Finally, we include a 
full set of year fixed effects 𝜁𝑡.

We present our findings in Table  3. The first three columns report 
the regression output for the full benchmark sample of firms and incor-
porate a progressively richer set of fixed effects. In the first column, we 
report the regression output when we include only the home currency 
fixed effect. Even in the absence of year and currency fixed effects, 
the geography of sales and home currency bias explain a very large 
proportion of the variability in the currency denomination of debt, as 
the regression 𝑅2 is 74%. The sales share coefficient 𝛽 is 0.59 and 
statistically significant at the 1% level. Furthermore, we document a 
substantial degree of home currency bias in firm debt: the home bias 
coefficient is 0.47, implying that firms denominate almost half of their 
debt in their domestic currency even in the absence of any domestic 
sales. In the second column, we additionally include year fixed effects 
and find very similar estimates. Finally, in the third column we add 
currency fixed effects — this is the richest specification in terms of fixed 
effects. The slope coefficient drops to 0.45, but remains very tightly 
estimated, and the home currency bias remains strong. Furthermore, 
we document the existence of a strong international currency bias: 
a global bias towards borrowing in either US dollars (USD) or euros 
(EUR). The USD and EUR fixed effects are not just strongly statistically 
significant, but also economically significant: our results indicate that 
the average firm in our sample would denominate more than a fifth 
of  its  debt  in  one  of  the  two  international  currencies  (15%  in  US 
dollars and 6% in euros) even in the absence of any sales in those two 
markets. Although the rest of the currency fixed effects are statistically 
significant, their point estimates are very close to zero and, therefore, 
those fixed effects are not economically meaningful. For this reason, 

they are omitted from the table. Finally, it is worth noting that the 
regression 𝑅2 is 77%, only slightly lower than the 𝑅2 of 81% in the 
regression that includes the full set of three-way fixed effects. This 
suggests that the parsimonious structure we impose on the fixed effects 
is able to capture most of the variation in the debt currency mix of 
firms, underscoring the importance of the international currency bias 
and the home currency bias as drivers of the currency denomination of 
firm debt.

The rest of Table  3 reports the same robustness checks as Table  2. 
In column four (five), we consider firms that have debt outstanding 
denominated in at least two (three) currencies. For both subsamples 
of firms, the slope coefficient is close to 0.5 and highly significant, 
so  the  relationship  between  sales  and  debt  is  the  same  as  that  for 
the full sample of firms. However, the relative magnitude of home 
currency and international currency bias changes: those more sophis-
ticated  firms  have  more  international  currency  bias  and  less  home 
currency bias. Next, we exclude either only US sales and USD debt 
observations, or both US and eurozone sales and USD and EUR debt 
observations (columns six and seven, respectively), but our findings do 
not materially change.

Finally, we consider different types of debt (columns eight, nine, 
and ten). As before, short-term debt exhibits a much weaker connection 
with sales than either bonds or loans. As regards currency biases, we 
document  substantial  heterogeneity  across  debt  types:  the  currency 
denomination of bonds exhibits the strongest international currency 
bias and the weakest home currency bias across debt types, with short-
term debt having exactly the opposite pattern and loans being in the 
middle. Our findings indicate that firms denominate short-term debt 
almost exclusively in their home currency, with the geography of sales 
and international currency biases having only secondary impact. To the 
extent that borrowing in an international currency is meant to attract 
foreign funding, our results suggest that firms rely on foreigners only 
for long-term funding and tap domestic resources for funding their 
short-term needs. On the other hand, sales geography and international 
currency effects appear to be first-order in long-term borrowing (bonds 
and loans), with international currency biases being especially strong 
for  bonds.  The  difference  between  bonds  and  loans  in  that  respect 
may be driven by differences in preferences between bond investors 
and financial institutions: whereas Maggiori et al. (2020) document 
strong international currency (and especially US dollar) bias for bond 
investors, that bias may be less prevalent for financial institutions that 
provide loans.

3.2. Time series results

To explore whether the home currency bias and the international 
currency bias exhibit any time variation during our sample period, 
we run cross-sectional regressions for each of the years from 2009 
to 2019 using the same specification as before (omitting year fixed 
effects, which are redundant in a cross-sectional setting). The results 
are presented in Fig.  1. As seen in Panel A, the sales share coefficient is 
quite stable over time. The same is true regarding the USD fixed effect, 
which has been roughly constant during our sample period, whereas 
the EUR fixed effect exhibits an increase from 2009 to 2019 (Panel B). 
As a result, although in 2009 there was effectively no EUR bias in debt 
shares (and, thus, the international currency bias in borrowing was, in 
essence, US dollar bias), by the end of our sample period the USD and 
the EUR fixed effects are quantitatively similar. All other currency fixed 
effects are consistently small and stable over time. Finally, Panel C of 
Fig.  1 shows that the home bias coefficient remains roughly constant 
until 2013 and starts slowly declining thereafter. In summary, our find-
ings suggest that over the last decade developed country firms exhibit a 
larger bias towards funding their assets in international currencies, and 
particularly in euros, somewhat reducing their bias towards domestic 
currency funding.

To address the possibility that our findings on the euro bias trend 
may be driven by loans and short-term debt, rather than bonds, we 
consider the same exercise separately for bond, loan and short-term 
debt shares and report our findings in Fig.  2. We find that the increase 
of the EUR fixed effect is observed only for bond shares, whereas the 
euro bias has been roughly constant over the last decade as regards 
loans and short-term debt.

Finally, one possibility for the fixed effect time variation we doc-
ument is that it may be due to exchange rate fluctuations and does 
not reflect fundamental changes in firm behavior over time. Even if 
the foreign currency amounts of sales and debt are constant over time, 
nominal exchange rate variation would change the shares of sales and 

debt expressed in a given currency and may affect the estimated regres-
sion coefficients. To address that issue and disentangle time variation in 
quantities and in prices, we rerun the cross-sectional regressions fixing 
nominal exchange rates at their 2009 level: for any year from 2009 to 
2019, we convert the year’s foreign currency amounts of sales and debt 
into USD using the corresponding 2009 exchange rate, and report our 
findings in Fig.  3. We see that our results remain almost unchanged, 
confirming that the time variation we uncover is not driven by time 
variation in exchange rates.

3.5. Controlling for interest rate differences

Our analysis so far has ignored the potential impact of borrowing 
rate differences across currencies on firms’ financing decisions. In par-
ticular, firms may be more willing to borrow in low-yielding currencies, 
so, other things equal, a firm’s debt share corresponding to a given 
foreign currency may be associated with the difference between the 
firm’s domestic interest rate and that currency’s rate.

To test our conjecture, we estimate panel regressions of the form

𝑟𝑘,𝑡, the 5-year riskless rate of currency 𝑘. For both currencies, the 
5-year  riskless  rate  is  proxied  by  the  yield  of  the  5-year  sovereign

and currency characteristics that are fixed over time. It follows that 
the two-way fixed effects absorb differences in the time-series means 
across currencies, so the 𝛾 coefficient is identified solely from the time 
variation in interest rates (and, hence, their differentials), and not from 
cross-sectional unconditional interest rate differences. The specification 
also includes a full set of year fixed effects, 𝜁𝑡.

portunistically, we expect to find a positive coefficient on the 5-year 
interest rate differential: a positive differential implies that borrowing 
in  the  foreign  currency  is  cheaper  than  borrowing  in  the  domestic 
currency and, hence, that the foreign currency share should be higher, 
other things equal. Indeed, when we include all firms (first column

our conjecture, whereas the magnitude of the sales share coefficient 
does not materially change with the inclusion of 𝐼𝑅𝐷 as a regressor.

difference between the domestic 5-year rate and its foreign counterpart 
is associated with a 0.20% increase of the share of the firm’s debt 
denominated in the foreign currency. To put that magnitude in context, 
it is worth to contrast it with the sales share coefficient in the same 
specification, which is 0.41: together, the two coefficients imply that a 
reduction of 1% in the 5-year rate of a foreign currency is associated 
with  the  same  average  increase  in  that  currency’s  debt  share  as  a 
0.5% increase in the firm’s sales share in the corresponding country. 
Our findings do not change substantially when we limit our sample 
to firms that have debt outstanding in at least two currencies, but the 
𝐼𝑅𝐷 coefficient becomes non-significant for firms that borrow in 3 or 
more currencies. When we re-estimate our regressions excluding US 
observations only, and excluding both US and eurozone observations

remains positive and significant. However, when both US and eurozone 
observations are excluded, the magnitude of the coefficient on interest 
rate differentials drops substantially (to 0.08), consistent with the idea 
that firms take into account borrowing cost differences between their 
domestic currency and the two international currencies, but are not 
very sensitive to borrowing cost differentials against other currencies.

The specification of Eq.  (7) is consistent with the hypothesis that 
firms take only interest rate differentials into account when comparing 
funding costs between currencies. However, the total return differential 
between currencies has both an interest rate component and an FX 
change component, so firms may also consider the (expected) appre-
ciation  or  depreciation  of  foreign  currencies  against  their  domestic 
currency when they make their decision. If firms believe that nominal 
exchange rates are well-approximated by a random walk (and, hence, 
their expectations of future exchange rate levels are identical to current 
levels), then the specification of Eq.  (7) remains valid, as expected FX 
changes are zero. On the other hand, if firms expect non-zero changes in 
nominal exchange rate levels, then we need to include those expected 
changes in our regression specification, as they affect the overall cost 
of borrowing in different currencies.

Unfortunately,  firms’  expectations  about  future  exchange  rate
changes are unobservable. However, if firms have rational expecta-
tions and full information, realized FX changes are identical to the 
sum of expected FX changes and an error that is orthogonal to all 
information available at the time expectations are formed. Since the 
rational expectations error is absorbed by the error term, we are able 
to replace expected FX changes by realized FX changes and, hence, 
consider regressions of the form 

𝐷𝑖,𝑘,𝑡 = 𝛽𝑆𝑖,𝑘,𝑡 + 𝛾𝐼𝑅𝐷𝑗,𝑘,𝑡 + 𝛿𝛥𝑠𝑗,𝑘,𝑡→𝑡+5 + 𝜉𝑗,𝑘 + 𝜁𝑡 + 𝜀𝑖,𝑘,𝑡,

(8)

where 𝛥𝑠𝑗,𝑘,𝑡→𝑡+5 is the 5-year log exchange rate change between the 
firm’s domestic currency (i.e., currency 𝑗) and currency 𝑘, realized 
between years 𝑡 and 𝑡 + 5. Exchange rates are expressed in units of 
foreign currency per unit of domestic currency, so a positive FX change 
implies a depreciation of the foreign currency. Under the assumption 
of rational expectations and full information, the estimated regression 
coefficient on the realized 5-year FX change should have the same mag-
nitude as the coefficient on the expected 5-year FX change. Therefore, 
if firms are more willing to borrow in low-cost currencies and take both 
components of the borrowing cost into consideration, we expect to find 
a positive coefficient on both the interest rate differential and the FX 
change.

The regression output is reported in Panel B of Table  7. We find 
that, across specifications, adding the FX change as a regressor does 
not substantially change the estimated coefficient on interest rate dif-
ferentials. Furthermore, the coefficient on log FX changes tends to be 
very small and, in most cases, is statistically indistinguishable from

4. Invoicing currency and borrowing currency choice

In this section, we augment our analysis by including aggregate data 
on the invoicing currency of cross-border exports, and we document 
two main results. First, we find that a higher proportion of invoicing in 
the firms’ domestic currency or in a vehicle currency weakens the link 
between the currency denomination of debt and the geography of firms’ 
sales, consistent with the hypothesis that one of the drivers of firms’ 
debt currency choice is their desire to hedge currency exposure arising 
from cross-border exports. Furthermore, we show that firms domiciled 
in countries that invoice a higher share of their exports in either the US 
dollar or the euro also exhibit a higher bias towards those currencies in 
their debt denomination mix, suggesting that the international currency 

bias in debt and the international currency bias in invoicing are not 
district phenomena, but connected through firms’ hedging efforts.

4.1. Invoicing currency and the link between debt and sales

So far, we have documented a strong link between the currency 
denomination of debt and the geography of sales, but we have been 
silent on the mechanism that generates such link. One potential expla-
nation for this connection is currency hedging: firms receive foreign 
currency-denominated cash flows from their sales abroad and hedge 
that  exposure  by  borrowing  in  the  corresponding  foreign  currency. 
Importantly, foreign sales can be either cross-border exports or local 
sales of the firms’ foreign subsidiaries. While local sales are almost 
always  invoiced  in  the  local  currency,  cross-border  exports  can  be 
denominated in either the source or the destination country’s currency 
(producer currency pricing or local currency pricing, respectively), or 
in a vehicle currency that is unrelated to both countries, typically an 
international currency. Therefore, if the link between debt and sales 
is (either partly or fully) driven by firms’ desire to hedge the foreign 
currency cash flows arising from their cross-border exports, then the 
higher the proportion of the firms’ exports that are invoiced in the 
currency of the destination country, the stronger the link between debt 
and sales should be. On the other hand, if the choice of invoicing 
currency does not affect the magnitude of the association between debt 
and sales, then we can infer that the mechanism that generates that 
connection has little to do with hedging currency risk arising from 
cross-border sales.

While we lack firm-level invoicing currency data, we can proxy the 
currency invoicing choice of each firm in our sample by the aggregate 
invoicing practices in its country of domicile. The firms in our sample

exports invoiced in either its domestic currency or in one of the two 
international currencies (the USD and the EUR) in year 𝑡, and it is 
defined as follows: 

𝐷𝑉 𝐼𝑗,𝑡 = 𝐷𝐼𝑗,𝑡 + 𝐼𝐼𝑗,𝑡 − 𝑈 𝑆𝑋𝑗,𝑡 − 𝐸𝑍𝑋𝑗,𝑡,

(9)

where 𝐷𝐼𝑗,𝑡 is the share of exports of country 𝑗 invoiced in its domestic 
currency in year 𝑡, 𝐼𝐼𝑗,𝑡 is the share of exports of country 𝑗 invoiced in

𝑡, and 𝑈 𝑆𝑋𝑗,𝑡 (𝐸𝑍𝑋𝑗,𝑡) is the share of exports of country 𝑗 to the US 
(the eurozone, respectively) in year 𝑡. We subtract the last two terms 
because we want to include only the excess invoicing in international 
currencies: some of the firms’ invoicing in USD or EUR is for exports 
either to the US or the eurozone, but, for those exports, international 
currency invoicing is not vehicle currency invoicing, but destination 
currency invoicing. Intuitively, 𝐷𝑉 𝐼 is a measure inversely related with 
the degree of destination currency invoicing: the higher 𝐷𝑉 𝐼𝑗,𝑡 is, the 
lower the degree of destination currency invoicing of country 𝑗 in year 
𝑡.

To measure the effect of destination currency invoicing, we estimate 

pooled regressions of the form 

𝐷𝑖,𝑘,𝑡 = 𝛽𝑆𝑖,𝑘,𝑡 + 𝜆𝐷𝑉 𝐼𝑗,𝑡𝑆𝑖,𝑘,𝑡 + 𝜉𝑗,𝑘,𝑡 + 𝜀𝑖,𝑘,𝑡.

(10)

This is the specification of Eq.  (1), augmented by an interaction term 
that aims to capture currency invoicing effects. In particular, if the 
link between debt and sales is partly due to firms’ hedging of currency 
exposure arising from their cross-border exports, then the estimated 
value for 𝜆 should be negative and statistically significant: a higher 
value of 𝐷𝑉 𝐼 implies that a lower share of the firm’s cash flows are 
likely  to  be  denominated  in  the  destination  country  currency  and, 
therefore,  that  the  firm  has  a  weaker  incentive  to  borrow  in  that 
currency for hedging purposes.

Table  8 reports the regression results. As seen in the first column of 
the table, when we include all firms in the benchmark sample for which 
we have aggregate invoicing data (704 firms), we get a 𝜆 estimate 
of −0.50, significant at the 1% level. Our regression estimates imply 
that the slope coefficient 𝛽 ranges from 0.14 (for firms with 𝐷𝑉 𝐼 = 1, 
i.e., firms that fully engage in domestic or vehicle currency invoicing) 
to 0.64 (for firms with 𝐷𝑉 𝐼 = 0, i.e., firms that do not do any domestic 

or vehicle currency invoicing). Given that the average 𝐷𝑉 𝐼 in our 
sample is 0.627, the average firm has a 𝛽 estimate of 0.33, very close 
to the corresponding estimate in Table  2. Furthermore, as seen in the 
rest of the columns of Table  8, the estimate for 𝜆 remains negative 
and statistically significant for different subsets of the sample (with 
the exception of firms that have debt denominated in at least three 
currencies), as well as for all three types of debt (bonds, loans, and 
short-term debt), consistent with the idea that the hedging motive is 
an important factor in firms’ debt currency choice.

4.2. Invoicing currency and debt currency biases

In the previous section, our pooled regression specification (Eq. (2)) 
imposed the same fixed effect for each currency of debt denomination 
regardless the firm’s domicile. However, as seen in our country regres-
sions (Eq. (4)), there are substantial differences in currency fixed effects 
across countries. We can now use the aggregate invoicing data to check 
whether those cross-country differences are linked to corresponding 
differences in invoicing currency patterns, i.e., to determine whether 
there  is  a  connection  between  debt  currency  biases  and  invoicing 
currency biases.

To do so, we consider the following specification:

𝐷𝑖,𝑘,𝑡 = 𝛽𝑆𝑖,𝑘,𝑡 + 𝜆𝐷𝑉 𝐼𝑗,𝑡𝑆𝑖,𝑘,𝑡 + 𝜁𝑡 + 𝜃𝑘 + 𝜇𝑘𝐹 𝐼𝑗,𝑘,𝑡 +

𝜃𝐻𝑜𝑚𝑒,𝑗 𝐻𝑖,𝑘 + 𝜇𝐻𝑜𝑚𝑒𝐷𝐼𝑗,𝑡𝐻𝑖,𝑘 + 𝜀𝑖,𝑘,𝑡,

(11)

where  𝐹 𝐼𝑗,𝑘,𝑡  is  the  share  of  country  𝑗  exports  invoiced  in  foreign 
currency 𝑘 in year 𝑡, and 𝐷𝐼𝑗,𝑡 is the share of country 𝑗 exports invoiced 
in its domestic currency in year 𝑡.

For non-home currencies, our specification imposes the assumption 
that, for a given country-currency pair, debt currency bias has not only 
a fixed component, which is common across countries, but also a time-
varying, country-specific component that relates to the invoice currency 
choice of the country’s exporters — in particular, for firms domiciled 
in country 𝑗, the total bias towards (non-home) currency 𝑘 in year 𝑡
is given by 𝜃𝑘 + 𝜇𝑘𝐹 𝐼𝑗,𝑘,𝑡. For example, US dollar debt bias may not be 
identical across (non-US) countries, but may also depend on the degree 
of dollar invoicing of each country’s firms. In that case, we should 
find that 𝜇𝑈 𝑆𝐷 > 0, i.e., that firms in countries that invoice a higher 
share of their exports in USD have a higher USD bias in debt issuance. 
Note that data on 𝐹 𝐼𝑗,𝑘,𝑡 are available only for 𝑘 = {𝑈 𝑆𝐷, 𝐸𝑈 𝑅}, 
so we can get estimates only for 𝜇𝑈 𝑆𝐷 and 𝜇𝐸𝑈 𝑅. However, this is 
not a significant limitation, as our results so far suggest that there is 
economically meaningful bias only for those two currencies. For that 
reason, we assume that 𝐹 𝐼𝑗,𝑘,𝑡 = 0 for the remaining currencies, for all 
domiciles and years.

As regards home currency bias, there are two components, both 
country-specific: a fixed component, and a time-varying component 
dependent on the degree of domestic currency invoicing in that coun-
try. To avoid a proliferation of parameters, we impose the restriction 
that the time-varying component has the same slope coefficient 𝜇𝐻𝑜𝑚𝑒
across countries. Thus, for firms domiciled in country 𝑗, the total home 
currency bias in year 𝑡 is given by 𝜃𝐻𝑜𝑚𝑒,𝑗 + 𝜇𝐻𝑜𝑚𝑒𝐷𝐼𝑗,𝑡.

Our findings are presented in Table  9. When all firms are included 
(first column), the estimated values for 𝜇𝑈 𝑆𝐷 and 𝜇𝐸𝑈 𝑅 are positive 
and significant, indicating that firms domiciled in countries that invoice 
a  larger  share  of  their  exports  in  US  dollars  or  euros  also  have  a 
larger  share  of  their  debt  denominated  in  those  two  currencies.  In 
other words, the international currency bias in debt is increasing in 
international currency invoicing. Again, this finding is consistent with 
the firms’ hedging motive: the more invoicing they do in international 
currencies,  the  larger  international  currency  exposure  they  need  to 
hedge. That said, the fixed, common component (𝜃𝑈 𝑆𝐷 and 𝜃𝐸𝑈 𝑅) of 
the US dollar and euro biases are both economically and statistically 
significant, indicating that firms’ currency hedging behavior cannot 
account for the entire international currency bias in borrowing and 
that other factors (for example, funding availability) may also play a 
role. On the other hand, home currency debt bias does not appear to 
be related to the degree of home currency invoicing, as the estimate for 
𝜇𝐻𝑜𝑚𝑒 is statistically indistinguishable from zero. We get similar results 
when we consider narrower samples of firms (columns two and three) 
and exclude some observations (columns four and five), with the caveat 
that the positive and significant value of 𝜇𝐻𝑜𝑚𝑒 in column five suggests 
a positive association between the magnitude of home currency debt 
bias and the share of home currency invoicing for firms domiciled 
in  countries  without  an  international  currency.  Furthermore,  in  all 
regressions, the coefficient 𝜆 estimate remains negative and significant.
When we consider each type of debt separately (columns six, seven 
and eight), we find that the connection between international currency 

fixed effects and invoicing is mainly driven by bonds. In particular, 
as seen in column six, the estimates for 𝜇𝑈 𝑆𝐷 and 𝜇𝐸𝑈 𝑅 are positive 
and strongly economically and statistically significant for bonds. On 
the other hand, short-term debt exhibits a (weak) link between US 
dollar debt bias and invoicing, but not such relationship for the euro, 
whereas the reverse is true for loans. Our findings are consistent with 
the hypothesis that the preferred natural hedge liability instrument of 
firms is bonds, rather than loans or short-term debt.

5. Extensions and robustness results

In this section, we extend our analysis by focusing on different de-
veloped country firm cross-sections, as well as on Latin American firms. 
Furthermore, we show that our main results are robust to excluding 
financial firms or home observations, and still hold when we focus on 
firms with high domestic sales shares. Finally, in Internet Appendix G 
we consider two alternative samples of developed country firms, the 
restricted sample and the full sample, and in Internet Appendix H we 
redo our analysis excluding zero-sales observations — in both cases, we 
confirm that our main findings continue to hold.

denomination?

5.3. Excluding financial firms

Since financial and non-financial firms exhibit substantial differ-
ences  in  the  level  and  composition  of  their  liabilities,  we  need  to 
confirm that our benchmark results in Table  3 are not driven by the 
inclusion of financial firms. Since we have 119 financial firms in our 
sample, our sample size is reduced to 659 firms, and our findings are 
reported in Table  12. As seen in the table, excluding financial firms does 
not substantially change our results: in our benchmark specification 
(third column), the sales share coefficient is 0.44, and the magnitudes 
of the international currency bias and home currency bias are very 
similar to their Table  3 values.

5.4. Excluding home observations

Given the special nature of, and bias towards, home currency debt, 
we would like to ensure that our findings remain robust to the exclusion 
of home observations. To do so, we re-estimate the pooled and country 
regressions excluding the home observations (i.e., domestic sales shares 
and shares of debt denominated  in the  home  currency) and  report 
our findings in Table  13. The first column reports the results for all 
firms, whereas the rest of the table presents the output of the country 
regressions.

Overall, the sales share coefficient remains positive and significant, 
with its value (0.51) being very close to the benchmark value of 0.45 

reported in Table  3, and the USD and EUR fixed effects remain positive 
and significant (14% and 3%, respectively). However, the regression 𝑅2
is now 0.32, much lower than the value in our full-sample specification, 
underscoring the importance of the home currency bias.

The country regressions produce similar results: the slope coeffi-
cients are positive and (with the exception of Norwegian firms) statis-
tically different from zero, and the USD and EUR fixed effects are gen-
erally positive and statistically significant, confirming the full-sample 
results. Thus, eliminating the home observations does not materially 
alter our results.

5.5. Firms with high domestic sales shares

Taken at face value, the results in Table  3 suggest that the firms in 
our sample borrow in international currencies even in the absence of 
any foreign sales. However, that result may be driven solely by firms 
with substantial foreign sales. To test whether domestically-oriented 
firms exhibit international currency bias, we run the pooled regression 
described in Eq.  (2) only including firms that earn at least 80% of 
their revenues domestically on average during our sample period. Our 
sample includes 113 firms, and we report our findings in Table  14.

In consistence with the findings in Table  10, we find that the link 
between sales and debt is weaker for those firms: in the benchmark 
specification (third column), the sales share coefficient is 0.33, but 
still significant at the 1% level. Importantly, both the USD and EUR 
fixed  effects  are  still  both  economically  and  statistically  significant 
(8% and 5%, respectively). For bonds, the international currency fixed 
effects are even larger, together accounting for 22% of bond shares 
outstanding above and beyond any sales exposure. Therefore, our find-
ings show that even firms focused on domestic sales exhibit substantial 
international currency debt bias.

towards the firms’ domestic currency and the two main international 
currencies, the US dollar and the euro. Furthermore, we show that

invoicing currency patterns. In more detail, we find that firms domi-
ciled in countries that invoice a larger share of their exports in either 
their domestic currency or in a vehicle currency exhibit a weaker link 
between the currency denomination of their debt and the geography 
of their sales, and that firms in countries that invoice a larger share of 
their exports in an international currency also exhibit a higher inter-
national currency bias in debt issuance. Those findings are consistent 
with the hypothesis that firms choose the currency denomination of 
their debt with an eye on hedging the currency exposure arising from 
their exports.

Overall, our findings provide insights into the currency composi-
tion of the global supply of corporate liabilities, complementing the 
literature that focuses on the drivers of international investor demand. 
Furthermore, by documenting the magnitude of international currency 
(and, especially, US dollar) bias in firm borrowing and its relationship 
with international currency bias in export invoicing, our results con-
tribute to the literature on the special role of international currencies, 
and particularly the US dollar, in the global financial system.
