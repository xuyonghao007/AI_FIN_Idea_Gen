Industrial policy serves as a key instrument of macro–meso economic coordination, yet its impact on capital market pricing remains underexplored. Using a comprehensive sample of China's A-share market from 2004–2024, this paper provides the first large-scale evidence that industrial policy generates no significant long-term excess returns for supported firms—directly challenging the conventional wisdom that policy announcements constitute persistent "good news" for stock prices.We show that this long-run irrelevance arises because industrial policies fail to improve firm fundamentals, yet trigger systematic short-run pricing distortions. We propose a unified theoretical framework that reconciles the policy-evaluation literature with behavioral asset pricing: policy acts as a pure expectation shock that temporarily alters investor beliefs without shifting the underlying cash-flow process. This framework generates two novel, testable predictions.First, retail investor sentiment drives immediate overreaction upon announcement, followed by correction as fundamentals fail to materialize. Second, the effect is highly heterogeneous: within industries, large-cap "leader" firms capture disproportionate gains (a new "policy-leader effect"), while across industries high-tech sectors exhibit stronger long-term price responses than traditional sectors.Crucially, we uncover a striking behavioral asymmetry: institutional investors trade aggressively right after announcements and capture most short-term gains, whereas retail investors enter with a significant lag—often buying at peak prices. This redistributive mechanism not only explains the over- and under-reaction patterns but also reveals previously unrecognized wealth-transfer consequences of policy-driven mispricing.This paper contributes to the literature in three fundamental ways. First, we reconcile the long-disconnected policy-evaluation and market-efficiency literatures by showing that industrial policy operates as an expectation shock rather than a fundamental shock, thereby reversing the conventional prediction of sustained positive returns. Second, we open the black box of policy-induced pricing anomalies by identifying two unanticipated mechanisms—retail sentiment amplification and institutional front-running—that are "intuitive in hindsight yet surprising ex ante." Third, we define a new research benchmark question: "When does government policy improve versus distort capital market pricing efficiency?"
